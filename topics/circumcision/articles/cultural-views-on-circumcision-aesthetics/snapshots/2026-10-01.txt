# Cultural views on circumcision aesthetics

Cultural views on circumcision aesthetics encompass societal perceptions of the visual appeal, perceived cleanliness, and normality of circumcised versus uncircumcised male genitalia, often influenced by cultural, religious, and historical contexts. [1] Women's preferences for circumcised versus uncircumcised penises vary widely and are subjective, influenced by cultural exposure, personal experience, hygiene perceptions, aesthetics, and sexual sensation. Some women prefer circumcised penises due to perceived cleanliness (less smegma), more appealing appearance, and ease during oral sex or intercourse. Others prefer uncircumcised for the natural look, foreskin gliding sensation, and potentially greater stimulation. Scientific studies show no consistent overall preference or significant difference in female sexual satisfaction between circumcised and uncircumcised partners; results are mixed and often depend on cultural context. Conversely, ancient civilizations like the Greeks emphasized the beauty of the intact prepuce, viewing it as integral to physical harmony and natural integrity, in contrast to emerging practices in Jewish and Muslim traditions. [2] These divergent aesthetics are frequently tied to ritual significance, where circumcision serves as a marker of identity rather than purely visual preference, though modern demands in some regions incorporate aesthetic motivations alongside tradition. [1] Global variations persist, with ongoing discussions reflecting not universal standards but localized norms shaped by conformity, health perceptions, and evolving social debates. [3]

## Historical Perspectives

### Ancient Civilizations

In ancient Egypt, circumcision held symbolic value tied to purity and elite social standing, as evidenced by tomb reliefs and mummified remains depicting the procedure among nobility and priests, where the circumcised form visually represented ritual cleanliness and distinction from commoners. [4] [5] These depictions, such as those from the Sixth Dynasty, emphasized the aesthetic of the altered genitalia as a marker of elevated status rather than mere functionality. [4]

Greek and Roman societies idealized the uncircumcised prepuce for its aesthetic harmony with bodily proportions, particularly in athletic contexts where the covered glans was seen as enhancing visual balance and modesty during nudity in gymnasia and competitions. [6] Circumcision was often viewed as mutilating this natural form, prompting practices like foreskin restoration (epispasm) or the kynodesme to conceal scars and restore the preferred preputial appearance. [7] Literary and artistic sources, including statues and texts, reinforced this preference for the intact foreskin as emblematic of civilized beauty and virility. [6]

In some ancient Near Eastern societies, circumcision scars served as visible status symbols or tribal identifiers, adorning the body to signify group affiliation or warrior initiation rather than emphasizing hygiene. [8] [5] Prehistoric tribal practices similarly treated such modifications as ritual embellishments denoting maturity or clan prestige, with archaeological inferences from ritual sites highlighting their ornamental role in communal aesthetics. [5]

### Medieval to Early Modern Eras

In Islamic traditions during the medieval period, hadith collections such as those in Sunan al-Fitra emphasized male circumcision (khitan) as one of the natural inclinations (fitrah) promoting bodily cleanliness and purity, aligning with broader ideals of hygiene that extended to aesthetic perceptions of a refined, groomed appearance. [9] This framing positioned the circumcised state as enhancing overall bodily harmony in line with prophetic practices, though explicit visual descriptors remained tied to ritual purity rather than isolated aesthetics. [10]

Medieval European Christian art largely avoided direct depictions of circumcision, particularly in scenes of the infant Jesus, to uphold the uncircumcised form as emblematic of the natural divine creation unmarred by Jewish ritual alterations. [11] Such omissions reflected theological preferences for portraying Christ in a gentile-compatible intact state, viewing circumcision as a disfiguring mark that contrasted with ideals of bodily wholeness. [12] This selective iconography reinforced cultural distinctions, preserving uncircumcised aesthetics as normative in Christian visual symbolism. [13]

In Ottoman contexts, elaborate circumcision ceremonies for princes highlighted the rite as a public marker of manhood and maturity, though textual emphasis remained on communal spectacle over detailed genital aesthetics. [14] Medieval Jewish texts, building on earlier precedents, similarly framed circumcision as a covenantal sign integrating ritual with physical maturity, without extensive focus on visual appeal in surviving records. [15]

## Western Societies

### North America

In the United States and Canada, cultural preferences have long favored the circumcised penis for its perceived visual tidiness, uniformity, and reduced foreskin prominence, often framed as aligning with modern hygiene standards. Post-World War II hospital routines normalized neonatal circumcision as a standard procedure, with parenting magazines portraying the result as a streamlined, aesthetically preferable form compared to the intact state. [16] Pediatric advice in outlets like Redbook reinforced this by endorsing early circumcision for its purported cleanliness and conformity to societal norms. [17]

Surveys among Americans reveal a majority preference for circumcised aesthetics, frequently citing enhanced symmetry and a neater appearance over the uncircumcised form's perceived redundancy. For example, studies of women's partner preferences indicate around 69% favoring circumcision for visual and tactile appeal. [18] This aligns with broader cultural normalization, where the circumcised state is viewed as standard and desirable in social and intimate contexts. [19]

Indigenous North American communities present a contrasting perspective, where the uncircumcised penis retains traditional aesthetic and cultural value as the unaltered, natural form integral to pre-colonial identity. Circumcision was absent from most tribal practices prior to European contact and is often regarded as an imposition of settler medical norms rather than an enhancement. [20]

### Europe

In much of Europe, cultural perceptions emphasize the uncircumcised penis as the aesthetically natural and unaltered form, with routine circumcision often critiqued for disrupting visual harmony and proportions without compelling non-religious justification. French medical and societal discourse has historically resisted widespread adoption despite occasional endorsements, maintaining low prevalence rates that underscore views of the procedure as visually unnecessary for hygiene or appeal in secular contexts. Similarly, in Germany, cultural norms render non-therapeutic circumcision rare among the general population, prioritizing the intact state as standard and opposing interventions seen as imposing artificial modifications. [21]

This resistance gained prominence in a 2012 Cologne regional court ruling, which classified circumcision on minors for non-medical reasons as a breach of bodily integrity, framing it as an infringement on the child's natural physical autonomy and sparking debates that highlighted aesthetic and ethical concerns over genital alteration. [22] [23] In Scandinavian countries and the UK, preferences lean toward the foreskin as contributing to a complete, less invasive visual profile, aligning with broader continental sentiments that affirm uncircumcised aesthetics as normative and proportionate. Developments in Eastern Europe have reinforced these uncircumcised standards, fostering conformity in communal settings like baths where unaltered forms predominate visually. [24]

## Middle Eastern and African Societies

### Islamic Contexts

In Islamic traditions, male circumcision is regarded as enhancing bodily perfection and cleanliness, aligning with the concept of fitrah, the innate disposition of humans, by removing the foreskin to prevent accumulation of impurities like smegma and urine residues, thereby promoting a purified and refined appearance. [25] This practice is seen as emulating prophetic ideals, where the circumcised state is culturally normalized as the "natural" form, while uncircumcised genitalia may be perceived as abnormal or incomplete. [25] [26]

In Turkish culture, the sünnet ceremony serves as a festive rite of passage, often involving elaborate celebrations that emphasize the boy's visual and social transformation into manhood, with medical procedures prioritizing appropriate foreskin removal for aesthetic outcomes. [27] [28] Women's opinions in Turkish online forums such as Ekşi Sözlük and KızlarSoruyor reflect mixed views on uncircumcised penises, with many preferring the circumcised form for its cleaner appearance, better hygiene, and more direct stimulation during intercourse, while others favor uncircumcised for natural lubrication, reduced friction potentially leading to longer-lasting sex, and greater sensitivity. [29] This underscores the cultural bias toward circumcision in Turkey, where uncircumcised men are less common and sometimes viewed negatively. Similar narratives in Arab societies, such as in Saudi Arabia and Egypt, associate circumcision with aesthetic maturity, where the procedure's visible cleanliness is linked to hygiene standards valued in marital and social contexts, reinforcing perceptions of tidiness and conformity. [25]

Variations between Sunni and Shia practices influence timing, with Shia traditions favoring earlier intervention around the seventh day post-birth as mustahab, potentially yielding smoother visual results compared to later timings more common in some Sunni communities, though Islamic jurisprudence allows flexibility without a fixed mandate. [30]

### Traditional African Practices

In many sub-Saharan African societies, traditional circumcision practices intertwine with scarification, where deliberate ritual markings on the body, including genital regions, function as visible emblems of social identity, warrior prowess, and successful passage into adulthood, enhancing perceived aesthetic value through patterned scars that signify endurance and status. [31] [32]

Among the Xhosa of South Africa, the ulwaluko initiation ritual centers on circumcision, with the resulting scars regarded as enduring symbols of manhood achievement, embedding notions of maturity and responsibility that elevate the modified form's cultural prestige. [33] [34] Similarly, Maasai emuratta ceremonies involve public circumcision without anesthesia, positioning the scars as badges of bravery that visually affirm the initiate's transition to moran (warrior) status, thereby conferring aesthetic honor within communal displays of resilience. [35] [36]

Colonial interventions disrupted these hierarchies by imposing medicalized procedures that diminished emphasis on traditional scarring techniques, shifting perceptions from ritual-enhanced visuals to standardized hygiene, thereby challenging indigenous valuations of scar aesthetics as markers of authentic manhood. [37]

## Asian and Other Regions

### South and East Asia

In non-Muslim populations of South and East Asia, cultural attitudes toward circumcision aesthetics often reflect neutrality or a preference for the uncircumcised form as the natural, unaltered state, with low prevalence underscoring a lack of emphasis on visual modification for appeal or cleanliness. Hindu perspectives in India prioritize the intact body as integral to its inherent wholeness, discouraging non-therapeutic alterations that could disrupt this natural integrity and often associating circumcision with extraneous practices. [38]

Chinese cultural norms exhibit indifference to circumcision for aesthetic purposes, as evidenced by low parental acceptance and willingness for the procedure among newborns, where it is not traditionally viewed as enhancing visual tidiness or normality. [39] Similarly, in Japan, the rarity of the practice aligns with a normalized view of uncircumcised genitalia absent from aesthetic stigma in broader societal depictions.

Among Muslim communities in Southeast Asia, circumcision is occasionally performed as a rite but lacks strong mandates tied to visual harmony or enhancement, focusing instead on ritual observance rather than perceptual ideals of cleanliness or form. [40]

### Oceania and Indigenous Groups

In Indigenous Australian Aboriginal cultures, subincision—a ritual longitudinal incision along the underside of the penis into the urethra—serves as a key initiation practice that modifies the visual form of the otherwise intact, foreskin-covered genitalia, symbolizing the transition from boyhood to manhood and conferring tribal status. [41] This alteration, performed without foreskin removal in many groups, holds totemic significance, integrating the modified appearance into broader cultural narratives of identity and lineage rather than emphasizing hygienic or universal aesthetic ideals. [42]

In Polynesian island societies, traditional practices often include circumcision as a rite of passage in groups like Samoa and Tonga, with cultural emphasis on communal rituals rather than primarily aesthetic preferences, though prevalence varies (e.g., rare among Maori). [43] Parental attitudes in Pacific contexts reflect acceptance of circumcision primarily for cultural tradition and cleanliness, with minimal focus on visual appeal or alteration as a normative standard. [44]

European missionaries in Oceania disrupted these indigenous norms by condemning ritual genital modifications as uncivilized, advocating for modesty through clothing and hygiene practices that devalued traditional visual markers of manhood in favor of concealed, unmodified appearances aligned with Western propriety. [45] This influence gradually shifted perceptions, portraying unmodified or ritually altered forms as less aesthetically acceptable in colonized settings. [41]

## Religious Influences

### Abrahamic Traditions

In Judaism, the brit milah ritual culminates in the peria stage, where the mucous membrane is torn to fully expose the glans, establishing a visible emblem of the covenant between God and the Jewish people that signifies spiritual maturity and sacred identity. [46] [47]

Early Christian patristic writings, such as those of John Chrysostom, echo the Apostle Paul's characterization of physical circumcision advocates as promoters of "mutilation," portraying the procedure as an unnecessary alteration of the flesh that contrasts with God's original design redeemed through Christ's spiritual circumcision. [48] [49]

In Islam, a hadith recorded in Sahih Muslim lists circumcision among the five practices of fitrah, the innate human disposition ordained by Allah, framing the timely removal of the foreskin—ideally on or near the seventh day—as a sunnah act that aligns the body with divine perfection and completes natural creation. [50] [51]

### Non-Abrahamic Beliefs

In Hindu traditions, male circumcision lacks scriptural endorsement and is often regarded as contrary to the natural integrity of the body, which is seen as divinely ordained without need for such modifications, implying a cultural preference for the unaltered genital form as the normative aesthetic. [52] This perspective aligns with broader principles of preserving the body's inherent wholeness, where genital alteration is not tied to ritual purity or visual enhancement but viewed as superfluous to spiritual harmony.

Buddhist teachings do not incorporate circumcision as a rite or doctrinal element, treating the body as transient and not requiring specific genital modifications for enlightenment or aesthetic ideals, thus maintaining a neutral to minimalistic stance on the natural versus circumcised appearance. [53] In these contexts, emphasis lies on inner cultivation over external bodily changes, with uncircumcised genitalia considered aesthetically unremarkable and aligned with the impermanent nature of form.

## Modern Influences

### Media and Popular Culture

In the United States, the pornography industry has historically dominated global erotic media production and predominantly portrays circumcised penises as the aesthetic standard, reinforcing perceptions of tidiness and desirability in visual representations of male genitalia. [54] This prevalence aligns with broader cultural norms where such depictions in films and magazines emphasize the circumcised form as visually appealing for sexual contexts. [54]

European cinema, by contrast, frequently includes uncircumcised depictions in a matter-of-fact manner, often normalizing intact genitalia without alteration as part of authentic human portrayals that counter prevailing modification ideals in other media. [55]

Global online platforms and forums facilitate debates on circumcision aesthetics through anonymous sharing of images and personal comparisons, amplifying diverse views on visual preferences and challenging mainstream media standards via user-generated content. [56]

### Hygiene and Medical Narratives

In 20th-century United States medical discourse, circumcision was frequently advocated for enhancing genital hygiene by minimizing smegma accumulation beneath the foreskin, which was portrayed as promoting a cleaner appearance compared to the intact state. [57] This perspective positioned the exposed glans of circumcised penises as visually tidier and less susceptible to perceived uncleanliness, influencing routine neonatal procedures amid broader campaigns against masturbation and infections. [58]

The World Health Organization (WHO) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have shaped narratives in sub-Saharan Africa through voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) programs aimed at HIV prevention, where hygiene benefits are emphasized alongside reduced infection risk, implicitly linking the circumcised form to a more sanitary aesthetic in public health visuals. [59] These initiatives highlight circumcision's role in elective hygiene improvements, framing the removal of the foreskin as diminishing opportunities for debris buildup that could detract from a clean visual presentation. [60]

A 2014 qualitative study of 30 sexually active women in Kisumu, Kenya, found that 77% preferred circumcised men, primarily due to perceived better hygiene (less dirt, odor, and smegma). One participant stated she would not perform oral sex on an uncircumcised man due to perceived dirtiness but would on a circumcised man; hygiene perceptions strongly influenced such sexual behaviors. [61]

In contrast, European medical perspectives typically assert that adequate hygiene of the intact foreskin can be maintained through regular cleaning, obviating the aesthetic alterations associated with circumcision without compromising cleanliness. [62] This view underscores that visual perceptions of genital normality need not prioritize foreskin removal, as proper care suffices to prevent smegma-related concerns. [63]

## Psychological Dimensions

### Individual Body Image

In the United States, where circumcision prevalence exceeds 80%, uncircumcised males have reported psychological distress linked to perceived aesthetic differences, particularly in communal settings like locker rooms, where uncircumcised status contributes significantly to witnessed teasing about penile appearance. [64] This can foster long-term dissatisfaction with one's unaltered genitalia, viewed as less tidy or normative against the circumcised majority. [64]

In Europe, where routine circumcision is rare, intact pride initiatives within intactivist communities emphasize the natural form's aesthetic and functional integrity, countering external pressures for alteration and promoting self-acceptance among uncircumcised men. [65]

### Interpersonal Preferences

Women's preferences for circumcised versus uncircumcised penises vary widely and are subjective, influenced by cultural exposure, personal experience, hygiene perceptions, aesthetics, and sexual sensation. Some women prefer circumcised penises due to perceived cleanliness (less smegma), more appealing appearance, and ease during oral sex or intercourse. Others prefer uncircumcised penises for the natural look, foreskin gliding sensation during intercourse, and potentially greater stimulation. Scientific studies show no consistent overall preference or significant difference in female sexual satisfaction between circumcised and uncircumcised partners; results are mixed and often depend on cultural context. There is no reliable evidence of unique preferences specifically for Muslim men's penises beyond general circumcision-related factors.

In surveys of women's partner preferences, a significant portion in the United States expressed a strong favor for circumcised penises, citing streamlined visual appeal and perceived cleanliness as key factors. [66] [67] For instance, 71-83% of respondents preferred circumcised forms across various intimate activities, primarily due to aesthetic and hygienic attributes. [67]

A 2019 systematic review of 29 studies published in Sexual Medicine found that in the overwhelming majority of studies, women expressed a preference for circumcised penises. Key reasons included better appearance (cleaner, sexier look), improved hygiene (less odor, easier to clean), reduced perceived risk of infection/STIs, and enhanced sexual activity (vaginal intercourse, manual stimulation, fellatio). Preferences held even in low-circumcision settings. However, a significant minority had no preference or preferred uncircumcised (often due to gliding sensation or natural feel), especially those with experience only with uncircumcised partners. Hygiene is crucial: poor cleaning can lead to smegma buildup under foreskin, causing odor, while proper care mitigates this. Anecdotal reports from online forums such as Reddit indicate divided opinions, with many women expressing no strong preference or stating that it does not matter much; some prefer circumcised penises for aesthetic reasons or familiarity, especially in regions where it is common like the United States; others prefer uncircumcised for better natural lubrication, sensation, and pleasure. Cultural background heavily influences views, with no universal majority preference. Overall, preferences are subjective, not universal, and confidence/hygiene outweigh aesthetics for many. [3]

Evolutionary psychology discussions on circumcision's role in partner selection remain debated, with some positing that aesthetic preferences may influence mate choice but lacking consensus on adaptive advantages. [68]
