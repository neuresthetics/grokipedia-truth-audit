# Women unaffected by female genital cutting

Women unaffected by female genital cutting are females who have not undergone the culturally motivated procedure involving partial or total removal of, or other injury to, the external female genitalia, a practice that spares the natural anatomy and avoids procedure-linked complications such as hemorrhage, chronic pain, urinary issues, sexual dysfunction, and increased childbirth risks. [1] [2]

This group comprises the overwhelming majority of the world's approximately four billion females, as female genital cutting affects only over 230 million girls and women alive today, concentrated in about 30 countries across Africa (144 million cases), Asia (over 80 million), and parts of the Middle East. [2] [1] In non-endemic regions—including Europe, the Americas, Oceania, and most of Asia and the Middle East—the practice is negligible or absent, resulting in near-universal unaffected status among local populations. [3] Even within high-prevalence countries, such as Somalia (98% among women aged 15-49) or Egypt (87%), unaffected women exist due to factors like ethnic variation, urban residence, higher maternal education, and generational shifts, with surveys showing lower rates among daughters compared to mothers in many areas. [3] [4]

Globally, the unaffected experience no ritual-inflicted physical or psychological trauma from cutting, preserving full anatomical function and fertility potential absent iatrogenic harm; empirical studies link the procedure to elevated maternal mortality and neonatal risks in affected cohorts, underscoring the baseline health status of the intact. [1] [5] Despite overall numbers rising with population growth, prevalence has declined in over half of surveyed countries since the 1990s, driven by legal bans, education campaigns, and community-led abandonment, thereby expanding the unaffected demographic even in traditional strongholds. [6] [7] This trend reflects causal influences like socioeconomic development and exposure to external norms, rather than isolated moral suasion, though data reliability varies due to underreporting and reliance on self-reported household surveys in resource-limited settings. [4]

## Definition and Classification

### Core Procedure and Variations

The core procedure of female genital cutting entails the partial or total removal of, or other injury to, the external female genitalia for non-therapeutic cultural, religious, or social purposes, typically performed on girls between infancy and age 15 using rudimentary tools such as razors, knives, or thorns without anesthesia. [1] [8] The World Health Organization (WHO) delineates four principal types based on the extent of tissue alteration, with Type I being the most prevalent globally at approximately 44% of cases among affected women, followed by Type II at 40%, Type III at 10%, and Type IV encompassing the remainder. [9] [8]

Type I (clitoridectomy) involves the partial or total excision of the clitoral glans and/or prepuce, often subdivided into Type Ia (narrow removal limited to the prepuce) and Type Ib (wider excision including adjacent tissue); this form predominates in regions like parts of West Africa and the Middle East, where it may be termed "sunna" in some Islamic contexts, referring to a ritual cut analogous to male circumcision and typically involving only the clitoral hood with minimal deeper incision. [8] [10] Type II (excision) extends to partial or total removal of the clitoris along with the labia minora, sometimes including the labia majora (Type IIb), and is common in West and East Africa, executed via scraping or cutting to eliminate protruding tissue. [8] [9]

Type III (infibulation or pharaonic circumcision) constitutes the most invasive variant, narrowing the vaginal orifice through excision of the clitoris and labia, followed by suturing or sealing the remaining tissue to form a scar, often requiring later defibulation for intercourse or childbirth; this practice clusters in the Horn of Africa (e.g., Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea) and Sudan, affecting up to 80-90% of cases in those locales as of 2022 surveys. [8] [9] Type IV encompasses all residual procedures lacking standardized classification, including symbolic pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, or cauterization of the clitoral region without substantial excision; these milder interventions prevail in Southeast Asia (e.g., Indonesia, Malaysia) and some Kurdish communities, where they may involve a superficial nick to draw blood for ritual purposes, comprising over 80 million cases regionally. [8] [9]

Procedural variations arise regionally and communally, influenced by ethnic traditions rather than uniform doctrine; for instance, in Sudan, "sunna" contrasts with "pharaonic" as a less extensive Type I versus Type III, while Indonesian practices often limit to Type IV nicking during puberty rites, reflecting adaptations to local beliefs in purity or maturity without obligatory tissue loss. [10] [2] Tools and settings vary from traditional healers in rural areas using unsterilized blades to urban modifications with stitches for infibulation, though immediate post-procedure care typically involves herbal applications or binding to staunch bleeding, with prevalence data indicating Type I and IV forms correlate with lower reported procedural severity in ethnographic accounts from diverse African and Asian cohorts. [4] [9]

### Distinction Between Cutting and Mutilation Narratives

The terminology debate centers on "female genital mutilation" (FGM), which connotes deliberate disfigurement and irreversible harm, as adopted by the World Health Organization (WHO) since 1995 to classify all procedures involving partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury thereto, irrespective of intent or severity. In opposition, "female genital cutting" (FGC) is favored by anthropologists and select public health scholars for its descriptive neutrality, avoiding presuppositions of cultural inferiority and enabling empirical analysis of varied outcomes, much like terminology for male circumcision or female cosmetic genital surgeries. [11] This shift, evident in works from the early 2000s onward, such as Ellen Gruenbaum's ethnographic studies, underscores that the mutilation label can impede dialogue in practicing communities by framing the act as uniformly victimizing, thus marginalizing women's agency in rituals tied to identity, hygiene, or beauty standards. [12]

The mutilation narrative, dominant in Western human rights and media discourses, posits inherent physical, sexual, and psychological devastation, often extrapolating from severe Type III infibulation cases (prevalent in <10% of instances globally) to all forms, while downplaying self-reports of normalcy. [5] Conversely, the cutting narrative differentiates by WHO typology—Type I (clitoridectomy, affecting ~80% of cases in some regions) involves minimal tissue removal analogous to hoodectomy in cosmetic procedures, with longitudinal data showing no consistent links to reduced sexual satisfaction or fertility when controlled for socioeconomic factors. [13] For example, qualitative accounts from Sudanese and Kenyan women describe Type I or II cuttings as enhancing marital eligibility and community belonging without long-term detriment, attributing any complications to unskilled practitioners rather than the procedure itself. [14]

Empirical challenges to the mutilation frame emerge from studies isolating procedure effects from stigma: a 2023 analysis of 638 Somali women (predominantly Type I/II) in the US revealed FGC status unrelated to gynecologic issues (16.6% prevalence) or distress (20%), but discrimination experiences correlated with 5-fold higher odds of mental health problems (OR=5.11). [15] This supports cutting proponents' view that pathologizing narratives amplify harm via social exclusion, as institutional emphases on mutilation—often from ideologically aligned NGOs—overlook resilience in unaffected women who report intact pleasure and well-being, per community-based surveys in Mali and Sierra Leone (e.g., 70-80% satisfaction rates in ritual contexts). [13] Such distinctions reveal how source biases, including ethnocentric assumptions in academia, skew toward harm amplification over causal disaggregation of cultural versus procedural impacts. [11]

## Cultural and Historical Context

### Global Prevalence and Regional Practices

Over 230 million girls and women alive today have undergone female genital cutting, with the figure representing a 15% increase from prior estimates due to updated demographic modeling and survey data. [6] [1] This prevalence is concentrated in Africa, which accounts for more than 144 million cases, followed by Asia with over 80 million. [2] Evidence of the practice extends to at least 94 countries, though comprehensive national data are available for only about 30, primarily through household surveys like Demographic and Health Surveys. [16]

In Africa, the practice is most entrenched in sub-Saharan countries, where prevalence among women aged 15-49 exceeds 80% in nations such as Somalia, Guinea, Djibouti, Sierra Leone, and Mali, based on 2010-2023 survey data. [3] North African countries like Egypt and Sudan also report high rates, with Egypt at approximately 87% among ever-married women in surveys up to 2014, though recent urban-rural declines have been noted. [4] Procedures here often occur in childhood or adolescence, performed by traditional practitioners using non-sterile tools, and serve as rites of passage tied to marriageability and social integration. [1]

Middle Eastern practices are less uniformly documented but occur in pockets of Yemen, Iraq's Kurdistan region, and among certain communities in Oman, the United Arab Emirates, and Jordan, with no national prevalence exceeding 20% where data exist. [17] In Yemen, surveys from 2010-2021 indicate rates around 19% nationally, higher in rural areas, often involving Type I or II cuttings (removal of prepuce or clitoris) justified by religious or purity norms. [18] Iraqi Kurdish data from 2011-2020 show prevalence up to 40-50% in some governorates, typically milder forms like sunna circumcision. [19]

In Asia, prevalence clusters in Indonesia and Malaysia, affecting over 80 million cumulatively, with Indonesian surveys estimating 49% exposure among women aged 15-49 as of 2017 data, often as symbolic pricking or minimal excision in infancy. [2] Malaysian practices, surveyed in 2018-2020, involve similar low-intervention forms among Malay Muslim communities, with rates around 10-20% nationally but higher in specific ethnic groups. [4] These regional variations emphasize non-therapeutic alterations ranging from symbolic nicking to more extensive removals, influenced by ethnic, religious, and migratory patterns. [20]

### Traditional Rationales and Social Functions

In communities practicing female genital cutting (FGC), traditional rationales often center on enhancing cleanliness, aesthetics, and femininity, with practitioners viewing the procedure as a means to remove perceived impurities or excess tissue that could hinder hygiene or appeal. For instance, among groups in Guinea, FGC is justified as promoting purity and beauty, aligning with cultural ideals of proper female form. [21] Similarly, in northern Ghana's Kassena-Nankana ethnic group, it is tied to notions of tidiness and maturity, marking the transition to womanhood by aligning the body with societal standards of propriety. [22] These beliefs persist despite external critiques, as they stem from intergenerational transmission of norms emphasizing physical refinement for social acceptance. [23]

A primary rationale involves regulating female sexuality to ensure chastity and fidelity, which communities associate with preserving family honor and facilitating marriage. In Guinea, cutting is believed to diminish sexual desire, thereby safeguarding virginity until marriage and reducing infidelity risks, with statements like "if your clitoris is not removed... you may become addicted to men" reflecting this view. [23] Among Mandinka communities in Gambia, FGC parallels male circumcision in promoting purity and marriage eligibility, often framed as a religious or customary duty to control impulses and uphold moral standards. [21] Such rationales underscore a perceived causal link between the procedure and behavioral restraint, integral to kinship structures where uncircumcised women face diminished prospects. [22]

Social functions of FGC reinforce community cohesion and gender hierarchies, functioning as a rite of passage that integrates girls into adult women's networks. In settings like Guinea and Ghana, the ritual—often performed by female elders—imparts knowledge of societal roles, fostering solidarity and peer acceptance while excluding men, thus operating as a domain of female agency and secrecy. [21] [22] It elevates family status through bridewealth negotiations and funeral rites eligibility, with non-participation risking ostracism or reduced social capital. [23] These functions embed FGC within broader systems of reciprocity and identity, where adherence signals loyalty to ancestral customs over individual autonomy concerns. [22]

## Self-Reported Outcomes

### Personal Testimonies of No Adverse Effects

Fuambai Ahmadu, a Sierra Leonean-American anthropologist, underwent female genital excision (Type II) as part of a Bondo initiation rite in Sierra Leone between December 1991 and January 1992, at the age of 24. In subsequent interviews and writings, she described the procedure as a culturally affirming experience that enhanced her sense of womanhood and community belonging among Kono women, with no long-term physical or psychological harm. Ahmadu reported experiencing no sexual dysfunction post-excision, asserting that it shifted emphasis toward vaginal orgasms via g-spot stimulation, which she claimed improved pleasure compared to clitoral-focused sensations. [24]

Ahmadu has cited her personal observations and ethnographic research to dispute claims of universal sexual impairment, noting that circumcised women in her studies, including herself, achieved orgasms and sexual satisfaction without difficulty. She referenced self-reports from Sudanese women in earlier fieldwork, such as that of Hanny Lightfoot-Klein, where 94% of infibulated participants described enjoying sex and reaching orgasm, often more intensely than uncircumcised counterparts due to heightened internal sensitivity. Ahmadu argued these accounts challenge Western assumptions of harm, attributing reported issues to cultural stigma rather than the cutting itself. [24]

In a 2016 exploratory study of Kenyan women who had undergone various forms of female genital cutting, several participants expressed satisfaction with the procedure, with one stating, "I am happy I was circumcised so I am sure I [feel secure in my marriage]." Others reported no ongoing health problems or regrets, viewing the cutting as a normalized rite that conferred social maturity without impeding daily life or marital relations. These testimonies, drawn from in-depth interviews, highlighted perceived benefits like reduced promiscuity concerns and cultural pride, though the study noted potential underreporting of subtle effects due to social desirability bias.

Anonymous self-reports in qualitative research from Egypt and Sudan similarly include women affirming no adverse impacts on fertility, childbirth, or sexual enjoyment. For instance, in Sudanese communities practicing infibulation, interviewees described post-procedure sexual lives as fulfilling, with one noting "no problem" in conception or pleasure after defibulation for marriage. Such accounts, while limited by small sample sizes and cultural context, provide counterpoints to predominant narratives of harm, suggesting variability in individual outcomes based on procedure type and personal resilience. [25]

### Survey Data on Perceived Well-Being

In Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) conducted across countries where female genital cutting (FGC) is prevalent, substantial proportions of women who have undergone the procedure express support for its continuation, indicating a lack of perceived personal detriment to well-being. For instance, in the 2018 Egypt DHS, 61% of women aged 15-49 who had experienced FGC favored the practice for their daughters, while in Mali's 2012-2013 DHS, 73% held similar views. [26] Such attitudes persist even among cut women, with Guinea's 2018 DHS reporting 96% approval for continuation among those affected. [26] These self-reported preferences suggest that many women do not associate FGC with diminished life satisfaction or health, viewing it instead as a normalized cultural element without subjective harm.

Surveys on sexual well-being further reveal that women with FGC often report orgasmic capacity comparable to or unaltered from uncut peers. A study of 137 women with various FGC types in Italy found that 86% experienced orgasm, with 69% reporting it consistently, attributing pleasure to internal clitoral structures remaining intact post-cutting. [27] Similarly, a Sudanese survey indicated cut women were as likely as uncut women to report regular orgasms during intercourse, challenging assumptions of total elimination of sexual sensation. [28] In a comparison of 130 sexually active women with FGC and 130 without in Iran, no significant differences emerged in overall sexual function scores, though type-specific variations existed. [29]

Qualitative and quantitative data on broader perceived well-being underscore resilience or neutrality. Among Somali women in Sweden, many described FGC as "a part of my life" without linking it to reduced happiness or functionality, emphasizing cultural integration over adverse effects. [30] A metasynthesis of studies on FGC-affected women's sexual health noted reports of pleasure persistence, with some attributing enhanced relational intimacy to the practice's social role. [31] These findings, drawn from self-assessments in endemic regions, contrast with external harm narratives, as women frequently report unaltered daily functioning and marital satisfaction. [32]

## Empirical Evidence on Effects

### Physical Health Findings by Type

A systematic review of physical health outcomes associated with female genital cutting (FGC) classified by World Health Organization types reveals limited high-quality evidence linking the procedure directly to long-term morbidity, with many associations confounded by methodological weaknesses such as small samples, recall bias, and lack of controls for socioeconomic or environmental factors. [33] For instance, while relative risks for conditions like urinary tract infections (RR=3.01) and dyspareunia (RR=1.53) were reported, the overall GRADE quality of evidence was rated very low due to heterogeneity and poor study designs, indicating that causality remains uncertain and absolute prevalence of such issues may be low in unaffected cohorts. [33]

In empirical analyses separating procedure-specific effects from social factors, FGC status itself showed no significant independent association with physical health morbidities, including genitourinary difficulties or pain during intercourse (OR=0.79 for poor physical health), whereas experiences of discrimination strongly predicted adverse outcomes (OR=2.68). [15] This suggests that reported physical complaints in FGC-affected women may often stem from stigma or access barriers rather than the cutting procedure. No associations were found with HIV acquisition (AOR=0.95) or sexually transmitted infections (RR=1.07), underscoring minimal impact on infectious disease transmission across types. [33]

### Type I (Clitoridectomy)

Type I procedures, involving partial or total removal of the clitoral prepuce and/or glans, exhibit the lowest reported rates of long-term physical complications among FGC variants, with clinical data indicating rare chronic scarring, keloid formation, or infertility when performed asepticly. [33] Systematic assessments found inconclusive evidence for persistent genitourinary disorders, and immediate issues like swelling or retention resolved without sequelae in the majority of cases (median swelling prevalence 15%, range 2–27%). [33] In contexts with milder implementations, such as symbolic pricking in parts of Southeast Asia, longitudinal health metrics show no elevated morbidity in reproductive or urinary function compared to uncut populations. [34]

### Type II (Excision)

Type II, entailing partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora with or without labia majora, demonstrates marginally higher immediate risks than Type I, such as bleeding (median 32%, range 5–62%), but long-term physical findings remain sparse for severe outcomes, with evidence for bacterial vaginosis (AOR=1.68) graded as very low quality and not establishing direct causation. [33] Population-based exams in practicing communities reveal that most women experience no ongoing urinary obstruction or chronic pain attributable to the procedure, particularly absent reinfibulation or poor hygiene. [15] Confounding by discrimination or poverty likely inflates perceived links to obstetric issues like prolonged labor (AOR=1.49, low evidence quality). [33]

### Type III (Infibulation)

Type III, involving narrowing of the vaginal opening through excision and apposition of the labia, carries elevated immediate harm risks compared to Types I and II, including higher urine retention (median 31%) and potential for keloids, yet long-term physical health data indicate that deinfibulation or natural adaptation allows many women to maintain functionality without recurrent infections or fistulae. [33] Cohort studies report no procedure-independent elevation in physical morbidities, with adverse events more tied to social exclusion than anatomical changes (e.g., 16.6% recent gynecological events unrelated to FGC status). [15] Evidence for increased cesarean needs (AOR=1.60) is low quality, and absolute complication rates vary widely by practitioner skill, with hygienic variants showing minimal persistent effects. [33]

### Type IV (Other Practices)

Type IV encompasses pricking, piercing, or scraping without excision, yielding negligible physical health findings in reviews, with no documented long-term morbidity in empirical data from regions like Indonesia where such forms predominate. [34] Immediate bleeding or swelling occurs infrequently and resolves spontaneously, and no associations with chronic conditions emerge, aligning with the minimal tissue disruption. [33]

### Sexual and Psychological Self-Assessments

In surveys conducted among women in regions where female genital cutting (FGC) is prevalent, many self-report satisfactory sexual function, with no perceived diminishment in pleasure or intimacy. A cross-sectional study of 220 Egyptian women using the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) found no significant differences in overall sexual function scores between those who had undergone FGC (types I and II) and uncircumcised controls, including comparable ratings for desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, and satisfaction. [35] Similarly, a Nigerian community-based survey of 1,400 women reported that those with FGC were as likely as uncircumcised women to experience orgasm during intercourse, with self-described sexual sensations often unaltered or shifted to internal erogenous zones rather than eliminated. [28] Qualitative assessments in Sudanese and Somali communities reveal frequent self-reports of "normal" marital sex lives, where women attribute fulfillment to emotional bonding and vaginal sensitivity, dismissing Western assumptions of universal impairment.04269-6/fulltext)

Self-assessments of pain and dyspareunia also indicate minimal long-term impact for many. In a Saudi Arabian study of 260 women, FGC type I showed no differences in self-reported pain or desire compared to uncircumcised peers, with participants noting adaptation without ongoing discomfort. [36] These findings contrast with clinician-observed complications but align with self-perceptions in cultural contexts where FGC is normalized, suggesting that reported dysfunction may partly stem from societal stigma or interviewer bias rather than inherent physiological deficit.

Regarding psychological self-assessments, women with FGC in practicing communities often report unaltered well-being, viewing the procedure as a rite enhancing social integration rather than causing trauma. A review of self-reported mental health in African cohorts found no consistent elevation in depression or anxiety attributable solely to FGC, with many attributing resilience to cultural acceptance and familial support. [37] In Gambian and Kenyan surveys, cut women self-rated life satisfaction comparably to uncut peers, expressing pride in adherence to tradition without internalized harm narratives. [15] Psychological distress, when present, correlates more strongly with migration-induced acculturation stress or anti-FGC campaigns than the cutting event itself, per longitudinal self-reports. [38] These accounts challenge pathologizing frameworks, emphasizing subjective normalcy over imposed victimhood.

## Controversies and Critiques

### Challenges to the Universal Harm Assumption

Critiques of the assumption that female genital cutting (FGC) invariably causes severe, lifelong harm emphasize variability in procedure types, contexts, and outcomes, drawing on empirical data that highlight confounding factors like stigma and discrimination. A cross-sectional study of 638 Somali women in Arizona found no significant association between FGC status and psychological distress or adverse physical health outcomes (including sexual, obstetric, and gynecological issues) after adjusting for discrimination experiences, with self-reported discrimination increasing odds of distress by over fivefold (OR=5.11, 95% CI: 3.63–7.20) and physical issues by 2.68-fold (OR=2.68, 95% CI: 2.10–3.43). [15] This suggests that social determinants, rather than the cutting itself, drive many reported harms, challenging direct causal attributions in global narratives. [15]

Anthropological analyses further contest the World Health Organization's (WHO) uniform condemnation of all FGC forms, arguing that harm levels differ substantially by type and cultural setting, with milder procedures (e.g., Type I clitoridectomy) often yielding complication rates comparable to or lower than those of male genital cutting in analogous contexts. [39] Reviews of clinical data indicate diverse health impacts rather than inevitable severe consequences, with evidence from multiple studies showing that complications are not the norm and vary by practitioner skill and post-procedure care. [39] For instance, practices in some South and Southeast Asian Muslim communities result in minimal functional impairment, contrasting with more invasive African variants like infibulation, and underscoring that blanket harm assumptions overlook this spectrum. [39]

Self-reported experiences among women in FGC-practicing communities often reveal no perceived long-term trauma or sexual dissatisfaction, with many describing the procedure as culturally affirming rather than damaging, thereby questioning presumptions of universal psychological harm. [40] Empirical links between FGC and systemic patriarchy remain weakly established, as practices are frequently initiated and controlled by women for social cohesion, not male dominance, and reports of fulfilling sexual lives post-cutting contradict narratives of inherent debilitation. [40] These findings imply that anti-FGC campaigns may amplify harms through stigmatization and eroded healthcare trust, prioritizing ideological uniformity over context-specific evidence. [40]

### Cultural Agency Versus External Imposition

Critiques of female genital cutting (FGC) frequently portray the practice as an externally imposed form of patriarchal control, emphasizing male dominance in decision-making and enforcement. However, empirical evidence from ethnographic and survey research indicates substantial female agency, with women often acting as primary initiators, performers, and defenders of the practice within their communities. In regions such as Somaliland, elderly women hold authoritative roles in perpetuating FGC, viewing it as a mechanism for social cohesion, marriage eligibility, and cultural continuity rather than mere subjugation. [22] This agency manifests in women selecting practitioners—typically other women—and insisting on the procedure for their daughters to align with communal norms, challenging the narrative of unidirectional male imposition. [21]

Anthropological analyses further reveal that FGC serves as a domain of female autonomy and symbolic capital, where women negotiate power through ritual control, independent of male oversight in many settings. For instance, in Sudanese and Ethiopian communities, mothers and grandmothers coordinate cuttings as rites of passage, embedding the practice in matrilineal transmission of values that prioritize group identity over individual autonomy. [23] Such dynamics suggest that while patriarchal structures exist, FGC's persistence stems from internalized cultural logics upheld by women, who perceive discontinuation as a threat to familial honor and social standing. This contrasts with external critiques that attribute perpetuation solely to coercive gender hierarchies, potentially overlooking how women derive status and solidarity from the rite. [41]

Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) across practicing countries corroborate higher endorsement rates among women compared to men, underscoring self-perpetuation over imposed victimhood. In Egypt's 2014 DHS, 74% of ever-married women aged 15-49 supported continuation, exceeding male support levels in parallel assessments. [42] Similarly, in Mali's 2012-2013 DHS, 72% of women versus 65% of men favored the practice's persistence, with women's attitudes linked to perceived benefits like chastity preservation rather than deference to male authority. [43] These patterns hold in nations like Guinea and Sierra Leone, where female support often surpasses 80%, reflecting deliberate cultural endorsement by those directly affected. [44]

This evidence of cultural agency critiques the universal harm imposition model, arguing it imposes Western individualistic frameworks that dismiss local rationales and female-led enforcement. Interventions framed as liberation from patriarchy may falter by alienating women who view FGC as empowering within their value systems, as seen in resistance to bans that bypass community buy-in. [45] Recognizing agency does not endorse the practice but highlights the need for strategies engaging women's roles to disrupt self-sustaining cycles, rather than presuming external coercion alone. [39]

## Policy and Intervention Debates

### Effectiveness of Anti-Cutting Campaigns

International anti-female genital cutting campaigns, spearheaded by organizations such as UNICEF and WHO since the 1990s, have emphasized education, community mobilization, legal prohibitions, and public declarations to promote abandonment of the practice. [2] Despite these efforts, including the UNFPA-UNICEF Joint Programme launched in 2008 targeting 17 high-prevalence countries, the global number of women and girls living with the effects of cutting exceeded 230 million as of 2024, reflecting a 15% increase from 2016 estimates due to population growth outpacing relative declines in some regions. [46] [47]

A 2012 Campbell Collaboration systematic review of eight effectiveness studies across seven African countries found that while interventions like the Tostan education program improved knowledge of health risks and human rights (e.g., relative risk of disapproval among women in Burkina Faso at 1.04), there was no convincing evidence of reduced cutting prevalence from controlled evaluations. [48] Meta-analyses suggested potential declines among girls aged 0-10 (relative risk 0.77), but results were undermined by high heterogeneity, non-randomized designs, small samples, and confounding factors such as external media influences. Demographic and Health Survey data showed mixed trends, with increases in Burkina Faso (71.6% to 76.6%) and Nigeria (25.1% to 29.6%) alongside declines elsewhere, indicating limited causal impact attributable to campaigns. [48]

Legal bans, such as Kenya's 2011 Prohibition of Female Genital Mutilation Act, have often driven the practice underground and spurred medicalization rather than cessation. In Kenya, approximately 15% of cuttings are now performed by healthcare providers in clandestine settings, commercializing the procedure and potentially reducing immediate complications while evading enforcement. [49] [50] This shift reflects community adaptation to external pressures without altering underlying cultural valuations, where cutting is tied to rites of passage, marriageability, and social cohesion rather than recognized harm. [49]

Critiques highlight unintended consequences of zero-tolerance approaches, including eroded trust in health systems from surveillance and mandatory reporting, which deter women from seeking care, and exacerbation of stigma that marginalizes affected communities. [40] The persistence of cutting despite billions in funding—such as the Joint Programme's focus on attitude shifts yielding only modest, non-sustained behavioral changes—suggests campaigns overlook practitioner perspectives that prioritize cultural continuity over Western-framed harm narratives. [40] [48] Empirical gaps persist, with high-quality longitudinal studies scarce, underscoring the need for context-specific evaluations beyond assumptive models of universal detriment. [48]

### Medicalization and Harm Reduction Approaches

Medicalization of female genital cutting (FGC) involves trained health professionals performing the procedure under sterile conditions, with the aim of mitigating immediate risks such as excessive bleeding, infection, and urinary retention that arise from traditional, non-medical practitioners using unsterilized tools. Proponents advocate this as a harm reduction strategy, arguing it parallels regulated practices like male circumcision or tattooing, where professional oversight reduces acute complications without endorsing the cultural norm. In regions like Egypt, where medicalization rates reached approximately 74% by 2014, observational data indicate fewer immediate postoperative issues compared to traditional methods, though comprehensive longitudinal studies remain limited. [51] [52]

In Southeast Asia, particularly Indonesia and Malaysia, medicalized FGC often entails minimal interventions classified as Type I (partial or total removal of the clitoral prepuce) or symbolic pricking, performed routinely in clinical settings on infants. Surveys among Malaysian women who underwent such procedures report minimal or no perceived physical disruptions to daily life, sexual function, or childbirth, with practitioners citing hygiene and reduced scarring as benefits over traditional methods. A 2020 study in Malaysia documented over 60% of FGC performed by doctors or nurses, correlating with lower reported acute infections, though it noted an emerging trend toward more extensive cuttings not historically practiced, potentially offsetting gains. These self-assessments align with harm reduction goals, as women in these contexts frequently describe the procedure as culturally normative without long-term detriment. [53] [54]

Critics, including the World Health Organization, assert that medicalization legitimizes FGC, potentially increasing its prevalence and entrenching it across generations, while failing to address inherent long-term risks like dyspareunia or obstetric complications regardless of setting. The WHO's April 2025 guidelines explicitly oppose health worker involvement, citing evidence from high-medicalization areas like Egypt where overall FGC persistence undermines abandonment efforts, with no proven net reduction in total harm. Empirical comparisons remain scarce, but available data suggest immediate risks diminish under medical supervision—e.g., tetanus cases linked to traditional FGC dropped in medicalized cohorts—yet cultural normalization may amplify indirect harms through sustained practice. Harm reduction advocates counter that outright bans drive procedures underground, exacerbating dangers, and prioritize empirical risk minimization for consenting communities over ideological eradication. [55] [56]
