# Paraphimosis

Paraphimosis is a urologic emergency that occurs in uncircumcised males when the foreskin is retracted behind the glans penis but cannot be returned to its normal position, forming a constricting ring that impairs blood flow and leads to swelling, pain, and potential tissue damage. [1] [2] [3]

The condition typically arises from the failure to reposition the foreskin after retraction for activities such as cleaning, urination, or medical procedures like catheterization, though it can also result from retraction of the foreskin during erection (e.g., during sexual activity) without subsequent repositioning, penile injury, infection, or forceful manipulation during sexual activity. Paraphimosis itself does not cause erection; rather, erection-related retraction without reduction can lead to the condition. [3] [1] It is more prevalent among uncircumcised children aged 4 months to 12 years, with an incidence of about 0.2% in those with underlying foreskin issues, and affects approximately 1% of males over age 16, particularly elderly men in institutional settings due to poor hygiene or iatrogenic causes. [1] [2] Risk factors include phimosis (a tight foreskin), balanitis (inflammation of the glans), and diabetes, which can exacerbate swelling. [1] [3]

Symptoms manifest rapidly and include acute pain in the penis, edema and erythema of the glans and foreskin, and a characteristic "doughnut" appearance from the constricted band of skin around the coronal sulcus. [3] [1] As venous and lymphatic drainage becomes obstructed, the glans may become tender, tense, and discolored—ranging from red to blue, purple, or black—signaling progressive ischemia if circulation is severely compromised. [2] [1] In some cases, symptoms may initially be mild or even painless, but untreated progression can lead to ulceration or necrosis of the glans. [1]

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on physical examination revealing the trapped foreskin and assessing glans viability through color and tenderness, with no routine need for imaging unless complications like abscess are suspected. [1] [2] Treatment requires immediate intervention to restore blood flow, starting with conservative measures such as manual compression of the glans to reduce edema, application of osmotic agents like hypertonic saline or granulated sugar, or injection of hyaluronidase to facilitate reduction. [1] [3] If manual repositioning fails, urgent surgical options include a dorsal slit incision to relieve the constriction, followed by definitive circumcision to prevent recurrence. [1] [2]

Complications of untreated paraphimosis can be severe, including infection, permanent urinary obstruction, glans ischemia leading to gangrene, or in rare cases, partial penectomy. [3] [1] With prompt treatment, the prognosis is excellent, and most cases resolve without long-term sequelae, though recurrent episodes often necessitate circumcision as a preventive measure. [3] [2] Prevention involves educating uncircumcised individuals on gentle foreskin retraction and immediate repositioning after hygiene or procedures, alongside routine penile care to avoid balanoposthitis. [1] [3]

## Overview

### Definition

Paraphimosis is a urologic emergency that occurs exclusively in uncircumcised males, characterized by the retraction of the foreskin behind the corona of the glans penis, where it becomes constricted and cannot be returned to its normal position over the glans, resulting in venous congestion, edema, and potential ischemia of the distal penis. [4] [2] This condition arises when the tight ring of foreskin traps the glans, impairing lymphatic and venous drainage while allowing arterial inflow to continue, which exacerbates swelling and requires prompt intervention to prevent tissue damage. [3] [5]

The term "paraphimosis" derives from the Greek words para (meaning "beyond" or "resembling") and phimosis (meaning "muzzling" or "restriction"), reflecting its relation to but distinction from phimosis. [6] It has been documented in medical literature since the late 17th century, with early descriptions appearing in European surgical texts. [7]

A key characteristic of paraphimosis is its occurrence only in individuals with an intact foreskin, contrasting sharply with phimosis, which involves the inability to retract the foreskin over the glans due to tightness or adhesions, whereas paraphimosis specifically denotes the failure to replace the foreskin after it has been successfully retracted. [8]

### Relevant Anatomy

The foreskin, or prepuce, is a double-layered fold of tissue that covers the glans penis in uncircumcised males. It consists of an outer layer of keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, similar to the skin elsewhere on the body, and an inner mucosal layer composed of squamous mucous membrane that is thinner and more delicate, providing a protective interface with the glans. Between these layers lies the dartos fascia, a loose connective tissue layer containing smooth muscle fibers that allow the foreskin to contract and wrinkle, facilitating its retractable nature over the glans during erection or hygiene. This retractability is essential for normal function, enabling the foreskin to slide freely along the penile shaft without constriction. [9] [10]

The glans penis forms the bulbous distal expansion of the corpus spongiosum, characterized by a smooth, sensitive surface enriched with nerve endings. At its proximal border lies the corona, a prominent ridge that demarcates the glans from the penile shaft and serves as an anatomical landmark for the attachment of Buck's fascia. The frenulum, a midline fold of mucosa on the ventral aspect, anchors the inner foreskin to the underside of the glans near the urethral meatus, contributing to the stability of the preputial covering. Vascular supply to the glans and foreskin primarily arises from the dorsal artery of the penis, a branch of the internal pudendal artery, which runs along the dorsal surface; venous drainage occurs via the deep dorsal vein, which collects blood from a retrocoronal venous plexus around the corona and empties into the prostatic venous plexus. [11] [12]

The preputial space, the potential area between the inner foreskin and glans, facilitates lymphatic and venous drainage critical to penile homeostasis. Lymphatics from this space primarily drain to the superficial inguinal lymph nodes, with some pathways to deep inguinal and external iliac nodes, supporting fluid clearance from the mucosal surfaces. Venous outflow through the dorsal veins and emissary vessels prevents stagnation in the confined preputial compartment, where obstruction could theoretically promote fluid accumulation and edema due to the limited egress routes. [11] [12]

Developmentally, the foreskin originates from ectodermal folds that enclose the glans around the third to fourth month of gestation, forming as an invagination of the genital tubercle. In infancy, the inner foreskin remains fused to the glans by synechiae, a normal physiological adhesion that protects the developing structures and constitutes physiologic phimosis. Natural separation typically begins with desquamation and smegma production, with the foreskin becoming fully retractable in most boys during childhood and adolescence; studies indicate less than 1% have persistent physiologic phimosis by age 17 years. [11] [13]

## Clinical Presentation

### Signs and Symptoms

Paraphimosis typically presents with acute pain and tenderness in the glans penis and foreskin, resulting from the constricting ring formed by the retracted foreskin trapped behind the coronal sulcus. [1] This pain may vary in intensity but is often severe due to the mechanical obstruction and resulting vascular compromise. [2] Swelling of the glans and foreskin occurs rapidly as a direct consequence of venous engorgement distal to the tight band. [1]

Visible signs include a tight, constricting collar of foreskin visible behind the corona, with progressive edema of the exposed glans and prepuce. The glans often exhibits reddish-purple discoloration from venous stasis, which can intensify to bluish or darker hues as edema worsens within hours if untreated. [1] [2]

In adults, pain is the primary symptom, whereas children more commonly present with urinary obstruction. Associated symptoms may involve difficulty urinating due to the swelling obstructing the urethral meatus, potentially leading to urinary retention in more severe or prolonged cases. [14] If secondary infection develops, systemic signs such as fever can emerge alongside local inflammation. [2]

Symptoms generally escalate from initial mild discomfort to significant swelling and pain within 24 to 48 hours without intervention, driven by ongoing lymphatic and venous obstruction as described in the relevant penile anatomy. [1]

In toddlers presenting with a swollen penis, emergency care should be sought immediately if the foreskin is retracted and stuck behind the glans (paraphimosis, which cuts off blood flow causing severe swelling and pain); if there is severe pain; inability to urinate; or the penis appears blue or purple. Additional signs warranting urgent evaluation include fever, vomiting, or the child appearing very ill. If the swelling spreads to or includes the scrotum with sudden severe pain, it may indicate other issues such as testicular torsion, requiring immediate ER visit, though this is less common for isolated penile swelling. [2] [15] [16]

### Complications

If untreated, paraphimosis can lead to vascular compromise of the glans penis due to constriction by the retracted foreskin, resulting in impaired arterial inflow and venous outflow, which causes edema and ischemia. [4] Prolonged ischemia may progress to tissue necrosis or gangrene of the glans, with rare cases culminating in autoamputation or requiring partial penectomy. [17] Additionally, the constricting band can cause penile ulceration from pressure necrosis. [2]

In rare cases, external constrictive practices, such as the application of a rubber band around the foreskin, can cause gross preputial edema, discoloration, and necrosis of the foreskin, often requiring urgent circumcision. [18]

Secondary infections are a significant risk in paraphimosis, as edema and trapped moisture create an environment conducive to bacterial proliferation, leading to balanoposthitis (inflammation and infection of the glans and foreskin) or cellulitis of the surrounding penile skin. [1] These infectious complications can exacerbate swelling and pain, further delaying resolution if not addressed. [4]

Urinary complications arise from severe glans edema obstructing the urethral meatus, potentially causing acute urinary retention. [19] In prolonged cases, this obstruction may lead to hydronephrosis due to backpressure on the urinary tract. [19]

In extreme neglect, penile amputation may be necessary to prevent life-threatening gangrene. [3]

## Etiology

### Causes

Paraphimosis most frequently arises from iatrogenic factors, particularly in medical settings where the foreskin is retracted but not properly repositioned afterward. Common scenarios include urinary catheterization, cystoscopy, or routine penile examinations, especially among hospitalized or elderly patients in nursing homes who may lack the dexterity or awareness to restore the foreskin. [1] [4] [3]

Mechanical causes involve direct physical forces that retract and trap the foreskin without timely replacement. These include forceful manual retraction during personal hygiene, vigorous sexual intercourse or other sexual activity—particularly if the foreskin is retracted during erection and not returned to its normal position afterward—or self-examination, as well as less common triggers like penile piercings or embedded foreign bodies such as preputial beads. Paraphimosis itself does not cause erection; rather, failure to reduce the retracted foreskin following erection-related retraction can lead to the condition. [4] [1]

Rarely, external constriction of the foreskin using a rubber band has been reported, typically in cases of self-harm or misguided attempts at foreskin modification. This can cause gross preputial edema, dark red discoloration with clear demarcation at the constriction site, and progression to necrosis, potentially requiring urgent circumcision. Unlike classic paraphimosis, this mechanism does not involve retraction of the foreskin behind the glans, but produces similar complications through impaired blood flow. Immediate medical attention is required to prevent severe outcomes such as necrosis or infection. [18]

Inflammatory conditions can precipitate paraphimosis by inducing swelling of the glans or foreskin, which then becomes entrapped. Underlying balanitis, often due to poor hygiene leading to bacterial infections, or other infections such as those caused by Haemophilus ducreyi or Plasmodium falciparum, may cause sufficient edema to form a constricting ring; contact dermatitis and lichen sclerosus have also been implicated. [4] [3] [1]

The core pathophysiological mechanism begins with the retracted foreskin forming a tight constricting band behind the glans, which impairs venous and lymphatic drainage, leading to progressive edema of the glans and prepuce. As swelling intensifies, this constriction compromises arterial inflow, potentially resulting in ischemia and, if prolonged, tissue necrosis. [1] [4]

### Risk Factors

Paraphimosis predominantly affects uncircumcised males, as the presence of the foreskin is a prerequisite for the condition. Incidence is notably higher among infants and young children due to physiological phimosis, where the foreskin is naturally non-retractable and adherent to the glans, increasing the risk if forcibly retracted during cleaning or examination. In uncircumcised children aged 4 months to 12 years presenting with foreskin issues, the prevalence is approximately 0.2%. Among adolescents and adults, the condition is most common in the 10- to 14-year-old age group, with an overall prevalence of about 1% in uncircumcised males over 16 years. Elderly uncircumcised men also face elevated risk, particularly in institutional settings like nursing homes, where age-related loss of foreskin elasticity from chronic inflammation or scarring exacerbates vulnerability during medical interventions. [1] [2] [4]

Certain medical conditions predispose individuals to paraphimosis by promoting foreskin tightness, inflammation, or impaired tissue resilience. Congenital or acquired phimosis, characterized by a tight foreskin, is a primary underlying factor that hinders normal retraction and repositioning. Diabetes mellitus significantly increases susceptibility through recurrent balanitis and poor wound healing, which can lead to scarring and adhesions; nearly one-third of cases of acquired phimosis in adults are associated with undiagnosed or poorly controlled diabetes. Neurological disorders that impair mobility or self-care, such as those causing dependence on caregivers, further heighten risk by limiting proper foreskin management. Other contributing conditions include lichen sclerosus, which causes foreskin fibrosis, and peripheral vascular disease, common in the elderly, that compromises penile tissue health. [5] [1] [20]

Behavioral factors play a key role in precipitating paraphimosis among at-risk groups. Inadequate hygiene can foster smegma accumulation, infections, and adhesions between the foreskin and glans, making retraction more likely to become trapped. Frequent urinary catheterization, often in hospitalized or elderly patients, is a major iatrogenic risk, as the foreskin may be retracted for the procedure and not properly returned. These behaviors are amplified in populations reliant on others for care, where oversight in repositioning the foreskin post-retraction occurs. [5] [2]

Epidemiologically, paraphimosis is more prevalent in regions with low neonatal circumcision rates, such as parts of Europe, compared to the United States, where circumcision reduces incidence by eliminating the foreskin. Data on overall incidence remains limited, but estimates suggest that about 1% of uncircumcised males may require intervention for paraphimosis at some point, with higher rates in healthcare settings due to procedural factors. The condition exhibits a bimodal age distribution, peaking in childhood and again in older adulthood. [1] [4] [5]

## Diagnosis

### History and Physical Examination

The history-taking for paraphimosis begins with inquiring about recent events involving foreskin retraction, such as urethral catheterization, cystoscopy, endoscopic procedures, or hygiene practices that may have left the foreskin in a retracted position. [1] [21] Patients should also be asked about the duration of symptoms, which helps gauge the risk of progression to ischemia, typically developing over hours to days if untreated. [1] Pain level is assessed, as acute distal penile pain is common but may be absent in some cases, and any urinary symptoms, such as obstructive voiding or retention, are noted, particularly in pediatric patients. [21]

The physical examination focuses on direct visualization and gentle assessment of the penis to confirm the diagnosis. Inspection reveals a tight constricting band of foreskin behind the coronal sulcus, with marked edema and congestion of the glans and prepuce; the glans color is evaluated, where a pink or salmon hue indicates adequate blood supply, while dusky, pale, blue, or black discoloration suggests compromised viability. [1] [21] Gentle palpation assesses tenderness and the firmness of the glans, with soft and pliant tissue indicating viability and firm or inelastic areas potentially signaling necrosis. [21] During the exam, lubrication may be applied to facilitate an attempted manual reduction, and preputial edema is documented as mild (minimal swelling with easy reduction), moderate (significant swelling with moderate difficulty), or severe (marked congestion precluding non-invasive reduction). [1] [21]

Urgency is determined by signs of compromised circulation, including glans discoloration, cool temperature, or severe pain, which prompt immediate intervention to prevent tissue damage. [1] [21]

### Differential Diagnosis

Paraphimosis must be differentiated from other conditions presenting with penile swelling, pain, or foreskin abnormalities, as prompt recognition guides appropriate management. [1]

Phimosis involves the inability to retract the foreskin over the glans penis due to tightness or adhesions, typically presenting without the constricting ring seen in paraphimosis where the foreskin has already been retracted and becomes trapped behind the corona. [1] In contrast to the acute emergency of paraphimosis, phimosis is often chronic and non-progressive unless complicated by infection. [13]

Balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO), a form of lichen sclerosus, causes progressive scarring and fibrosis of the foreskin with characteristic white, atrophic plaques, leading to a fixed, non-retractable foreskin that mimics phimosis but lacks the dynamic entrapment of paraphimosis. [22] Unlike the venous congestion and edema in paraphimosis, BXO presents with chronic induration and may involve urethral meatal stenosis without acute ischemia. [22]

Penile trauma, including fracture, results from acute injury such as forceful bending during intercourse, causing localized swelling, ecchymosis, and a palpable hematoma or audible "snap," but without the circumferential constricting band of foreskin characteristic of paraphimosis. [23] Differentiation relies on history of trauma and absence of foreskin involvement. [23]

Other urologic emergencies like priapism involve a prolonged, painful erection due to impaired venous outflow or arterial inflow dysregulation, affecting the entire penile shaft rather than isolated glans edema from foreskin constriction in paraphimosis. [24] Fournier's gangrene, a necrotizing fasciitis, presents with rapidly spreading perineal infection, crepitus, systemic toxicity, and foul discharge, differing from the localized mechanical obstruction in paraphimosis, though untreated paraphimosis can predispose to such infections.

Diagnostic aids include swabbing the affected area to identify underlying infections such as balanitis, which may contribute to swelling and mimic or precipitate paraphimosis. [25] If glans viability is in question due to prolonged constriction, Doppler ultrasound can assess vascular flow to detect ischemia and guide urgency of intervention. [4]

## Management

### Initial Treatment

Paraphimosis is a urologic emergency, particularly in toddlers, requiring immediate presentation to the emergency department if suspected. Parents should seek emergency care promptly if the foreskin is retracted and stuck behind the glans, leading to severe swelling and pain due to compromised blood flow; if there is severe pain, inability to urinate, or the penis appears blue or purple; or if accompanied by fever, vomiting, or the child appears very ill. Swelling that spreads or includes the scrotum with sudden severe pain may indicate associated testicular issues, though less common in isolated penile swelling. These symptoms align with those described in the Signs and Symptoms section under Clinical Presentation. Immediate intervention is essential to restore blood flow and prevent complications such as tissue ischemia. [16] [15] [2]

The initial treatment of paraphimosis focuses on prompt, non-invasive interventions to reduce the constricted foreskin and alleviate edema, aiming to restore normal anatomy and prevent ischemia. Manual reduction is the first-line approach and involves gentle compression of the edematous glans and foreskin for 2-10 minutes to displace fluid proximally, followed by lubrication with an anesthetic gel such as 2% lidocaine and steady upward traction on the foreskin to slide it over the glans. [1] [26] [27] This technique is often facilitated by wrapping the penis with an elastic bandage or gauze for 5-20 minutes prior to reduction to further minimize swelling. [21] [1]

If edema hinders manual reduction, osmotic therapies are employed to draw fluid from the tissues via an osmotic gradient. Common methods include applying granulated sugar directly to the glans and foreskin, or wrapping the area with gauze soaked in 20% mannitol or hypertonic saline for 30-45 minutes, sometimes covered with a glove or condom to maintain contact. [1] [26] [28] For cases with refractory edema, minimally invasive techniques such as the puncture method—making 10-20 punctures in the edematous foreskin with a 21- to 27-gauge needle to allow fluid seepage—or glans aspiration using an 18- to 20-gauge needle to remove 3-12 mL of blood/fluid from the glans can be used to decompress the tissues. [1] [26] [29] [28] Ice packs may also be used intermittently as an adjunct to promote vasoconstriction and reduce swelling, though care is taken to avoid prolonged application that could exacerbate ischemia. [21] [27] These osmotic and decompression measures typically require 1-2 hours for effect and are particularly useful in cases with significant swelling. [26]

Pharmacologic aids enhance comfort and efficacy during reduction. Local anesthetics, such as topical EMLA cream (2.5% lidocaine and prilocaine) applied for 30 minutes or a dorsal penile nerve block with 1% lidocaine (2.5-10 mL injected at the 10 and 2 o'clock positions), are administered to manage pain without systemic effects. [1] [21] [26] Hyaluronidase (150-1500 units injected subcutaneously around the constricting band) may also be used to disperse extracellular edema and facilitate foreskin reduction. [1] [26] [30] In the absence of infection, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may be given orally to further reduce inflammation and edema. [1]

Following successful reduction, the penis is monitored for recurrence, with observation for 1-2 hours to ensure stable repositioning and adequate urinary output. Any abrasions are cleaned and treated with topical antibiotic ointment, and patients are advised to avoid foreskin retraction for at least one week. [1] [27] Conservative methods like these achieve success in approximately 80-90% of cases when initiated promptly, though recurrence risk remains without addressing underlying phimosis. [1] [31]

### Surgical Options

Surgical options for paraphimosis are indicated when conservative reduction methods fail, particularly in cases of recurrent episodes or severe constriction leading to ischemia. [1] The dorsal slit procedure serves as a minor surgical intervention performed under local anesthesia to relieve the constricting band. [32] In this technique, a 1- to 2-cm incision is made dorsally at the 12 o'clock position on the tight foreskin using hemostats to crush and divide the constricting tissue, followed by closure with absorbable sutures such as 3-0 or 4-0 chromic catgut. [1] This approach allows immediate reduction of the foreskin and prevents further vascular compromise without requiring general anesthesia, making it suitable for emergency settings. [32]

For definitive management, circumcision remains the standard procedure to eliminate the risk of recurrence by removing the foreskin entirely or partially. [21] Performed electively after initial stabilization, it involves excision of the prepuce under local or general anesthesia, with excellent outcomes in preventing future episodes, as paraphimosis does not recur following a proper circumcision. [4] In patients where foreskin preservation is preferred, such as for cultural or medical reasons, preputioplasty offers an alternative by widening the preputial opening through techniques like Y-V plasty or Z-plasty, which involve longitudinal incisions and transverse suturing to increase elasticity without full removal. [33] These methods achieve high success rates, with retractability reported in up to 100% of cases and low complication rates comparable to circumcision. [33]

In emergency situations involving prolonged ischemia of the glans, urgent surgical intervention is required to avert necrosis. [1] If gangrene develops, debridement of necrotic tissue may be necessary, potentially extending to partial penectomy in severe cases to preserve penile function. [1] Post-operative care for all procedures includes wound cleaning with mild soap and water, application of topical antibiotics like bacitracin to prevent infection, and avoidance of foreskin retraction for at least one week to allow healing. [1] Following circumcision, recurrence rates approach zero, emphasizing its role in long-term prevention. [4]

## Prevention

### Hygiene and Care

Maintaining proper hygiene is essential for preventing paraphimosis in individuals with an uncircumcised penis. The foreskin should be gently retracted only as far as it comfortably allows during cleaning, followed by rinsing the area underneath with warm water to remove smegma and debris without causing irritation. [34] Harsh soaps or perfumed products should be avoided, as they can lead to skin irritation and inflammation; instead, plain warm water or a mild, non-irritating soap may be used sparingly if necessary. [35]

After any retraction of the foreskin—for cleaning, urination, or other activities—it must be immediately returned to its natural position covering the glans to prevent swelling and entrapment. [1] Failure to do so can result in edema of the glans, increasing the risk of paraphimosis. [2] Daily gentle cleaning routines, integrated into regular bathing, help maintain penile health without excessive manipulation. [36]

For children, parents should be educated on avoiding forcible retraction of the foreskin, as this can cause pain, tearing, or adhesions between the foreskin and glans. [37] In infants and young boys, the foreskin naturally separates over time, and cleaning should involve only washing the external penis with warm water during baths, without attempting to pull back the foreskin until it retracts voluntarily, typically around ages 3 to 5. [34] Parents should monitor for signs of adhesions or inflammation and consult a healthcare provider if issues arise, emphasizing gentle care to support normal development. [38]

Individuals with diabetes require special attention to hygiene due to their heightened susceptibility to infections like balanoposthitis, which can contribute to foreskin complications. [39] More frequent but still gentle cleaning of the genital area with warm water is recommended to prevent bacterial or fungal overgrowth, with prompt return of the foreskin to its covering position after each session. [40] This diligent approach helps mitigate infection risks associated with poor glycemic control. [39]

### Prophylactic Measures

Prophylactic measures for paraphimosis primarily target underlying phimosis and procedural risks to enhance foreskin mobility and prevent entrapment. Topical corticosteroids, such as 0.05-0.1% betamethasone or hydrocortisone, applied twice daily for 4-8 weeks, effectively treat mild phimosis in uncircumcised males by increasing skin elasticity and retraction ease, with success rates exceeding 80% in reducing the risk of subsequent paraphimosis. [41] This non-invasive approach is recommended as a first-line intervention before considering more definitive procedures, particularly in pediatric and young adult populations where phimosis contributes to paraphimosis incidence. [42]

Elective circumcision is advised for individuals with recurrent paraphimosis or persistent phimosis unresponsive to conservative treatments, eliminating the foreskin and thereby preventing future episodes in high-risk groups such as those with balanitis xerotica obliterans or cultural predispositions to delayed intervention. [43] This procedure serves as a reliable prophylactic strategy, nearly eliminating paraphimosis risk while also offering broader benefits like reduced penile carcinoma incidence, though it is typically reserved for cases where non-surgical options fail. [44]

In healthcare settings, standardized protocols during urinary catheterization are crucial to avert iatrogenic paraphimosis, which accounts for a significant proportion of cases in hospitalized patients. Caregivers must be trained to immediately reposition the foreskin over the glans upon completing catheter insertion or any penile manipulation, as failure to do so can lead to constriction within hours. [1] Patient education in clinical environments emphasizes recognizing early retraction issues and seeking prompt care, integrated into discharge instructions for uncircumcised males undergoing procedures. [21]

Regular urologic follow-up is recommended for at-risk populations, including elderly uncircumcised males who experience fewer erections that naturally maintain foreskin pliability, allowing monitoring for phimosis progression and timely intervention. [45] These check-ups, typically scheduled every 6-12 months post-episode or in chronic cases, facilitate early detection and reinforce preventive strategies without necessitating routine invasive measures. [27]
