# Brit shalom (naming ceremony)

Brit Shalom, meaning "covenant of peace," is a contemporary Jewish naming ceremony developed as a non-surgical alternative to the traditional Brit Milah ritual for uncircumcised newborn boys and also used for newborn girls, typically on the eighth day after birth to symbolize entry into the Abrahamic covenant through blessings, naming, and communal welcome rather than physical alteration. [1] [2] The rite, officiated by a rabbi rather than a mohel, centers on reciting parental vows, assigning a Hebrew name often honoring ancestors, and affirming the child's spiritual place within Judaism, drawing from progressive interpretations that prioritize ethical and inclusive practices over ancient mandates for male circumcision. [3] [4] Emerging in Reform and Reconstructionist communities since the late 20th century, it reflects adaptations to modern sensibilities on bodily autonomy while invoking biblical themes of peace and wholeness, though Orthodox traditions view it as insufficient to fulfill covenantal obligations outlined in Genesis.

## History

### Origins in Modern Judaism

The Brit Shalom ceremony emerged in the late 20th century within non-Orthodox streams of Judaism, particularly Humanistic Judaism, as a ritual alternative to the traditional brit milah that omits circumcision while affirming the covenant through naming and communal welcome. Humanistic Judaism, founded in the early 1960s by Rabbi Sherwin Wine, inherently questioned the necessity of circumcision as a religious obligation, viewing it as a cultural rather than supernatural imperative; Wine reportedly officiated one of the earliest known such ceremonies around 1970, though without initially employing the specific term "Brit Shalom." This reflected broader modernist reinterpretations of Jewish practice, emphasizing ethical humanism and parental choice over ancient mandates rooted in Genesis 17. [5]

By the mid-1980s, the practice gained traction through grassroots efforts, with Rabbi Nathan Segal (1949–2019) conducting some of the first documented rabbi-led Brit Shalom ceremonies, possibly introducing the term "Brit Shalom" (Hebrew for "covenant of peace") in the early 1980s to contrast with "brit milah" (covenant of circumcision). Segal, a non-circumcised Jew and performer who appeared at the 1969 Woodstock festival, operated in a context where sympathetic rabbis shared improvised liturgies informally, often via photocopied documents, due to resistance from mainstream institutions. These early rituals focused on bestowing a Hebrew name, blessings, and family participation, adapting elements of the brit milah—such as timing on the eighth day—without surgical intervention, amid rising parental concerns over infant pain, infection risks (generally under 1% per medical studies), and bodily autonomy. [6] [7] [8]

Formalization accelerated in the 1990s and 2000s through advocacy by figures like Lisa Braver Moss, who critiqued circumcision in a 1990 essay, and later collaborated with Rebecca Wald to outline standardized ceremonies in their 2015 book Celebrating Brit Shalom. This publication disseminated sample rituals, drawing from Reconstructionist, Reform, and secular Jewish traditions, and coincided with outreach at events like the Reform movement's 2015 convention. The ceremony's development paralleled declining U.S. circumcision rates (from about 65% in the early 1980s to 58% by 2010, per CDC data) and intactivist influences, though it remained marginal, with estimates of dozens to hundreds of annual performances by the 2010s, primarily in progressive communities. Sources promoting Brit Shalom, often tied to anti-circumcision advocacy, highlight its alignment with modern ethics, yet Orthodox Judaism uniformly rejects it as insufficient for covenantal entry. [9] [10]

### Key Figures and Organizational Development

Rabbi Sherwin Wine, founder of the Society for Humanistic Judaism, performed the earliest documented Brit Shalom ceremony around 1970 as a non-circumcision alternative for welcoming male infants into the Jewish covenant. [11] This initiative aligned with Humanistic Judaism's emphasis on secular, rational reinterpretations of Jewish rituals, prioritizing ethical concerns over traditional practices involving physical alteration.

In 2002, Dr. Mark Reiss, a physician and Jewish advocate against routine infant circumcision, established an online directory of rabbis and lay officiants willing to conduct Brit Shalom ceremonies, expanding access beyond Humanistic circles to include progressive and Renewal Judaism rabbis. [12] The list, initially hosted on personal and advocacy sites, grew to include over 150 celebrants by the 2020s, facilitating ceremonies in multiple countries and denominations while highlighting medical and ethical objections to circumcision. [12]

Organizational growth accelerated in the 2010s with the founding of Beyond the Bris in 2010 by Rebecca Wald, a forum providing resources, personal stories, and ceremony guides for Jewish parents opting out of Brit Milah. [13] This grassroots effort emphasized community support amid social stigma, drawing from intactivist movements while rooting alternatives in Jewish textual reinterpretations of covenant as symbolic rather than surgical. In 2021, Bruchim emerged as a dedicated nonprofit, assuming stewardship of the celebrant directory and offering standardized ceremony templates, educational materials, and advocacy to normalize Brit Shalom within broader Jewish life-cycle events. [1] These developments reflect a shift from isolated rabbinic innovations to networked support structures, though adoption remains marginal, with most ceremonies still led by independent rabbis rather than denominational institutions. [14]

## Religious and Theological Context

### Comparison to Brit Milah

Brit Shalom, as a non-circumcision welcoming ceremony, contrasts sharply with Brit Milah, the traditional Jewish covenantal rite of circumcision for male infants mandated in Genesis 17:10-14 and performed on the eighth day after birth by a trained mohel. While Brit Milah incorporates surgical removal of the foreskin as a physical sign of the Abrahamic covenant, symbolizing Jewish identity and continuity, Brit Shalom omits any invasive procedure, emphasizing symbolic rituals like foot-washing, naming, and parental vows to raise the child in Jewish tradition. This distinction arises from ethical concerns over neonatal circumcision's medical risks, including pain, infection (occurring in 0.2-0.6% of cases per CDC data), and potential long-term effects on sexual function, which proponents of Brit Shalom cite as justification for an alternative.

Theologically, Brit Milah is viewed in Orthodox and Conservative Judaism as a halachic obligation with spiritual significance, including the belief that circumcision refines the child's soul and integrates the body into God's covenant, a perspective reinforced in Talmudic texts like Shabbat 132a. In contrast, Brit Shalom, developed in the late 20th century within Reform and Reconstructionist circles, reinterprets the covenant as relational and ethical rather than corporeal, drawing on prophetic ideals of justice over ritual sacrifice (e.g., Isaiah 1:16-17), though critics argue this dilutes biblical imperatives. Orthodox authorities, such as Rabbi Moshe Feinstein, have deemed non-circumcision a violation of divine command, potentially barring the child from full covenantal status until rectified.

Ceremonially, both rites often occur in a home or synagogue setting with blessings, Torah readings, and communal participation, but Brit Milah includes unique elements like the mohel's declaration "Just as he entered the covenant, so may he enter Torah, marriage, and good deeds," absent in Brit Shalom's gender-neutral adaptations. Brit Shalom extends eligibility to girls, addressing historical gender disparities in infant rituals, whereas Brit Milah applies solely to boys, with girls' naming (Simchat Bat) as a separate, less formalized tradition. Adoption rates reflect denominational divides: Brit Milah remains near-universal among Orthodox Jews (over 99% compliance per surveys), while Brit Shalom appeals to a minority of non-Orthodox parents opting out of circumcision, per reports from progressive Jewish organizations.

### Views Across Jewish Denominations

Orthodox Judaism maintains that the brit milah, including circumcision, constitutes a binding biblical commandment (Genesis 17:10-14), rendering Brit Shalom an invalid substitute for male infants, as it omits the required surgical act central to the covenant. [15] Orthodox authorities, such as those in traditional halachic responsa, emphasize that failure to circumcise on the eighth day incurs severe spiritual consequences, including karet (excision from the community), and no alternative ritual can fulfill this obligation. [16]

Conservative Judaism similarly upholds circumcision as essential to the brit milah rite, with the Rabbinical Assembly's Committee on Jewish Law and Standards affirming it as non-negotiable for covenantal entry, paralleling requirements in conversion processes where even prior circumcision necessitates hatafat dam brit (extraction of a drop of blood). [17] While Conservative synagogues may conduct naming ceremonies for girls (simchat bat), Brit Shalom for uncircumcised boys is not recognized as equivalent, though some individual rabbis might offer supportive rituals without endorsing non-compliance. [16]

Reform Judaism, emphasizing personal autonomy and ethical considerations, does not mandate circumcision as halachically obligatory but traditionally encourages brit milah as a marker of Jewish identity and continuity. [18] Some Reform rabbis perform Brit Shalom ceremonies for boys whose parents forgo circumcision, framing it as a welcoming ritual focused on peace and community inclusion, particularly in progressive congregations where parental choice prevails over ritual uniformity. [19]

Reconstructionist Judaism approaches Brit Shalom with openness to ritual evolution, viewing circumcision as culturally significant but not inviolable, and advocating reconstruction of brit milah to align with contemporary ethics, such as bodily integrity, while preserving covenantal themes through non-surgical alternatives. [20]

Humanistic and secular Jewish movements, such as those affiliated with the Society for Humanistic Judaism, explicitly recognize and promote Brit Shalom as a covenant of peace, conducting ceremonies that emphasize humanistic values, family commitment, and Jewish heritage without supernatural or surgical elements. [21]

## Ceremony Description

### Core Elements and Rituals

The Brit Shalom ceremony centers on welcoming a newborn into the Jewish covenant through non-surgical rituals emphasizing naming, blessings, and communal affirmation, typically led by a rabbi or spiritual officiant without the circumcision performed in brit milah. [2] [1] Core elements include the public announcement of the child's Hebrew name, selected by parents to honor ancestors, biblical figures, or desired attributes, thereby integrating the infant into Jewish tradition and family lineage. [8] [2] Blessings are recited over kosher wine or grape juice and challah bread, invoking divine protection and the Shehecheyanu prayer to celebrate the occasion's sanctity. [8] [1]

Rituals often incorporate symbolic acts to signify wholeness and transition, such as slicing a pomegranate to represent the 613 mitzvot and the child's potential for ethical deeds, or lighting a candle for warmth and healing. [8] [1] The baby may be wrapped in a tallit or have their feet washed with warm water, drawing from Torah imagery of hospitality and respect. [8] [1] Family members and honored guests, including roles like kvatter (who carries the child) or sandek (a symbolic holder), participate in readings, songs such as "Olam chesed yibaneh" ("We will build this world from love"), and shared expressions of values like justice and integrity. [1] A naming certificate may document the event for future lifecycle milestones. [2]

The ceremony concludes with a festive meal featuring traditional foods like challah, bagels, and spreads, fostering community bonds. [2] [1] While paralleling brit milah in structure—gathering participants, invoking covenantal themes, and ending in feasting—Brit Shalom omits genital cutting, prioritizing bodily intactness as a form of covenantal peace. [8] Variations allow flexibility in location (home, synagogue, or outdoors) and additional personalized elements, such as ribbon braiding or poetry, to suit family preferences. [8] [1]

### Timing, Participants, and Variations

Brit Shalom ceremonies are commonly scheduled on the eighth day following the infant's birth, paralleling the timing of the traditional brit milah, though this is not a halachic requirement and families often select a more convenient date based on logistical factors such as weather, travel, or health considerations. [8] [1] [2]

The primary participants include the parents, the newborn, and an officiant—typically a rabbi, cantor, or trained lay celebrant sympathetic to the practice—who leads the proceedings and bestows the Hebrew name. Extended family members, friends, and honored guests may attend, with traditional roles like kvatter (a couple who carries the baby into the room) or sandek (a holder for the child) sometimes adapted or assigned to symbolize community welcome. [1] [8] [22]

Lacking a prescribed liturgy, Brit Shalom exhibits significant variations tailored to family preferences, denomination, or officiant style, often incorporating symbolic rituals such as blessings over wine and challah, foot washing, ribbon braiding, or cutting a pomegranate to represent wholeness and covenant entry without physical alteration. Ceremonies may occur at home, in a synagogue, outdoors, or other settings, with customizable elements drawn from resources like the 2015 book Celebrating Brit Shalom, which outlines multiple ritual templates including prayers, readings, and festive meals ranging from simple potlucks to catered events. [1] [8] [2] [22]

## Adoption and Popularity

### Demographic and Geographic Trends

Brit Shalom ceremonies are primarily adopted by non-Orthodox Jewish families, including those identifying with Reform, Reconstructionist, or Humanistic Judaism, as well as secular or culturally Jewish parents who prioritize bodily autonomy and reject surgical circumcision. [23] These families often include influences from the natural childbirth movement, contemporary feminism, and concerns over infant pain or ethical issues with non-therapeutic procedures. [23] Quantitative demographic data remains scarce due to the private, non-institutional nature of the practice and lack of centralized tracking, but anecdotal reports highlight its appeal among interfaith, LGBTQ+, and multi-ethnic Jewish households seeking inclusive rituals. [24]

Geographically, Brit Shalom is most prevalent in North America, particularly the United States, where progressive Jewish communities in urban areas facilitate access to supportive rabbis and organizations like Beyond the Bris. [23] In Israel, adoption occurs mainly among secular Jews, with estimates from the early 2010s indicating that 1-2% of Jewish male infants avoided circumcision, a subset of whom underwent Brit Shalom or similar naming rites; a 2006 survey reported a higher non-circumcision rate of 4.8% among Israeli parents. [23] Presence in Europe and other diaspora communities is noted but limited, often tied to humanistic or anti-circumcision advocacy groups rather than mainstream synagogues.

Adoption trends reflect gradual growth since the early 2000s, driven by rising awareness through online resources and intactivist campaigns, with Beyond the Bris documenting incremental increases in website traffic and parental inquiries following its 2010 launch. [23] Efforts to formalize ceremonies, such as crowdfunding for dedicated Brit Shalom ritual books, underscore expanding interest among Jews questioning traditional practices amid broader declines in U.S. circumcision rates. [23] In 2021, a new U.S. Jewish group emerged aiming to normalize non-circumcision and encourage synagogue support for alternatives like Brit Shalom. [25] However, the practice remains marginal within global Judaism, comprising a tiny fraction of infant rituals compared to brit milah, with no evidence of widespread institutional endorsement outside progressive denominations.

### Motivations for Choosing Brit Shalom

Parents elect Brit Shalom as an alternative to Brit Milah primarily due to ethical objections to neonatal circumcision, emphasizing the procedure's status as a non-therapeutic surgery performed without the infant's consent, which they regard as a violation of bodily autonomy and an infliction of unnecessary pain and potential trauma. [8] This perspective aligns with broader intactivist arguments that prioritize the child's right to an intact body, viewing the foreskin as a functional anatomical structure with protective roles rather than superfluous tissue warranting removal. [8]

Health-related motivations also feature prominently, with parents citing documented risks of circumcision complications—such as infection, excessive bleeding, or improper healing, which occur in approximately 0.4% of infant cases per comprehensive reviews—alongside concerns over acute pain and possible long-term psychological effects from early trauma. [26] In interfaith or secular Jewish families, these issues are compounded by discomfort from non-Jewish partners who may view the ritual cutting as akin to genital modification practices scrutinized in human rights contexts, prompting a search for inclusive alternatives that avoid such elements. [27]

Theologically, adherents in progressive Jewish circles reinterpret the Abrahamic covenant as achievable through symbolic affirmation of life and community inclusion rather than physical alteration, framing Brit Shalom as a "covenant of peace" that honors Jewish heritage without mandating excision, a view supported by rabbis in Reform and Reconstructionist traditions who facilitate such ceremonies to affirm parental autonomy. [20] Personal anxieties over the vulnerability of newborns further drive choices, with some parents deferring any decision to the child at maturity (e.g., age 13 per bar mitzvah customs) to mitigate immediate distress while preserving ritual welcoming. [27] These motivations reflect a tension between tradition and modern ethical sensibilities, often leading families to customize ceremonies with blessings, naming, and communal participation to maintain cultural continuity. [8]

## Controversies and Criticisms

### Theological and Halachic Objections

From the perspective of Orthodox Judaism and traditional halachic authorities, Brit Shalom fails to fulfill the biblical commandment of brit milah, which requires the physical circumcision of male infants on the eighth day as a perpetual sign of the covenant between God and the Jewish people, as stipulated in Genesis 17:10-14. This mitzvah is classified as a positive commandment (mitzvah aseh) of exceptional importance, surpassing many others in obligatory force, with non-compliance incurring severe spiritual consequences including karet (excision from the community). [28]

The Shulchan Aruch (Yoreh De'ah 260-261) explicitly mandates that a father must circumcise his son; if he neglects this, the rabbinic court (beit din) is obligated to intervene and perform it, and upon maturity, the individual himself bears the responsibility, underscoring the non-optional nature of the act. [28] Rabbi Eliezer Melamed emphasizes that brit milah embodies an eternal, indelible bond between the Jewish soul and divine purpose, etched into the body itself, rendering any ceremony omitting circumcision theologically deficient and a rejection of this core covenantal element. [28]

Orthodox rabbis have critiqued Brit Shalom as an ahistorical innovation that trivializes or circumvents halachic imperatives. Rabbi Naftali Brawer describes a non-circumcision brit as "newfangled" and "a synthetic ceremony highlighting the absence of brit," arguing it substitutes ritual symbolism for the mandated physical compliance essential to Jewish identity and continuity. [29] Such views hold that while Jewish status derives matrilineally regardless of circumcision, forgoing the procedure constitutes deliberate disobedience to Torah law, potentially weakening communal adherence to mitzvot and echoing historical assaults on Jewish practice, such as Roman bans on circumcision aimed at eradicating the faith.

### Health, Ethical, and Social Debates

Proponents of Brit Shalom argue that forgoing neonatal circumcision avoids documented procedural risks, including bleeding, infection, and rare but severe complications such as penile damage, which occur in approximately 1-2% of cases according to systematic reviews of surgical outcomes. [30] They contend that purported health benefits, such as reduced urinary tract infections (UTIs) in infancy and lower heterosexual HIV transmission rates later in life, can be mitigated through hygiene practices and safe sex, rendering the intervention unnecessary and disproportionate to its harms. [31] In contrast, medical bodies like the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) maintain that evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses shows benefits outweigh risks, including a 90% reduction in penile cancer and approximately 90% lower UTI incidence (10-fold reduction) in the first year, positioning circumcision as a net positive for public health when performed neonatally under sterile conditions. [32] Critics of Brit Shalom highlight that uncircumcised males face elevated risks of conditions like balanitis and phimosis, potentially requiring later interventions with higher complication rates, though long-term population studies in low-circumcision regions like Europe show no epidemic of such issues attributable to intact status. [30]

Ethically, advocates for Brit Shalom emphasize infant bodily autonomy and the principle of informed consent, viewing circumcision as a non-therapeutic alteration of healthy tissue that violates the child's right to an intact body, akin to arguments advanced in bioethics literature against irreversible procedures without medical necessity. [33] In Jewish contexts, some progressive rabbis and scholars reinterpret the biblical covenant (Genesis 17:10-14) as symbolic rather than requiring physical cutting, proposing Brit Shalom as a faithful alternative that honors peace (shalom) over ritual injury, while acknowledging halachic traditions that deem uncircumcised males ineligible for certain communal roles. [34] Opponents counter that parental authority in religious upbringing, supported by legal precedents in many jurisdictions affirming religious circumcision, supersedes minor autonomy claims, and that forgoing the rite undermines intergenerational covenantal obligations, potentially eroding Jewish identity; this tension is evident in debates where ethicists weigh cultural continuity against modern human rights frameworks like those from the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. [35] Empirical data on regret is limited, but surveys of circumcised adults show mixed satisfaction, with some reporting diminished sensitivity, fueling ethical scrutiny independent of religious framing. [31]

Socially, Brit Shalom has sparked debates over community integration, with Orthodox and Conservative Jews often viewing non-circumcision as a rejection of core halachah, leading to potential exclusion from rituals like mikveh immersion or marriage matchmaking in traditional circles, as reported in anecdotal accounts from families opting for the alternative. [36] In Reform and secular Jewish communities, adoption is rising, with organizations like Bruchim providing ceremonies to affirm belonging without surgery, yet parents frequently encounter stigma or family pressure, exemplified by cases where uncircumcised boys face teasing or identity questions in Jewish education settings. [27] Broader societal shifts, including intactivist campaigns, frame Brit Shalom as empowering parental choice amid declining U.S. circumcision rates (from 64.5% in 1979 to 58.3% in 2010 per CDC data [9]), but critics argue it risks diluting Jewish distinctiveness in multicultural contexts, where foreskin intactness aligns with majority norms but may complicate interfaith relationships or aliyah processes in Israel requiring proof of circumcision for some military exemptions. [14] These dynamics underscore a divide between tradition-bound cohesion and individualistic ethics, with no consensus on long-term social outcomes for Brit Shalom-raised children.

## Recognition and Impact

### Institutional and Community Acceptance

Brit Shalom ceremonies are performed by rabbis and spiritual leaders primarily within progressive Jewish denominations, including Reform, Reconstructionist, and Jewish Renewal movements, as well as secular Humanistic Judaism. These groups view the ritual as a valid adaptation that emphasizes ethical concerns over strict adherence to traditional circumcision, allowing families to welcome male infants into the covenant without surgery. For instance, networks like Bruchim connect families with officiants trained in such ceremonies, reflecting acceptance in non-Orthodox circles where halachic requirements are interpreted flexibly. [1]

In contrast, Orthodox Judaism categorically rejects Brit Shalom as a substitute for brit milah, maintaining that Genesis 17:10-14 mandates physical circumcision for covenantal inclusion, rendering uncircumcised males ineligible for full ritual participation until adulthood. Conservative Judaism similarly upholds circumcision as obligatory under halacha, with rabbinical authorities like the Rabbinical Assembly not endorsing alternatives despite occasional debates on procedure. No major Orthodox or Conservative institutions officially recognize or promote Brit Shalom, often counseling families toward compliance with traditional practice.

Community acceptance remains niche and geographically varied, concentrated in liberal urban areas of North America, the UK, and Israel among secular or progressive Jews. Organizations such as Beyond the Bris and Intact America advocate for it, providing resources like the 2015 book Celebrating Brit Shalom by Lisa Braver Moss and Rebecca Wald, which documents over 50 sample ceremonies developed by supportive rabbis. However, even in accepting communities, participants may face social stigma from traditionalist relatives or synagogues. [13] [8]

### Broader Cultural Implications

Brit Shalom signifies a shift toward secular reinterpretations of Jewish ritual, enabling families to maintain covenantal symbolism—rooted in Genesis 17—without physical alteration, thereby prioritizing bodily autonomy and ethical consent in an era of heightened scrutiny over infant medical procedures. Emerging in the mid-1980s through grassroots efforts by rabbis like Nathan Segal, the ceremony reflects broader trends in humanistic and progressive Judaism, where personal choice supplants halachic absolutism, as endorsed by organizations such as the Society for Humanistic Judaism. [1] This adaptation addresses the cultural assumption of circumcision as a Jewish default, particularly in North American communities where Reform and secular parents increasingly opt out, reducing stigma around non-conformity and promoting transparent family practices. [37]

In wider Jewish culture, Brit Shalom parallels expansions like simchat bat for girls, fostering ritual equity and inclusivity by accommodating intersex or gender-diverse children, thus challenging male-centric traditions and aligning with modern values of non-binary recognition. [8] Among secular Jews, including Israeli atheists, it sustains ethnic and communal ties sans orthodoxy, embodying a heterodox vitality that evolves customs to fit Enlightenment-derived principles of individualism. [38] Such innovations underscore Judaism's capacity for internal pluralism, potentially influencing global intactivist discourses by framing non-circumcision as compatible with heritage rather than rejection thereof.

Critics within Orthodox circles contend this dilutes the covenant's biblical mandate, yet its rise—facilitated by resources like officiant directories—signals eroding taboos around ritual questioning, contributing to fragmented yet resilient Jewish identities amid assimilation pressures. [1] By customizing elements like symbolic fruit-cutting or tallit-wrapping, Brit Shalom exemplifies ritual creativity, mirroring historical reforms while inviting debates on whether spiritual essence inheres in intent over form. [39]
