# Circumcision in China

Circumcision in China involves the surgical removal of the foreskin from the penis, a procedure with low overall prevalence of approximately 2-5% among males, primarily limited among the Han majority to medical indications such as phimosis and typically performed in adulthood, while holding greater religious significance among Muslim ethnic minorities like the Hui and Uyghur. [1] [2] [3]

Historically, the practice lacks deep roots in traditional Han Chinese culture, contrasting with its ritual importance in Islamic communities where it serves as a rite of passage for boys, often conducted in childhood. [2] In modern contexts, circumcision has gained some traction through medical campaigns promoting hygiene, disease prevention—such as HIV—and parental preferences, though uptake remains minimal due to cultural unfamiliarity and perceptions of it as non-essential. [4] [2] Studies indicate high theoretical acceptability among Chinese parents and adults for infant or voluntary circumcision, yet actual rates stay low, influenced by factors like education, urban migration, and targeted interventions in regions with higher HIV risk. [1] [5] Among non-Muslim populations, procedures like the Shang Ring device have been developed and evaluated for safety and efficacy in adult male circumcision. [6]

## History

### Ancient and Imperial Periods

Circumcision was not a routine or culturally embedded practice among the Han Chinese during ancient and imperial periods, reflecting its absence from Confucian societal norms and traditional rituals. Unlike in some neighboring regions, no widespread evidence exists for its adoption in Han-dominated medical or ceremonial contexts, distinguishing China from areas with established circumcision traditions.

The procedure found limited introduction through Central Asian interactions along the Silk Road, particularly with the arrival of Islam during the Tang dynasty, where it became associated with emerging Muslim communities that would later form groups like the Hui. These minorities maintained the practice as a religious rite, though it never permeated Han society.

In imperial policies, such as under the Yuan dynasty, edicts explicitly prohibited circumcision among Muslims and Jews, underscoring its confined role to non-Han religious groups rather than any normative or punitive application across the empire. This restriction highlights the practice's marginal status, even as ethnic and trade contacts persisted, without integration into military or courtly institutions beyond eunuch castration, which differed fundamentally in scope and purpose. [7]

### Republican and Early Communist Era

Circumcision remained uncommon among the Han Chinese population during the Republican era (1912–1949) and early People's Republic, primarily conducted as a therapeutic intervention for conditions like phimosis or redundant prepuce rather than routine hygiene or cultural rite. Western medical influences, including those from missionaries and modern public health reformers, introduced surgical options for genital hygiene, yet adoption stayed limited due to entrenched traditional views prioritizing bodily integrity.

In the Maoist period, state-driven hygiene drives and rural health initiatives, such as barefoot doctor programs, focused on basic sanitation and preventive care but did not institutionalize circumcision, with procedures confined to cases of clinical necessity in urban hospitals or collective clinics. Overall rates persisted at low levels, underscoring the procedure's marginal role amid broader socialist health reforms.

## Cultural and Religious Dimensions

### Traditional Chinese Perspectives

In traditional Han Chinese society, Confucian philosophy underscored the importance of bodily integrity and filial piety, regarding non-essential modifications to the body—such as circumcision—as disruptions to natural harmony and the filial duty to preserve one's inherited form. [8] This perspective aligned with broader East Asian cultural norms that historically rendered body alterations rare, prioritizing the unaltered human form as integral to moral and social order. [9]

Folk attitudes in mainstream Chinese culture further reinforced the view of circumcision as superfluous or foreign, with traditional medicine perceiving the body as a holistic system where each part served a purposeful function, discouraging elective removals like foreskin excision. [10] Unlike rites of passage in certain Abrahamic traditions, circumcision held no ritual significance in Han family or gender dynamics, where intactness symbolized unadorned maturity rather than a transformative ceremony. [1]

### Minority Religious Practices

Among China's Muslim ethnic minorities, such as the Hui and Uyghur, male circumcision, known as khitan or sunnät, serves as a key religious rite marking entry into Muslim identity and often performed around puberty. [11] [12] In Uyghur communities of Xinjiang, it functions as a communal sunnät toy ceremony intensifying Islamic adherence, while Hui practices in regions like Ningxia integrate similar traditions through mosques or imams, though recent restrictions have impacted rituals. [11]

These practices trace roots to Muslim influx during the Yuan dynasty under Mongol rule, when Islamic communities established distinct customs separate from Han Chinese norms, despite periodic imperial prohibitions. [13]

Jewish circumcision occurred historically among small communities like the Kaifeng Jews, who maintained the ritual as part of their observances, and later among limited expatriate groups in treaty ports such as Shanghai. [14] Instances among Christians remain negligible, confined to isolated converts or foreign influences without broader adoption.

## Prevalence and Demographics

### Current Incidence Rates

The prevalence of male circumcision in China is low, with estimates indicating rates below 5% among males, particularly Han Chinese. [15] These figures reflect the practice's limited cultural entrenchment in the majority population, where it is not routinely performed absent medical indications. [16]

Incidence is modestly higher in urban settings, driven by greater exposure to medical recommendations emphasizing hygiene and preventive health benefits. [5]

Surveys from the 1990s onward document a slight upward trend in these rates, coinciding with broader dissemination of international health information. [5]

### Regional and Ethnic Variations

Circumcision rates vary significantly by ethnic group in China, with Muslim minorities exhibiting much higher prevalence than the Han majority. Among Uyghurs in Xinjiang, the practice is a longstanding Islamic tradition, though recent restrictions have impacted its observance. [17] Similarly, Hui communities adhere to circumcision as a religious custom, often regardless of their geographic dispersion across central and other regions. [18] In contrast, rates remain negligible among Han Chinese populations, contributing to the overall low national baseline excluding these minorities. [2]

Regionally, northwestern provinces like Xinjiang show elevated circumcision linked to Uyghur demographics, while eastern Han-dominated areas report minimal incidence. Urban-rural divides further influence patterns, with larger cities experiencing a gradual increase in parental acceptance of the procedure. [5] Hui groups in central China sustain these traditions independently of local majorities.

## Medical Practices

### Surgical Techniques

In Chinese medical practice, the Shang Ring device is a commonly employed technique for adult male circumcision, involving the placement of an inner ring at the coronal sulcus followed by an outer ring to clamp the foreskin, which is then excised after a set period, typically under local anesthesia in outpatient settings. [19] [20] This method, developed in China, standardizes the procedure with minimal operative time and reduced bleeding. [21]

For pediatric cases, techniques such as dorsal slit or clamp methods are utilized to address phimosis or routine circumcision, freeing adhesions and excising the foreskin while preserving the glans. [22] In instances of adult phimosis with scarring, a modified dorsal slit-guillotine-sleeve approach may be applied, combining incision, circumferential excision, and sleeve resection for precise removal. [23] These approaches adapt to local contexts by prioritizing efficiency and accessibility in hospital environments.

### Indications and Complications

In China, circumcision is primarily indicated for therapeutic purposes, such as pathological phimosis, recurrent balanitis, or paraphimosis, rather than as routine prophylaxis or preventive hygiene. [24] [25] These conditions necessitate surgical intervention to alleviate symptoms like inflammation, urinary obstruction, or tissue retraction issues, with clinical guidelines emphasizing conservative treatments first where feasible. [26]

Complication rates from Chinese clinical studies vary but generally range from 6% to 12%, encompassing short-term issues like bleeding, infection, and wound adhesions, alongside rarer long-term effects such as meatal stenosis. [25] [27] These rates are typically lower in specialized urological centers employing standardized local anesthesia protocols, reflecting improved outcomes through experienced practitioners and controlled settings. [28]

Post-operative care protocols in Chinese medical practice focus on wound monitoring and hygiene, with patients advised to attend follow-up visits at 2-4 weeks to assess healing and address any residual foreskin or adhesions. [19] Adherence to these measures, including avoidance of strenuous activity, contributes to high satisfaction rates exceeding 97% in reported cohorts. [27]

## Policy and Regulation

### Government Health Guidelines

The National Health Commission of China does not incorporate routine male circumcision into public health policies, treating it as a non-essential procedure absent medical necessity, with procedures generally requiring out-of-pocket payment by families. Guidelines addressing circumcision are embedded within broader frameworks for urological and male genitourinary conditions, emphasizing therapeutic indications over routine or preventive applications. Authorities have refrained from endorsing circumcision for HIV prevention, citing insufficient readiness for policy adoption despite global evidence. [12] This stance aligns with limited public health campaigns, where non-medical circumcision is often portrayed as unnecessary, reflecting cultural and resource priorities.

### Insurance Coverage Status

In China, circumcision is generally classified as a non-essential procedure and is not covered under basic medical insurance schemes unless it addresses a specific pathological condition, such as phimosis or recurrent balanitis requiring surgical intervention. [29] Routine or preventive circumcisions, often sought for hygienic or cosmetic reasons, require full out-of-pocket payment by patients, as they do not qualify as treatments for covered diseases under national reimbursement policies. [30] [31]

Exceptions may apply in cases of documented medical necessity, where partial reimbursement could be available if the procedure is performed during hospitalization or meets criteria for essential urological treatment, aligning with the scope of urban employee or resident basic medical insurance programs. [32] However, even then, coverage is limited, and ancillary costs like examinations or medications might use insurance cards without full procedural reimbursement. [30] This status reflects broader guidelines prioritizing reimbursements for disease-related interventions over elective surgeries.
