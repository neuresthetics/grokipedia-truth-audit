# Female genital mutilation in India

Female genital mutilation in India, referred to as khatna, khafd, or khafz, involves the ritual removal of the clitoral prepuce from prepubescent girls, predominantly within the Dawoodi Bohra community, a Shia Ismaili Muslim denomination with an estimated 1 million adherents worldwide, the majority residing in India. [1] [2] The procedure, typically performed at age 6 or 7 by traditional cutters in approximately 75% of instances, is classified by the World Health Organization as Type I female genital mutilation, entailing partial or total removal of the clitoral hood without altering other genitalia. [2] Empirical surveys indicate a prevalence of 75% to 85% among Dawoodi Bohra women aged 18 to 85, though national data remains limited due to the practice's confinement to this and possibly smaller sects like Sulemani Bohras. [2] While proponents within the community frame it as a religious rite paralleling male circumcision, promoting purity and discipline, critics highlight associated health risks including immediate pain, bleeding, infection, and potential long-term complications such as scarring and sexual dysfunction, notwithstanding claims of minimal harm in its localized form. [3] [4] Legally, no dedicated statute prohibits it, but a 2017 public interest litigation before the Supreme Court argues its unconstitutionality under Articles 14, 15, and 21 for infringing bodily integrity and gender equality; the case was referred to a constitution bench in 2018 and remains unresolved as of 2024. [5] This tension underscores broader debates on balancing religious freedom with individual rights, fueled by activist campaigns against the practice amid community resistance. [6]

## Overview

### Definition and WHO Classification

Female genital mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. [7] This definition, established by the World Health Organization (WHO), excludes medically necessary procedures and emphasizes the absence of therapeutic intent, distinguishing FGM from interventions justified by health benefits. [8]

The WHO classifies FGM into four types based on the extent of cutting: Type I involves partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or its prepuce (clitoridectomy); Type II entails partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora (excision); Type III includes narrowing of the vaginal opening through apposition of the labia, with or without clitoral excision (infibulation); and Type IV covers all other harmful procedures, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, or cauterization. [8] In Indian contexts, particularly among the Dawoodi Bohra Muslim community where the practice known as khatna or sunna is reported, the procedure aligns with Type I, specifically the removal of the clitoral prepuce (Type Ia), involving excision of the fold of skin covering the clitoris without anesthesia or sterile conditions. This classification holds despite community descriptions framing it as a minor ritual cut, as the procedure meets WHO criteria for non-therapeutic genital alteration. [9]

Unlike male circumcision, which epidemiological studies link to reduced risks of urinary tract infections, penile cancer, and heterosexual HIV transmission in high-prevalence settings, FGM lacks any verifiable medical justification and consistently correlates with adverse outcomes, including immediate risks of hemorrhage, infection, and shock, as well as long-term issues like chronic pain, urinary problems, sexual dysfunction, and increased maternal mortality from childbirth complications. [4] [10] These effects stem from the targeted injury to highly innervated sexual anatomy without offsetting benefits, underscoring a fundamental disparity in rationale and consequences. [11]

UNICEF estimates indicate over 80 million girls and women affected by FGM in Asia as of 2024, with India's prevalence undocumented in national data due to its concentration in insular sects like the Dawoodi Bohras, where surveys report up to 80% participation among community women. [12] [9] This underreporting highlights challenges in applying global standards to localized practices, yet the WHO typology remains applicable for empirical assessment and cross-cultural comparison. [13]

### Terminology in Indian Contexts

In Indian contexts, particularly within the Dawoodi Bohra community—a Shia Muslim sect with approximately one million members in India—the practice is commonly termed khatna, khafz, or khafd, referring to a ritual procedure involving the removal or nicking of the clitoral prepuce for purposes of religious purity and tradition, rather than intentional harm or disfigurement. [14] [15] These terms, derived from Arabic and Gujarati linguistic influences, frame the act as a sunna (customary religious rite) analogous to male circumcision, emphasizing its role in curbing perceived promiscuity and ensuring moral hygiene without connotations of mutilation. [6] Community adherents often describe it as a "tiny cut" or symbolic prick performed in secrecy by female elders using rudimentary tools, distinguishing it semantically and procedurally from broader Western or international classifications. [15]

Bohra representatives and supporters consistently reject equivalence to severe forms of female genital mutilation, such as African infibulation (WHO Type III), portraying khatna instead as a benign, non-deforming ritual confined to the prepuce without removal of the clitoris or labia, and arguing that anti-FGM campaigns conflate it with more harmful variants to impose external moral judgments. [16] [17] For instance, Bohra advocacy groups have defended the "right to practice khafz (female circumcision)" on religious freedom grounds, highlighting its limited scope as a "minor modification" rather than a procedure causing lasting genital alteration. [16] This self-framing contrasts with activist terminology like "FGM/C" (female genital mutilation/cutting), which some organizations adopt to align the practice with global bans and WHO classifications, despite empirical surveys indicating that 75-85% prevalence among Bohra women involves primarily Type I procedures without widespread infibulation or extensive excision. [2] [18] [9]

The terminological divergence underscores perceptual gaps: while international bodies and Indian activists invoke "mutilation" to evoke harm and advocate prohibition, community narratives prioritize cultural continuity, with khatna invoked in intra-community discourse as an innocuous rite of passage, not a violation warranting the pejorative "FGM" label that homogenizes disparate practices. [19] This framing persists despite opposition, as evidenced by Bohra women's testimonies in surveys describing the procedure as a "small prick" for spiritual eligibility, rejecting mutilation rhetoric as culturally alien. [9] [18]

## Historical Context

### Origins and Introduction to India

Female genital mutilation in India is predominantly confined to the Dawoodi Bohra community, a sect of Ismaili Shia Muslims whose religious and cultural practices trace back to Yemen. [20] Historical records indicate that the Dawoodi Bohras established a significant presence in Gujarat, India, during the 16th century, following migrations from Yemen where the community's leadership had been based. This period marks the shift of the Bohra da'is (spiritual leaders) from Yemen to Indian soil, with the 24th dai relocating before his death in 1567 CE, solidifying the sect's Indian foothold. The practice of khatna, a form of Type I female genital cutting involving the removal of the clitoral prepuce, is linked to these esoteric Ismaili traditions imported by the migrants, rather than indigenous Indian customs. [9]

No empirical evidence supports the existence of female genital mutilation in India prior to Islamic influences, and it lacks association with mainstream Hinduism, ancient Vedic rituals, or Sunni Muslim communities on the subcontinent. [21] Community-specific accounts attribute khatna to the Bohras' religious framework, emphasizing purity and esoteric symbolism derived from Fatimid Ismaili heritage, which originated in North Africa and Yemen before the 16th-century migrations. [21] Unlike African contexts where the practice often predates Islam and stems from diverse tribal etiologies, India's variant remains strictly sect-bound, with no records of colonial-era expansion or adaptation beyond Bohra enclaves. [14]

Early external documentation of the practice emerges from mid-20th-century anthropological observations within Bohra settlements, highlighting its continuity as a clandestine rite tied to Yemenite-Ismaili roots rather than broader Indian societal norms. [22] This causal trajectory underscores a migration-driven introduction, preserved through the community's insularity and religious authority, without verifiable ties to speculative ancient subcontinental precedents. [23]

### Pre-Independence and Post-Independence Evolution

Prior to Indian independence in 1947, female genital cutting, known as khatna or khafd among the Dawoodi Bohra community, had been practiced for centuries following the community's migration to the Indian subcontinent around 1067 AD from Yemen, with roots tracing to Fatimid-era Egypt and North Africa in the 9th–11th centuries. [24] This insular Shia Ismaili sect, primarily merchants in princely states and British-administered areas, maintained the ritual as an internal cultural and purportedly religious custom without facing colonial prohibitions, in contrast to practices like sati that were regulated under British law from 1829 onward. The absence of bans allowed continuity within tight-knit Bohra enclaves, where the practice was conducted privately by community elders, reinforced by the authority of the Dai al-Mutlaq, the spiritual leader whose edicts, drawn from texts like Daim al-Islam, framed it as essential for purity and modesty. [25]

Following independence and the adoption of a secular constitution in 1950, khatna persisted unabated, with the Bohra leadership actively resisting state encroachments on communal autonomy; for instance, the 51st Dai al-Mutlaq, Syedna Taher Saifuddin, challenged the Bombay Prevention of Excommunication Act of 1949 in court, a move upheld by the Supreme Court in 1962, preserving the community's ability to enforce participation through social sanctions like boycotts. [25] Throughout the 1970s and 1990s, as broader women's rights discourse gained traction in India amid global feminist movements, the practice retreated further into secrecy, often performed in homes or by trusted practitioners to evade scrutiny, yet surveys indicate over 70% prevalence among Bohra women by the early 1990s without significant interruption. [24] This resilience stemmed primarily from internal religious imperatives rather than external legal pressures, with successive Dai al-Mutlaq endorsing the ritual—Daim al-Islam citing benefits like hygiene and urge control post-age seven—outweighing nascent secular influences until diaspora activism intensified scrutiny in the 2010s. [25] [24]

## Prevalence and Demographics

### Primary Communities Practicing FGM

The practice of female genital mutilation (FGM), referred to as khatna within the community, is predominantly confined to the Dawoodi Bohra sect, a denomination of Shia Ismaili Muslims. This community, known for its tight-knit structure, endogamous marriages, and urban mercantile traditions, numbers approximately 1 to 1.2 million members in India, forming a small fraction—about 0.5%—of the country's overall Muslim population. Empirical surveys indicate high prevalence within this group, with around 80% of Dawoodi Bohra women reporting having undergone the procedure during childhood, typically between ages 6 and 9, as a rite of passage performed on girls from middle-class, educated families in urban settings. [9] [26] [14]

While the Dawoodi Bohras represent the epicenter of FGM in India, isolated and unverified reports suggest limited occurrences among other Muslim subgroups, such as certain Sunni communities in regions like Kerala, where anecdotal accounts describe similar rituals termed khatna or sunat. However, these claims lack systematic data or large-scale surveys, and no peer-reviewed studies confirm widespread adoption outside the Bohra sect; prevalence elsewhere remains negligible or undocumented, underscoring significant data gaps in non-Bohra contexts. [27] [2]

No credible evidence links FGM to non-Muslim communities, including Hindu castes or tribal groups, despite occasional unsubstantiated assertions in advocacy literature; the practice's persistence is tied exclusively to specific Islamic interpretive traditions within insular sects like the Dawoodi Bohras, without extension to broader Indian Muslim demographics or other religious groups. This concentration counters generalizations portraying FGM as a pan-Indian Muslim phenomenon, as surveys and reports consistently highlight the Bohras' distinct cultural and religious insularity as the primary vector. [14] [22]

### Estimated Scale and Geographic Spread

The practice of female genital mutilation (FGM), known locally as khatna or khafd among the Dawoodi Bohra community, lacks comprehensive national-level data in India due to its secretive nature, absence of census inclusion, and reliance on community-specific surveys that face methodological limitations such as self-selection bias in anonymous online responses. [2] [16] No official government estimates exist, and broader Asian figures from UNICEF—encompassing over 80 million affected girls and women across the region—do not disaggregate India-specific prevalence, which appears confined to select ethnic groups rather than widespread. [12] Empirical assessments, primarily from Bohra-focused studies, indicate high intra-community rates but highlight underreporting challenges, as participants may understate or overstate experiences due to cultural stigma or advocacy influences from organizations conducting the research. [28] [18]

Within the Dawoodi Bohra community—the primary group documented as practicing FGM—prevalence estimates range from 75% to 85% among girls and women, based on a 2017 global survey by Sahiyo involving over 400 respondents, with 80% reporting having undergone the procedure typically between ages 6 and 9. [2] [9] [18] With the Bohra population in India numbering approximately 800,000 to 1 million (concentrated in urban areas), this suggests thousands of cases annually, though exact figures remain speculative absent birth cohort tracking or mandatory reporting. [16] National extrapolations, such as 10,000 to 100,000 procedures yearly, derive from unverified activist claims rather than rigorous demographic modeling, underscoring the need for caution against inflated scales without verifiable baselines.

Geographically, the practice is most prevalent in western India, particularly Maharashtra (including Mumbai, home to the largest Bohra concentrations) and Gujarat, where community networks facilitate its transmission through family rituals. [16] [6] Smaller incidences occur in adjacent states like Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh among Bohra populations, but documentation is sparse outside urban hubs. [2] Migration patterns extend the practice beyond India: Bohra diaspora communities in Pakistan, the United States, and the United Kingdom continue it, with reports of cross-border procedures as recent as 2024, complicating containment efforts and highlighting export via familial relocation rather than indigenous spread. [9] [29] Verification remains hindered by the absence of clinical registries or population-based sampling, with studies often limited to advocacy-driven efforts that may not capture non-Bohra instances, if any exist at scale. ## Description of the Practice

### Types Performed and Procedural Details

In the Dawoodi Bohra community, where female genital mutilation is most prevalent in India, the procedure known as khatna or khafz aligns with World Health Organization Type I classification, entailing partial or total removal of the clitoral prepuce (hood). [9] This involves a superficial incision or excision limited to the external genital area, without extension to the labia minora or majora, distinguishing it from more invasive Type II (excision) or Type III (infibulation) forms documented in regions like sub-Saharan Africa, which often include stitching to narrow the vaginal orifice. [7] Accounts vary slightly, with community descriptions emphasizing prepuce removal only, while survivor testimonies and medical examinations report occasional inclusion of the clitoral tip or minor adjacent tissue. [22] [30]

The procedure employs rudimentary tools such as a razor blade, wielded by untrained lay practitioners—often elderly women designated as traditional cutters—in non-sterile home settings. [14] [22] Anesthesia is absent in most cases, rendering the 1-2 minute intervention acutely painful, with post-procedure application of substances like black powder to staunch bleeding rather than medical dressings. [14] [22] Girls undergo it without informed consent, typically restrained or deceived into compliance. [14]

A minority trend toward medicalization has emerged, with some procedures shifting to clinics where physicians use sterilized scalpels and local anesthetics to mitigate infection risks, though surveys indicate over 70% persist via traditional methods. [22] Legal petitions and forensic reviews, including anatomical imaging from affected individuals, corroborate these mechanics as confined excisions yielding minimal scarring, absent the extensive tissue loss seen in global Type II/III variants. [30]

### Age, Rituals, and Performers

The khatna procedure in the Dawoodi Bohra community is typically performed on girls between the ages of 6 and 8, prior to puberty, to instill early control and adherence to community norms. [31] [32] [14] Personal accounts from community members confirm this timing, with procedures often scheduled during family gatherings or vacations to maintain discretion. [31]

Historically, the cutting has been carried out by older women within the community, referred to as khatne-wali, who acquire the skill through generational transmission without formal training. [32] In more recent practices, particularly in urban or diaspora settings, female physicians from the Bohra community have been engaged to perform the procedure, motivated by perceptions of greater hygiene and reduced risk compared to traditional methods. [31] [14] These performers operate in private home settings, ensuring the event remains concealed from outsiders.

Rituals surrounding the procedure emphasize secrecy and familial involvement, with the girl often accompanied only by her mother for reassurance during the brief, anesthesia-free process. [32] Unlike male circumcision, which may involve communal acknowledgment, khatna lacks public ceremonies or feasts, reinforcing its clandestine nature to avoid external scrutiny or internal dissent. [31]

Enforcement relies on peer and familial pressure framed as religious duty, as decreed by the community's spiritual leader, the Syedna, with non-participation risking severe social consequences such as ostracism, exclusion from communal events, marriage prohibitions, or even denial of burial rites. [31] [32] [14] Community accounts highlight this coercion, where opting out invites isolation, underscoring the practice's role in maintaining intra-group conformity despite growing internal debates. [31]

## Health Consequences

### Immediate Physical Risks

Immediate physical risks of khatna, the form of female genital cutting practiced in the Dawoodi Bohra community, include severe pain due to the absence of anesthesia during the procedure, which involves excision of the clitoral hood using a non-sterile razor by untrained community women on girls typically aged 6-8. [16] [32] Excessive bleeding can occur, as evidenced by a 2017 case in India where a Bohra girl bled heavily post-procedure, soaking three bedsheets and necessitating hospital intervention. [16]

Infections arise from unsterile tools and home settings, with vulnerability heightened by the immature anatomy of prepubescent girls, potentially leading to urinary retention or shock from pain and blood loss, though mortality remains low. [7] A 2018 survivor-led survey of 83 Bohra women across Indian states found 50% experienced post-procedure irritation and 30% reported immediate discomfort during urination or walking, indicating complication rates of 10-50% in small samples, contrasting claims by community advocates of a "minor nick" with negligible harms. [16] [32] These risks stem causally from procedural factors like lack of medical oversight, rather than inherent severity, as khatna aligns with WHO Type Ib cutting. [7]

### Long-Term Medical and Psychological Effects

Long-term medical complications from khatna, the form of female genital cutting practiced primarily in India's Dawoodi Bohra community and classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as Type 1 (partial or total removal of the clitoral prepuce), include scarring, keloid formation, and chronic infections due to tissue damage and impaired healing. [7] These effects arise from the procedure's disruption of sensitive nerve endings and skin integrity, potentially leading to neuromas—abnormal nerve growths causing localized pain or hypersensitivity—though India-specific longitudinal data remains limited to small cohort studies rather than randomized controlled trials (RCTs). [33] Dyspareunia, or painful intercourse, is reported in up to 30-50% of affected women in global Type 1 cases, attributed to scar contracture and reduced lubrication, with analogous findings in Bohra surveys where participants described ongoing discomfort during sexual activity. [34] [35]

Chronic urinary and menstrual issues, such as dysuria or dysmenorrhea, may persist from adhesions or altered anatomy, exacerbating infection risks like recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), as documented in WHO classifications for Type 1 procedures where even minimal excision alters vulvar function without medical benefit. [7] However, the absence of large-scale Indian epidemiological studies—due to underreporting and cultural secrecy—limits causal attribution, with global analogies from African Type 1 contexts showing higher complication rates than in less invasive variants, though Bohra khatna's ritualistic nature (often performed by non-medical practitioners) amplifies variability. [36]

Psychological effects encompass trauma responses, with survivor accounts from a 2016-2017 Sahiyo survey of over 400 Bohra women in India revealing elevated reports of anxiety, shame, and sexual dissatisfaction linked to the procedure's violation of bodily autonomy, though self-selection bias in activist-led sampling tempers generalizability. [37] Peer-reviewed analyses indicate potential for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and low self-esteem, stemming from the procedure's early age (typically 6-7 years) and secrecy, which fosters dissociation and long-term emotional suppression. [38] [39] Countervailing community narratives, often from Bohra leaders, assert minimal psychological harm due to normalized cultural framing, yet these lack empirical validation against survivor testimonies and overlook causality debates where stigma may confound self-reported distress rather than the cutting itself. [35] No comprehensive Indian cohorts exist to quantify prevalence, highlighting reliance on global meta-analyses that associate Type 1 FGM with 1.5-2 times higher odds of mood disorders, adjusted for confounders like socioeconomic status. [40]

## Cultural and Religious Dimensions

### Significance in Dawoodi Bohra Beliefs

In Dawoodi Bohra Ismaili doctrine, female genital cutting—termed khafd or khatna—constitutes an esoteric rite for achieving spiritual purity and chastity, with roots in Fatimid-era traditions as codified in the 10th-century jurisprudential text Da‘a’im al-Islam by Al-Qadi al-Nu’man, which emphasizes genital cutting for both sexes to foster moral discipline. [41] The practice holds sunnah status, reinforced by fatwas from the Dai al-Mutlaq, including Syedna Mufaddal Saifuddin's directive on June 6, 2016, mandating it as a religious imperative for purification and alignment with prophetic traditions. [42] [43]

Community interpretations parallel khafd with male circumcision (khatna), viewing both as complementary rites of passage performed around age seven that instill self-control and sectarian loyalty, thereby sustaining high adherence rates—evidenced by 56% of surveyed Bohra women attributing the practice to doctrinal requirements—which bolsters intra-community cohesion and obedience to the Dai's interpretive authority. [44] [45]

Doctrinally, the female variant underscores modesty by mitigating desires, enabling Bohra women to access sacred sites like mosques, cemeteries, and the Kaaba—privileges tied to demonstrated purity—while distinguishing the sect's esoteric causality from broader Islamic norms. [44] This gendered emphasis integrates with Ismaili esotericism, where ritual adherence signals deeper metaphysical alignment and family honor (izzat). [41]

### Justifications Including Purity and Social Control

Practitioners within the Dawoodi Bohra community justify khatna, or the ritual nicking of the clitoral hood, as a means to promote female purity and modesty by dampening excessive sexual desires and preventing promiscuity. Community members describe the procedure as a way to "keep young girls on the right path," associating the clitoris with a "sinful" appendage whose removal fosters moral superiority and chastity. [32] This belief aligns with broader rationales cited in surveys of Bohra women, where moderating or curbing sexual desire is invoked to ensure lifelong fidelity and uphold family honor.

The practice is also framed as enhancing cleanliness and hygiene, paralleling male circumcision (khatna for boys) and removing perceived impurities to prepare girls for womanhood. Bohra adherents link khafd to religious obligations derived from Ismaili traditions, viewing it as essential for spiritual purity and distinguishing their sect from other Muslims who do not perform it. Terms like khafz and khatna themselves evoke notions of cleanliness and purity, reinforcing the procedure's role in maintaining ritual sanctity. [32] [46] [41]

Socially, khatna serves as a marker of community identity and integration, increasing marriageability within the endogamous Bohra group by signaling adherence to norms of chastity and readiness for marital roles. Proponents claim it enriches prospects for stable unions and family cohesion, with some anecdotal reports from Bohra women indicating personal satisfaction and no disruption to marital harmony. Opposition to the practice is often portrayed by defenders as an external imposition, influenced by Western-educated reformists or global anti-FGC campaigns, which they see as eroding minority religious autonomy rather than addressing verifiable harms from the minimal Type 1a procedure. [6] [32] [47]

## Legal Framework

### Relevant Indian Penal Code Provisions

The Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC), addresses practices akin to female genital mutilation (FGM) through general provisions on causing hurt and grievous hurt, rather than a targeted prohibition. Sections 319 to 326 criminalize voluntarily causing hurt or grievous hurt, with punishments ranging from two years' imprisonment to life for grievous cases involving instruments or endangering life. [48] [49] Grievous hurt under Section 320 includes emasculation or permanent bodily disfigurement, potentially encompassing FGM procedures that involve cutting or removal of genital tissue, as affirmed in government statements classifying such acts as penal offences under these sections. [50] Section 509 further applies, prohibiting acts intended to insult a woman's modesty, which courts have interpreted to cover invasive non-consensual procedures on female genitalia. [51]

For minors, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (POCSO) supplements IPC provisions, treating penetrative or non-penetrative sexual assault on children under 18 as offences punishable by seven years to life imprisonment, depending on severity. Government policy relies on POCSO alongside IPC to address FGM, viewing it as a form of child sexual abuse when performed without medical necessity, though applicability hinges on proving sexual intent or assault elements, which introduces interpretive ambiguity since FGM often lacks explicit sexual gratification motives. [52] Consent is invalid for children under POCSO, rendering parental or communal authorization legally irrelevant for those below 18.

Legal gaps persist due to the absence of an explicit FGM ban, forcing reliance on broad hurt provisions that require case-specific proof of injury severity—e.g., whether clitoral hood excision qualifies as "grievous" under Section 320, which demands evidence of lasting impairment. Religious freedom claims under Article 25 of the Constitution complicate enforcement, as practitioners may argue the practice constitutes an essential religious tenet exempt from general criminal laws, though IPC overrides such defenses absent a statutory carve-out. [53]

Enforcement remains infrequent, with no centralized data on prosecutions; reported cases from the 2010s, such as isolated FIRs in Maharashtra against Bohra practitioners, highlight evidentiary hurdles in secretive, community-insulated settings where victims may not disclose due to stigma or coercion. [54] [55] These challenges underscore the limitations of general provisions, as underreporting and prosecutorial reluctance prevent consistent application, despite IPC's punitive framework. [56]

### Absence of Specific Bans and Gaps

India has not enacted a specific federal law prohibiting female genital mutilation (FGM), in contrast to over 20 countries worldwide that have introduced targeted legislation criminalizing the practice, such as the United Kingdom's Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003 and the United States' federal prohibition under 18 U.S.C. § 116 since 1996. This absence stems from judicial and legislative deference to minority religious freedoms under Article 25 of the Indian Constitution, which protects the right to practice and propagate religion subject to public order, morality, and health, allowing communities like the Dawoodi Bohras to frame FGM—known locally as khatna or khafz—as an essential religious rite despite petitions arguing its non-essentiality. Community lobbying by Bohra organizations has further influenced this restraint, emphasizing cultural preservation and religious autonomy over uniform criminalization, thereby prioritizing federalism's accommodation of diverse practices in a multi-religious polity. [29]

Enforcement gaps persist under existing general provisions of the Indian Penal Code (IPC), particularly Sections 319–326 on causing hurt or grievous hurt, which could theoretically apply to FGM but lack specificity, leading to interpretive ambiguity and minimal deterrence. No centralized monitoring or reporting mechanisms exist to track FGM incidents, exacerbating underreporting due to the practice's clandestine execution within private homes or communities, with petitions noting the absence of empirical data on prosecutions or convictions under these IPC sections for khatna. [57] The trend toward medicalization—where procedures are increasingly delegated to gynecologists or performed in private clinics—further circumvents scrutiny, as practitioners may classify the cutting as a benign "sunna" procedure akin to minor female circumcision, evading charges of intentional harm while reducing immediate visibility of traditional risks. [58]

These legislative voids highlight a reliance on ad hoc judicial clarification rather than proactive statutory reform, with advocates in petitions underscoring that general laws fail to address FGM's unique cultural entrenchment and the lack of public awareness campaigns tailored to affected sects. [5] Empirical evidence of deterrence remains scant, as no systematic studies document reductions in prevalence attributable to IPC applications alone, underscoring the need for explicit prohibitions to bridge enforcement disparities observed in jurisdictions with dedicated FGM statutes.

## Judicial Developments

### Key Petitions and Court Observations

In April 2017, advocate Sunita Tiwari filed Writ Petition (Civil) No. 286 in the Supreme Court of India, seeking a nationwide ban on female genital mutilation (FGM), referred to as khatna or khafd within the Dawoodi Bohra community. The petition contended that the practice constitutes gender discrimination by subjecting girls to non-consensual cutting of genital tissue, violating equality under Article 14 and the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 of the Constitution, while also contravening international commitments like the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. It urged enforcement of existing Indian Penal Code provisions, such as Sections 319–338 on causing hurt, until specific legislation is enacted. [59] [60]

Hearings began on July 9, 2018, before a Division Bench comprising Justices A.K. Sikri and Ashok Bhushan, focusing on the tension between fundamental rights protections and religious freedoms asserted under Articles 25 and 26. Petitioners highlighted empirical evidence of physical and psychological harm, drawing on World Health Organization classifications of FGM as a violation causing immediate risks like hemorrhage and long-term issues including infections and trauma. Community representatives defended khatna as a minimal, hygienic ritual integral to their faith, not equivalent to global FGM forms and performed only on pre-pubescent girls without severe consequences, arguing state interference would infringe on essential religious practices. The court directed impleadment of states like Gujarat and Maharashtra with significant Bohra populations and scheduled further arguments. [61] [62]

On September 24, 2018, the Bench referred the case to a five-judge Constitution Bench to resolve whether khatna qualifies as an essential religious practice protected from regulation, emphasizing the need for deeper scrutiny of constitutional balances amid stalled progress. Subsequent interim proceedings in November 2019 saw the court acknowledge documented health hazards, including pain and potential complications, while probing the practice's scriptural basis and societal impact. By October 2021, the matter remained pending before the larger bench, with no final adjudication, underscoring protracted judicial deliberation on prioritizing bodily integrity against cultural claims. [5] [63]

[64] [65] These remarks, made in the context of broader discussions on child rights, marked a significant departure from previous judicial reticence on the issue, highlighting its occurrence particularly within communities like the Dawoodi Bohras. [65]

The acknowledgment signals potential for increased institutional scrutiny, including the possibility of government-led data collection on FGM prevalence, which has historically lacked comprehensive national statistics in India. [65] However, it did not result in an immediate ruling or directive, leaving the practice without a specific ban under Indian law despite existing penal provisions that could apply. [66] This development underscores ongoing tensions between cultural practices and constitutional rights, with warnings against medicalization—where procedures are performed by health professionals—further complicating enforcement. [67]

While viewed as progress by advocacy groups like Equality Now, the remarks contrast with the Supreme Court's earlier deferrals in related petitions, emphasizing the need for empirical evidence and legislative action to address gaps in prohibition and prosecution. [65]

## Anti-FGM Activism

### Domestic Campaigns and Survivor-Led Efforts

Sahiyo, established in early 2015 by five Dawoodi Bohra women including survivors Fiza Ranalvi Jha and Mariya Taher, has led domestic efforts against khatna through survivor-driven awareness initiatives, online documentation, and advocacy centered on empirical harms and lack of consent. [68] [20] The organization conducted an initial online survey in June 2015 to quantify prevalence among Bohras, revealing self-reported experiences of cutting typically performed on girls aged 6-9 without anesthesia, often by untrained practitioners. These campaigns prioritize survivor testimonies to underscore long-term physical and psychological effects, such as pain, infections, and diminished sexual function, over religious rationales. [22]

Survivor networks like WeSpeakOut, formed in 2015 as a Bohra-specific platform, have facilitated anonymous online forums for sharing experiences and building solidarity, amassing hundreds of accounts that document procedural secrecy and intergenerational coercion. [24] In December 2015, Sahiyo-backed survivors launched the "Speak Out on FGM" petition on Change.org, collecting over 200,000 signatures by 2017 to demand governmental acknowledgment of khatna as a rights violation. [69] Efforts extended to challenging community fatwas endorsing the rite, with public critiques in 2018 highlighting inconsistencies between claims of minimal harm and documented complications like scarring. [70]

The Voices to End FGM/C initiative, started in 2018 with partner StoryCenter, has empowered over 57 survivors from 16 countries—including India—to create and screen digital stories focusing on autonomy and intergenerational dialogue. [71] These platforms have spurred internal community fractures, evidenced by reform petitions from Bohra members in 2016-2018 urging leaders to reconsider mandates amid rising awareness of health risks. [72] [6] By fostering male allyship through groups like Bhaiyo, campaigns have amplified calls for voluntary cessation, though resistance persists due to entrenched social controls. [73]

### Collaborations with NGOs

Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) such as WeSpeakOut, founded by Dawoodi Bohra survivors, have partnered with international groups like Equality Now and the Asia Network to End FGM/C to deliver training programs for community educators and legal aid for affected individuals in India. [74] [75] [76] These efforts include workshops on recognizing khatna as a form of female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) and supporting petitions to enforce existing laws, with a focus in the 2020s on countering the rise in medicalized procedures performed by health professionals. [58]

Collaborations have yielded reports documenting prevalence and advocacy gaps, such as the 2024 Asia Network short report on FGM/C in India, which highlights training initiatives to boost reporting through community outreach. [76] Effectiveness metrics remain sparse, with campaigns correlating to heightened awareness—evidenced by increased media coverage and judicial references post-2020—but no verified data on reduced incidence or widespread bans, as the practice persists without targeted legislation. [65]

Dawoodi Bohra community defenders have critiqued these NGO partnerships as instances of external interference, arguing that they impose outsider cultural norms on a religious rite distinct from severe African FGM variants and disrupt internal community harmony. [77] [78] Such accusations frame activism as biased advocacy that overlooks practitioner claims of minimal harm and familial consent, though empirical health studies contradict assertions of benignity. [14]

## Advocacy for the Practice

### Community Defenses and Religious Freedom Claims

Communities practicing female genital mutilation, or khatna, in India have defended the rite as an essential religious practice protected under Article 25 of the Constitution, which guarantees freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practice, and propagate religion. [5] In interventions before the Supreme Court in the Sunita Tiwari v. Union of India case (WP (C) No. 286/2017, filed in 2017 and referred to a Constitution Bench in September 2018), representatives argued that khatna constitutes a core tenet of their faith, equivalent to male circumcision, and thus falls within the scope of constitutionally permissible religious observances rather than arbitrary customs subject to state prohibition. [5] They contended that any legislative or judicial ban would infringe on communal autonomy in managing internal religious affairs, emphasizing that the practice predates modern scrutiny and aligns with scriptural sunnah traditions. [22]

Proponents have asserted that the procedure involves minimal intervention, described as a "minuscule nick at the prepuce" above the clitoris, causing no long-term harm such as sexual dysfunction, and comparable in scope to male circumcision. [22] Community advocates, including legal counsel in the 2018 hearings, distinguished khatna from more invasive forms of genital cutting outlined by the World Health Organization, positioning it as a safe, non-mutilatory rite performed discreetly by trained practitioners. [5] In response to petitions seeking bans, such as the 2016 online campaign against the practice, defenders highlighted the absence of widespread formal complaints from within the community, attributing this to the rite's normalization and lack of public debate rather than coercion. [79]

To shield the practice from external interference, communities have maintained secrecy around its execution, often limiting knowledge to female relatives and conducting it in private settings like clinics or homes without male involvement, a strategy framed as preserving religious sanctity amid activism. [79] This discretion extends to diaspora populations in countries like Australia, the UK, and the US, where the rite persists despite local legal pressures, with some trusts issuing certificates to verify compliance for religious ceremonies such as weddings. [80] Advocates have petitioned for continued autonomy, arguing that external campaigns misrepresent the practice and overlook internal consensus, as evidenced by low reported dissatisfaction in India compared to heightened scrutiny abroad. [80]

### Groups Like Dawoodi Bohra Women for Religious Freedom

Dawoodi Bohra Women for Religious Freedom (DBWRF), established in May 2017 by women from India's Dawoodi Bohra community, campaigns to safeguard khafz—a ritual entailing the excision of the clitoral prepuce—as an integral religious and cultural observance tied to spiritual purification and hygiene. [81] The group distinguishes khafz from female genital mutilation, which it denounces, portraying the former as a non-harmful procedure comparable to male circumcision that confers community cohesion and personal fulfillment without impairing function or health. [82]

DBWRF maintains that the rite embodies women's autonomy, with community members attesting in adulthood to its voluntary affirmation as a tradition fostering social bonds, mental resilience, and religious identity, rather than coercion or detriment. [81] Opposing legislative prohibitions, the organization asserts that such measures violate India's constitutional guarantees of religious practice (Articles 25 and 26), cultural minority rights (Article 29), and gender equality (Article 14), as male circumcision remains unregulated despite analogous bodily alteration. [82]

In July 2018, DBWRF intervened as a party in the Supreme Court of India during hearings on Sunita Tiwari v. Union of India, representing thousands of Bohra women through an accepted impleadment application that underscored khafz's divergence from mutilative practices and its affirmative role in communal well-being. [82]

## International and Comparative Views

### UN Positions and Global Framing

The United Nations, through agencies like the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF, classifies female genital mutilation (FGM) as a violation of human rights, encompassing procedures that involve partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury for non-medical reasons. [7] [12] This stance frames FGM universally as harmful, with WHO emphasizing risks including severe pain, hemorrhage, infection, and long-term complications such as urinary issues and childbirth difficulties. [7] UNICEF data from 2024 estimates over 230 million girls and women alive today have undergone FGM, marking a 15% rise from prior figures, with Asia accounting for more than 80 million cases. [13] [12]

In response, UN entities advocate for global elimination by 2030 under Sustainable Development Goal 5.3, including calls for legislative bans and rejection of medicalization, particularly in Asia where practices persist among communities like India's Dawoodi Bohras. [67] A 2012 UN General Assembly resolution condemned FGM and urged member states to enact prohibitions, though enforcement relies on national sovereignty rather than binding treaties specific to FGM. [83] This universalist approach prioritizes individual rights over cultural relativism, critiqued by some as overlooking variations in procedure severity—such as Type I cuttings in Bohra contexts, which involve clitoral hood removal without broader excision—and potential community benefits claimed in religious or purity rituals. [7]

India, while party to broader instruments like the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (ratified 1993), has not adopted FGM-specific international protocols, such as regional bans like the African Maputo Protocol, reflecting sovereignty-based resistance to externally imposed norms on customary practices. UN inquiries into Bohra practices, including references in periodic reviews, have highlighted concerns but yielded no direct enforcement, as India's federal structure defers such issues to domestic judicial processes amid debates over cultural autonomy versus universal protections. [29] This tension underscores a causal divide: UN absolutism assumes uniform harm across contexts, yet empirical gaps in Asia-specific prevalence data—reliant on self-reported surveys—limit verification, with India's non-endorsement of targeted conventions preserving space for community defenses rooted in religious freedom. [13]

### Trends in Asia Including Medicalization

In South and Southeast Asia, female genital mutilation (FGM) affects an estimated 80 million girls and women, accounting for 35% of the global prevalence as of 2024 data updated in regional snapshots. [84] The practice, often termed khitan perempuan in Indonesia and sunat perempuan in Malaysia, has seen a marked increase in medicalization, with healthcare professionals performing procedures in clinical settings to mitigate risks perceived by communities, such as infection from traditional practitioners. [85] [58] This shift parallels emerging patterns in India, where certain communities have moved toward doctor-led interventions framed as safer alternatives, contributing to broader regional causal trends driven by cultural persistence and efforts to evade legal scrutiny. [86] [87]

A October 2025 policy brief by Equality Now, Orchid Project, and partners highlights that medicalization rates are accelerating across these countries, with surveys indicating up to 50% of procedures in urban Indonesian settings now involving nurses or physicians, compared to under 20% a decade prior. [58] In Malaysia, government-endorsed "Type I" minimal cuttings are routinely medicalized, normalizing the practice under health auspices despite WHO classifications of all forms as harmful. [85] These developments reflect a causal response to anti-FGM campaigns, where communities adapt by professionalizing rituals to claim hygiene and reduced severity, yet data from survivor testimonies and health audits reveal no diminution in outcomes like urinary issues, scarring, and psychological trauma. [86]

On October 8, 2025, the World Health Organization, alongside FIGO, UNFPA, and regional bodies, issued the "Do No Harm" joint statement explicitly condemning medicalization in the Asia-Pacific, asserting that no form of FGM is safe or ethical when performed by health workers, as it violates medical oaths and perpetuates harm without evidence of benefit. [88] [67] This position underscores empirical realities: medicalized FGM evades traditional detection methods like community monitoring, fosters perceived legitimacy that hinders advocacy, and sustains complications including hemorrhage and dyspareunia, as documented in longitudinal studies across affected Asian cohorts. [89] [90] Despite these trends, gaps in standardized prevalence surveys—reliant on self-reported data from biased community sources—persist, complicating causal attribution but affirming that medical involvement does not alter the procedure's inherent risks. [58]

## Recent Developments

### Rise in Medicalization Post-2023

Following increased scrutiny and legal challenges against traditional forms of female genital cutting (known as khatna) in the Dawoodi Bohra community, reports indicate a post-2023 shift toward medicalization, with parents increasingly seeking urban doctors to perform the procedure on younger girls, often citing sterility and reduced infection risk as motivations. [58] This trend mirrors observations in Pakistani Bohra populations, where a similar preference for clinical settings has emerged, driven by perceived hygienic benefits amid post-pandemic parental anxieties over non-sterile traditional practices. [58] Qualitative interviews highlight a decline in reliance on traditional circumcisers, with procedures now occurring in private clinics in Indian urban centers like Mumbai and Surat. [91]

Activism backlash, including community resistance to external campaigns framing khatna as harmful, has contributed to this pivot, as some Bohra families view medical involvement as a culturally sensitive compromise that aligns with religious continuity while addressing safety concerns amplified by COVID-19 disruptions to informal practices. [91] Equality Now's 2025 policy brief warns that such medicalization entrenches the practice by lending it professional legitimacy, potentially normalizing it among educated urban Bohras despite lacking empirical evidence of harm reduction. [58] Drivers include fears of legal repercussions for traditional methods post-high-profile cases, alongside a broader Asian pattern where healthcare providers perform an estimated 20-30% of FGM/C cases in urban settings for purported sterility. [91]

Despite these shifts, medicalized khatna—typically involving clitoral hood removal—yields the same core harms as non-medical forms, including acute pain, bleeding, infection risks, and long-term complications like scarring, sexual dysfunction, and psychological trauma, as evidenced by global studies on Type I FGM/C. [92] The World Health Organization's April 2025 guidelines emphasize that clinician involvement does not mitigate these outcomes and may hinder elimination efforts by eroding ethical standards in medicine. [92] In India, this medical trend risks broader normalization within insular communities, complicating enforcement under existing laws like the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, absent targeted regulations banning healthcare participation. [58]

### Ongoing Debates and Empirical Data Gaps

Ongoing debates surrounding female genital mutilation (FGM), known locally as khatna among communities like the Dawoodi Bohras, center on the tension between harm minimization arguments and absolute human rights prohibitions. Proponents within affected communities assert that the procedure, often described as a superficial "nick" to the clitoral hood, yields no long-term fertility impairments or sexual dysfunction, citing anecdotal community surveys and self-reported data from participants who deny significant trauma. [93] Critics counter that even minimal cuttings inflict irreversible clitoral damage, as evidenced by MRI scans of Bohra women showing scarring and tissue alteration, and argue for universal recognition of psychological and physical harm irrespective of intent or severity. [94] These positions reflect deeper causal questions: whether purported cultural benefits, such as enhanced hygiene or rite-of-passage identity, withstand empirical scrutiny against documented risks like infection and chronic pain, without conflating tradition with necessity. [7]

A core empirical gap lies in the scarcity of unbiased, longitudinal studies tracking health outcomes from khatna in India, where most available data derive from small-scale, cross-sectional surveys prone to selection bias—either from activist-led survivor testimonies or community-insider reports that may understate adverse effects to defend the practice. [6] Prevalence estimates remain unreliable, with underreporting exacerbated by the ritual's secrecy within closed sects, leading to official denials or minimal national statistics despite anecdotal evidence of persistence among Dawoodi Bohras and other Muslim subgroups. [95] Global analyses highlight similar measurement challenges, but India-specific causal research is absent, leaving untested claims about "no harm" from pro-practice sources against WHO-classified risks without direct, prospective validation. [96] [7]

Resolving these debates demands rigorous, ideologically neutral investigations prioritizing causal inference—such as randomized or cohort designs assessing fertility, sexual function, and mental health metrics over decades—over reliance on polarized narratives from either rights advocates or cultural defenders. Peer-reviewed efforts underscore the need to disentangle procedure variations (e.g., Type IV minimal cutting) from broader FGM typologies, but funding and access barriers in insular communities hinder progress, perpetuating reliance on extrapolated African data ill-suited to South Asian contexts. [66] Until such gaps are filled with verifiable, multi-source evidence, policy responses risk favoring unproven assumptions, underscoring the imperative for transparent, data-driven inquiry to affirm or refute harm claims empirically. [97]
