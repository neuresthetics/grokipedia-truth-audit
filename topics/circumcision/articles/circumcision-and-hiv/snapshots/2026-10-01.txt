# Circumcision and HIV

Circumcision and HIV encompasses the body of empirical research investigating the protective effect of male circumcision against HIV acquisition, primarily through randomized controlled trials demonstrating that voluntary medical male circumcision reduces the risk of heterosexually transmitted HIV in men by approximately 60% in high-prevalence sub-Saharan African settings. [1] [2] Three landmark trials conducted between 2005 and 2007 in Uganda, Kenya, and South Africa—each involving thousands of heterosexual men—provided robust evidence of this efficacy, with hazard ratios ranging from 0.40 to 0.47 for HIV incidence in circumcised versus uncircumcised groups, after adjusting for behavioral factors. [3] [4] These findings prompted the World Health Organization and UNAIDS in 2007 to endorse voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) as a key HIV prevention strategy in regions with generalized heterosexual epidemics, leading to the scale-up of programs that have delivered over 27 million procedures across 15 priority countries by 2023. [2] [5]

The mechanism underlying this protection stems from the removal of the foreskin, which harbors Langerhans cells susceptible to HIV entry and provides a moist environment conducive to viral persistence during heterosexual intercourse, thereby diminishing the biological vulnerability to infection via causal pathways independent of behavioral changes. [6] Meta-analyses of observational and trial data affirm consistent risk reductions, with limited evidence of behavioral disinhibition offsetting benefits, though efficacy appears context-specific to high-incidence, heterosexual-dominant epidemics and does not extend reliably to men who have sex with men or low-prevalence settings like the United States. [7] [8] Controversies persist, including critiques of trial methodologies—such as early termination potentially inflating efficacy estimates and inadequate controls for viral load or partner HIV status—but these have been rebutted by reanalyses and subsequent programmatic evaluations showing sustained population-level impacts without methodological collapse. [9] [10] VMMC's integration into broader prevention portfolios, alongside condoms and antiretrovirals, underscores its role as a one-time, cost-effective intervention, averting an estimated millions of infections, though debates over infant versus adult procedures and ethical promotion in diverse cultural contexts highlight ongoing tensions between evidence-based public health and individual autonomy. [11] [12]

## Scientific Evidence of Efficacy

### Randomized Controlled Trials in Heterosexual Men

Three randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in sub-Saharan Africa provided the primary evidence for the efficacy of male circumcision in reducing HIV acquisition among heterosexual men. These trials targeted HIV-negative men in regions with high heterosexual HIV transmission rates, primarily through vaginal intercourse with female partners. The studies were ethically halted early by data safety monitoring boards due to the evident protective effect, with follow-up periods ranging from 21 to 24 months. [13] [2]

The first trial, led by Auvert et al. in Orange Farm, South Africa, enrolled 3,274 uncircumcised men aged 18-24 years between 2002 and 2003. After 21 months, HIV incidence was 1.42% in the circumcision group versus 3.57% in the control group, yielding a relative risk reduction of 60% (adjusted hazard ratio 0.40, 95% CI 0.24-0.68). No significant behavioral disinhibition, such as increased sexual partners or reduced condom use, was observed. [14] [15]

In Kisumu, Kenya, Bailey et al. randomized 2,784 men aged 18-24 years from 2002 to 2005. Over 24 months, HIV incidence rates were 2.1% (95% CI 1.2-3.0) in the circumcised group and 4.2% (95% CI 3.0-5.4) in controls, corresponding to a 51% relative risk reduction (p=0.0065). Post-trial surveillance confirmed sustained protection, with incidence of 0.50 per 100 person-years in circumcised men versus 1.93 in uncircumcised men, and no evidence of risk compensation. [16] [17]

The Rakai trial in Uganda, conducted by Gray et al. from 2002 to 2003, involved 4,996 men aged 15-49 years. After 24 months, circumcision reduced HIV incidence by 51% (adjusted incidence rate ratio 0.49, 95% CI 0.26-0.93), with rates of 0.7% in the intervention group versus 1.3% in controls among men adhering to the protocol. The trial found no increase in adverse sexual behaviors, supporting circumcision's role without compensatory risk-taking. [18] [19]

Meta-analyses of these RCTs confirm a consistent 50-60% reduction in HIV acquisition risk for heterosexual men, with protective effects persisting in long-term follow-up and no offsetting increases in transmission to female partners within trial data. These findings, derived from double-blind randomization and intention-to-treat analyses, established circumcision as an additive prevention strategy in high-prevalence heterosexual epidemics, though generalizability to low-prevalence or non-African settings remains untested via RCTs due to ethical constraints. [13] [2]

### Evidence from Men Who Have Sex with Men

Observational studies and meta-analyses have yielded mixed results on the association between male circumcision and HIV acquisition among men who have sex with men (MSM), with no large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) completed until recently. A 2008 meta-analysis of 18 studies found no overall protective effect of circumcision against HIV or other sexually transmitted infections in MSM, though subgroup analyses indicated a potential association in pre-highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) era studies. [20] Similarly, a 2011 systematic review reported inconsistent findings across 29 studies, with some showing increased HIV odds among circumcised MSM, potentially due to confounding factors like behavioral differences or higher-risk sexual practices among uncircumcised groups. [21]

More recent meta-analyses suggest a modest protective association. A 2019 analysis of studies primarily from high-prevalence settings found circumcision linked to 23% lower odds of HIV infection overall among MSM, with stronger effects (up to 29% reduction) in regions with higher HIV endemicity, though protective estimates weakened after adjusting for confounders such as sexual role (insertive vs. receptive).30038-5/fulltext) A 2024 meta-analysis of 62 observational studies reported a 23% reduction in HIV acquisition risk for circumcised gay and bisexual men, but emphasized that this effect was primarily observed among primarily insertive (PI) MSM, with odds ratios as low as 0.57 in unadjusted models for this subgroup, compared to null or weaker effects for versatile or receptive partners where anal transmission predominates. [22] These findings align with Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) pooled data from observational studies indicating partial protection against HIV acquisition in MSM, though not quantified as robustly as the 50-60% efficacy seen in heterosexual RCTs. [23]

Limitations persist due to reliance on observational data, which may be biased by selection effects—such as circumcised MSM engaging in fewer high-risk behaviors or having better access to care—and lack of randomization. An ongoing RCT protocol from 2023 aims to directly test voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) efficacy in MSM, but results remain pending as of 2025. [24] A 2025 preprint meta-analysis reinforced protection specifically for PI-MSM (OR 0.57), supporting circumcision as a potential adjunctive strategy in this subgroup, but cautioned against broad extrapolation to all MSM given predominant receptive anal intercourse risks. [25] Neither the World Health Organization nor CDC recommends routine circumcision for HIV prevention in MSM populations, prioritizing instead proven interventions like pre-exposure prophylaxis and condom use, reflecting the evidence's observational nature and smaller effect size compared to heterosexual transmission. [5]

### Observational Studies and Meta-Analyses

Observational studies in sub-Saharan Africa, conducted before the scale-up of voluntary medical male circumcision programs, frequently reported lower HIV prevalence and incidence among circumcised men compared to uncircumcised men in heterosexual populations. For example, a cohort study in Kericho, Kenya, found that circumcised men had an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.32 (95% CI 0.15–0.68) for HIV acquisition, equivalent to a two-thirds risk reduction after controlling for confounders. [1] Similarly, analyses from multiple cross-sectional surveys in the region, such as those in Nairobi, showed uncircumcised men with genital ulcers had over 50% higher HIV seroconversion rates than circumcised counterparts. [1] However, some prevalence-based studies in South Africa, Kenya, Lesotho, and Tanzania reported no significant association after adjustment, highlighting potential confounding from behavioral or socioeconomic factors. [1]

Meta-analyses of observational data have synthesized these findings, often confirming a protective association in heterosexual men, though with heterogeneity due to study design and epidemic context. A 2020 systematic review encompassing 20 studies, including high-risk and community-based cohorts, reported pooled incidence ratios for HIV acquisition ranging from 0.29 (95% CI 0.19–0.43) in high-risk groups to 0.56 (95% CI 0.49–0.64) in community cohorts during circumcision scale-up, corresponding to relative risk reductions of 44–71%. [7] These estimates align with pre-scale-up community data showing a 52% reduction (pooled IR 0.48, 95% CI 0.33–0.70), but effectiveness appeared slightly attenuated in lower-incidence settings, possibly reflecting selection biases or changing sexual networks. [7] Such analyses underscore consistency with randomized trial results while noting limitations like residual confounding and self-reported circumcision status.

In men who have sex with men (MSM), observational evidence has been more variable. An early 2008 meta-analysis of pooled data from multiple studies found a nonsignificant reduction in HIV odds (OR approximately 0.80), concluding insufficient evidence for protection. [26] Subsequent reviews incorporating broader global data reported modest associations, with a 2019 systematic review of 62 studies estimating a 23% lower odds of HIV infection overall (OR 0.77), particularly in low- and middle-income countries and among those primarily engaging in insertive roles. [27] These findings suggest weaker or context-dependent effects compared to heterosexual transmission, attributable to differences in viral exposure during receptive anal intercourse, though confounding from partnership selection remains a concern across MSM studies. [27]

### Regional and Population Variations

The randomized controlled trials establishing male circumcision's efficacy against HIV acquisition were conducted in high-prevalence sub-Saharan African regions, specifically South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda, where they demonstrated a consistent approximately 60% risk reduction for heterosexual men engaging primarily in vaginal sex. [1] These trials, initiated between 2002 and 2005, informed targeted voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) programs in eastern and southern Africa, where HIV incidence among uncircumcised men exceeded 2-3% annually. [28] Regional ecological data from Africa further indicate an inverse correlation between baseline male circumcision prevalence—often tied to ethnic, religious, or cultural practices—and HIV prevalence, supporting but not proving causality. [29]

In lower-prevalence settings outside sub-Saharan Africa, such as North America and Europe, evidence relies on observational studies prone to confounders like sexual partnering patterns and condom use, yielding inconsistent associations between circumcision and reduced HIV risk. [26] Post-hoc analyses of trial follow-up data suggest circumcision's protective effect may diminish in cohorts with HIV incidence below 1% per year, potentially due to reduced statistical power or differing transmission dynamics. [30]

Population-level variations highlight stronger protection against female-to-male heterosexual transmission compared to male-to-male transmission. Meta-analyses of observational studies report a 20-23% lower HIV odds among circumcised men who have sex with men (MSM), with high heterogeneity (I²=77%) and no randomized trial confirmation, contrasting the robust RCT data for heterosexual men. [21] Earlier syntheses of pre-antiretroviral therapy era MSM studies found insufficient evidence of protection, particularly for receptive anal intercourse, while recent analyses indicate modest benefits possibly confined to predominantly insertive MSM. [26] [31] Across populations, adjusted relative risks from meta-analyses of incidence studies range from 0.40 to 0.60, aligning with trial efficacy but varying by baseline HIV exposure and circumcision timing (adult versus neonatal). [7]

## Biological Mechanisms

### Cellular and Mucosal Factors

The inner foreskin mucosa harbors a high density of HIV-1 target cells, including CD4+ T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, and Langerhans cells, which express receptors such as CCR5 and Langerin that facilitate viral attachment and entry. [32] [33] These cells are more abundant and activated in the foreskin compared to other penile tissues, creating a favorable microenvironment for HIV replication and dissemination to lymphoid tissues. [34] [35] In ex vivo studies of foreskin explants from African men, HIV-1 rapidly infects Langerhans cells in the inner foreskin via cell-cell viral synapses, often within one hour of exposure, leading to efficient transmission to underlying T cells. [36] [37]

The mucosal epithelium of the inner foreskin is characterized by a thin, non-keratinized or thinly keratinized layer, which contrasts with the thicker, stratified keratinized epithelium of the outer foreskin, glans, and shaft; this reduced barrier function allows deeper viral penetration to target cells during heterosexual intercourse. [38] [39] Elevated chemokine levels, such as CCL5 (RANTES), in the inner foreskin further promote HIV entry by recruiting susceptible immune cells and enhancing viral synapse formation. [40] Post-circumcision, removal of the inner foreskin eliminates this vulnerable portal, while the exposed mucosal surfaces undergo adaptive keratinization, thickening the epithelial barrier and reducing microbial translocation that could exacerbate local inflammation and HIV susceptibility. [41] [42]

These cellular and mucosal features contribute to the observed 50-60% reduction in HIV acquisition risk among circumcised heterosexual men in randomized trials, as the foreskin represents a primary entry site enriched for viral targets and deficient in protective barriers. [43] [44] Experimental models confirm that foreskin-derived tissues exhibit higher HIV infectivity than keratinized penile skin, underscoring the causal role of these factors independent of behavioral confounders. [45]

### Viral Entry and Transmission Dynamics

The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) primarily enters the male genital tract during heterosexual intercourse through contact with infected vaginal or cervical secretions, targeting mucosal epithelia and submucosal immune cells such as CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells like Langerhans cells. [46] In uncircumcised males, the inner foreskin (prepuce) and frenulum represent key entry portals due to their thin, non-keratinized mucosal epithelium, which facilitates viral penetration and lacks the protective barrier of stratified squamous keratinized skin found elsewhere on the penile shaft. [47] Histological studies indicate that the inner foreskin harbors a high density of Langerhans cells—specialized dendritic cells expressing HIV co-receptors CCR5 and CXCR4—that efficiently capture cell-free HIV virions and transmit them to underlying CD4+ T cells, amplifying local infection. [32] Ex vivo tissue explant models demonstrate that foreskin-derived Langerhans cells uptake HIV-1 at rates several times higher than those from cervical or keratinized penile tissues, with the virus replicating more readily in the submucosa due to elevated chemokine levels like CCL5 that recruit susceptible target cells. [37] [48]

Circumcision alters these dynamics by excising the foreskin, thereby eliminating approximately 50-70% of the penile mucosal surface area vulnerable to HIV ingress during penile-vaginal intercourse. [41] Post-circumcision, the exposed glans penis and coronal sulcus undergo adaptive keratinization, thickening the epithelial layer and reducing permeability to viral particles, as evidenced by increased transepithelial resistance and decreased HIV penetration in keratinized versus mucosal tissues. [49] This structural change, combined with reduced micro-abrasions and chronic inflammation under the foreskin—conditions that upregulate HIV co-receptors and pro-viral cytokines—lowers the density of target cells and the efficiency of viral seeding in the male genital tract. [50] Experimental data from penile biopsy studies show that uncircumcised foreskin exhibits higher baseline expression of CCR5 on keratinocytes and Langerhans cells compared to circumcised glans tissue, correlating with greater susceptibility to R5-tropic HIV strains predominant in heterosexual transmission. [51] Additionally, the removal of the foreskin diminishes the subpreputial space where smegma accumulation and bacterial overgrowth foster an inflammatory milieu that enhances HIV attachment and entry via upregulated adhesion molecules. [52]

Transmission dynamics are further influenced by circumcision's impact on viral trafficking: in uncircumcised men, HIV infection often localizes initially in foreskin Langerhans cells before disseminating to draining lymph nodes, whereas circumcision shifts potential entry to less permissive keratinized sites, reducing the probability of establishing a productive genital infection. [53] Quantitative models estimate that these biological modifications account for the observed 60% reduction in HIV acquisition risk in randomized controlled trials, primarily by curtailing the initial viral inoculum's access to high-yield target cells during the brief exposure window of intercourse. [41] [50] While male-to-female transmission dynamics show no direct reduction from circumcision in HIV-positive men—due to persistent urethral and ejaculate reservoirs—population-level effects arise indirectly from lowered male prevalence, illustrating how entry barriers in recipients modulate epidemic trajectories. [54] These mechanisms, validated through histological, virological, and epidemiological convergence, underscore circumcision's role in disrupting HIV's mucosal foothold without relying solely on behavioral factors. [49]

## Public Health Recommendations

### World Health Organization Guidelines

In 2007, following the publication of three randomized controlled trials in South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda that demonstrated a 51–60% reduction in HIV acquisition risk among heterosexual men undergoing voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC), the World Health Organization (WHO) and Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) jointly recommended the scale-up of safe, voluntary VMMC services as part of a comprehensive HIV prevention package in 14 priority countries in eastern and southern Africa with generalized HIV epidemics and low prevalence of male circumcision. [2] [55] These trials, conducted between 2002 and 2007, involved over 10,000 participants and showed consistent efficacy against heterosexual transmission, prompting WHO to position VMMC as an additional, efficacious intervention alongside condom promotion, HIV testing, and antiretroviral therapy, while emphasizing that it offers only partial protection and does not eliminate risk. [56]

WHO guidelines specify that VMMC targets males aged 10 years and older in priority settings, prioritizing adolescents and adult men aged 15–49 where HIV incidence is high, with services required to be provided by trained health professionals under aseptic conditions to minimize complications, which occur at rates below 2% in program data. [2] Updated in 2020, the guidelines—"Preventing HIV through safe voluntary medical male circumcision for adolescents and adults"—incorporate evidence on surgical techniques, including device-based methods for faster procedures, adolescent-specific counseling to ensure informed consent, and integration with sexual health services, while modeling estimates that sustained VMMC scale-up could avert up to 3.3 million HIV infections in eastern and southern Africa by 2025. [56] These updates address implementation challenges, such as reaching men at higher risk, and stress sustainability through country-led financing transitions, as donor funding has supported over 30 million procedures since 2008 but faces declines. [57]

WHO does not recommend VMMC for HIV prevention among men who have sex with men (MSM) or in low-prevalence settings outside generalized heterosexual epidemics, citing insufficient evidence from randomized trials for these populations, though observational data suggest potential benefits in some subgroups. [2] In October 2023, WHO reaffirmed VMMC's cost-effectiveness—estimated at $2,000–$4,000 per infection averted—at current coverage levels below 60% in many priority countries, urging intensified efforts to achieve 90% coverage among males aged 15–29 to maximize population-level impact amid competing prevention priorities like pre-exposure prophylaxis. [57] Guidelines explicitly require client education on VMMC's limitations, including no protection against receptive anal transmission or other sexually transmitted infections, and promote combination prevention to address residual risks. [56]

### National and Regional Policies

Following the 2007 World Health Organization (WHO) and Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) recommendation, national governments in high HIV-prevalence regions of Eastern and Southern Africa adopted policies to scale up voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) as an additional HIV prevention strategy, targeting a 60% reduction in heterosexual female-to-male transmission risk based on randomized controlled trials. [2] These policies emphasize safe, voluntary procedures for HIV-negative males aged 10–49 years, integrated with counseling, testing, and condom promotion, and are supported by international funding such as the U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR). [58] By 2023, 15 priority countries—Botswana, Eswatini, Ethiopia, Kenya, Lesotho, Malawi, Mozambique, Namibia, Rwanda, South Africa, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and Angola—had implemented VMMC programs, performing over 27 million procedures since 2007, though coverage remains below the 90% target in many areas due to operational challenges. [2] [59]

In South Africa, the national policy framework was formalized through the 2014 National Guidelines for Medical Male Circumcision, updated periodically to align with WHO standards, mandating provider training, adverse event monitoring, and integration into primary health care to address the country's high HIV burden, where VMMC is projected to avert significant infections and costs. [60] [61] Kenya launched its National Strategy for VMMC in 2008, initially focusing on the Nyanza region with low circumcision prevalence, expanding nationwide with task-shifting to clinical officers for procedures, achieving substantial coverage increases while adhering to quality assurance protocols. [62] [63] Similar policies in countries like Malawi, Uganda, and Zimbabwe prioritize demand creation, supply chain management, and sustainability transitions from donor to domestic funding, reflecting regional adaptations to epidemiological data showing greater efficacy in generalized epidemics. [64]

Outside Africa, policies are less aggressive due to differing HIV transmission dynamics. In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) issued recommendations in 2014–2018 for providers to inform uncircumcised heterosexual men at elevated HIV risk about the procedure's approximately 50–60% efficacy against acquisition from female partners, based on African trials extrapolated to U.S. contexts, without mandating neonatal or routine programs. [65] [5] European and other low-prevalence regions generally do not promote VMMC for HIV prevention, prioritizing behavioral and biomedical interventions like pre-exposure prophylaxis over surgical ones, as evidenced by the absence of national endorsements in bodies like the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control. [66] These variations underscore policies' grounding in local prevalence and trial evidence, with African programs emphasizing scale-up amid debates on long-term adherence and indirect benefits.

## Implementation of Voluntary Medical Male Circumcision

### Program Rollouts in High-Prevalence Areas

Following the World Health Organization's 2007 recommendation for voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) as an HIV prevention strategy in high-prevalence areas, programs began rolling out in 2008 across 15 priority countries in eastern and southern Africa, where heterosexual transmission predominates and HIV prevalence exceeds 10% in adult populations. Initial implementations targeted countries including eSwatini, Kenya, Malawi, South Africa, and Zambia, with support from the U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), UNAIDS, and national health ministries. These efforts aimed to achieve 80% coverage among HIV-negative men aged 15-49 by 2016, focusing on safe, voluntary procedures integrated into existing health systems. [67] [68]

By 2019, over 26.8 million VMMCs had been performed in these priority countries, with PEPFAR alone supporting 32.5 million procedures since 2007 through funding for mobile clinics, demand creation campaigns, and surgical training. In South Africa, rollout accelerated after 2010, reaching 1.9 million circumcisions by March 2014, including mass circumcision camps during festive seasons to boost uptake. Kenya achieved notable success in Nyanza Province, circumcising over 230,000 men by 2013 through community mobilization and device-assisted methods, attaining coverage rates exceeding 90% in some districts. Uganda and Zambia similarly scaled services post-trials, incorporating early infant male circumcision to sustain long-term prevalence. [69] [70] [71] [72]

Scale-up extended to other high-prevalence nations like Botswana, Lesotho, Mozambique, Namibia, Rwanda, Tanzania, and Zimbabwe by the early 2010s, emphasizing quality assurance via WHO standards for adverse event monitoring and HIV testing integration. Achievements included averting an estimated 340,000 HIV infections between 2008 and 2019 through combined program impacts, though progress varied due to supply chain logistics and cultural resistance in low-traditional-circumcision areas. Recent data indicate over 27 million total procedures by 2023, with ongoing efforts to address coverage gaps amid shifting priorities toward device innovations and adolescent-focused outreach. [2] [73] [74]

### Coverage, Costs, and Operational Challenges

Voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) programs in 15 priority sub-Saharan African countries have achieved approximately 26 million procedures between 2008 and 2019, averting an estimated 340,000 new HIV infections. [75] [76] Male circumcision prevalence in the region rose from 40% during 2010–2015 to 56% during 2016–2023, reflecting substantial but spatially heterogeneous progress. [77] [78] However, UNAIDS targets for coverage have not been met in most countries, with national rates such as Tanzania's 84% in recent years falling short of the 95% goal set for 2023. [79] [80] In fiscal year 2024, PEPFAR-supported VMMCs reached over 762,000 men and boys in the first quarter, comprising 28% of the annual target, amid efforts to sustain momentum in high-prevalence areas. [81]

Unit costs for VMMC procedures typically range from US$90 to US$132 per individual, encompassing direct surgical expenses, supplies, and personnel. [11] [82] [71] Programs have demonstrated high cost-effectiveness, with analyses across multiple countries showing VMMC to be cost-saving over extended horizons, even assuming higher unit costs up to US$120. [57] [11] For instance, in Tanzania, the cost per HIV infection averted was estimated at US$11,300 for 2010–2015 scaling but dropped to US$3,200 when extended to 2025, underscoring long-term fiscal benefits. [83] These efficiencies arise from reduced HIV treatment burdens, though sustaining funding remains critical as antiretroviral therapy scale-up alters incidence dynamics. [84]

Operational challenges include demand generation difficulties, particularly among high-risk older men and hard-to-reach populations, leading to uneven uptake. [12] [85] The COVID-19 pandemic caused sharp declines, such as a 33.8% drop in procedures for males aged 10 years and older in one province during 2020, compounded by fears of complications (31% of barriers cited) and access issues. [86] [87] Supply chain constraints, including high costs for circumcision devices, and the need for trained personnel have hindered efficiency, with recommendations to lower device prices for broader adoption. [88] Recent shifts targeting adolescents aged 15–29 years, per PEPFAR guidance ceasing services for those under 15 since 2020, aim to optimize impact but require integrating VMMC into routine health systems to address sustainability gaps. [89] [90]

## Historical Context

### Early Observational Data and Hypotheses

In 1986, initial hypotheses emerged suggesting that male circumcision might reduce HIV acquisition risk in men, based on anatomical considerations and preliminary seroprevalence data. Alcena observed lower HIV rates among circumcised individuals in early screening efforts, proposing circumcision as a potential preventive factor. Similarly, Fink argued that the foreskin's inner mucosa, with its thin epithelial layer and abundance of target cells for HIV, could serve as a primary entry point for the virus during heterosexual intercourse, hypothesizing that its removal would diminish susceptibility. [91]

Cross-sectional and cohort studies in the mid-to-late 1980s provided supporting observational evidence, particularly in high-prevalence African settings. Among Nairobi truck drivers in Kenya, uncircumcised men exhibited significantly higher HIV seroprevalence (13% versus 3% in circumcised men), with prospective follow-up confirming elevated incidence linked to uncircumcised status after adjusting for confounders like sexual behavior. These findings, replicated in studies of female sex workers' clients, indicated odds ratios of HIV infection 5- to 8-fold higher in uncircumcised men, attributing part of the risk to increased vulnerability to genital ulcers and other sexually transmitted infections that facilitate HIV entry. [92] [93]

Ecological analyses in the late 1980s and early 1990s reinforced these patterns at population levels across Africa, revealing an inverse correlation between male circumcision prevalence and national HIV rates. Countries with predominantly non-circumcising populations in East and Southern Africa reported HIV prevalences exceeding 10-20% in urban adults by the early 1990s, compared to under 2-5% in circumcising Muslim-majority nations in West Africa, despite similar sexual network structures. Meta-analyses of over 30 such studies estimated a consistent 50-70% lower HIV risk among circumcised men in high-risk groups, prompting hypotheses that circumcision biologically limits viral ingress by eliminating the foreskin's Langerhans cells—dendritic cells highly susceptible to HIV—and reducing mucosal trauma during sex. [94] [92]

### Pivotal RCTs and Policy Adoption (2005–2007)

The first randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating male circumcision for HIV prevention was conducted in Orange Farm, South Africa, by Auvert et al., enrolling 3,274 uncircumcised HIV-negative men aged 18–24 years between 2002 and 2005. [15] Interim analysis after 473 person-years of follow-up in the intervention group and 499 in the control group revealed 20 HIV infections in the circumcision arm versus 49 in the control arm, yielding a relative risk reduction of 60% (95% CI: 32–77%), prompting early termination of the trial on ethical grounds. [15] No significant increases in sexual risk behavior were observed, and adverse events were minimal (3.8% moderate or severe complications, mostly resolving without intervention). [15]

Building on these findings, two larger RCTs reported results in early 2007. The Kenyan trial in Kisumu, led by Bailey et al., randomized 2,784 HIV-uninfected men aged 18–24 to immediate circumcision or delayed (after 24 months), with follow-up through September 2006. [17] Among 69 incident HIV cases (22 circumcised, 47 uncircumcised), circumcision conferred a 53% protective efficacy (95% CI: 22–72%; adjusted RR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.28–0.78), with no evidence of behavioral risk compensation—circumcised men reported similar or slightly fewer sexual partners and condom use rates. [17] Moderate/severe adverse events occurred in 0.3% of procedures. [17]

Concurrently, the Ugandan Rakai trial by Gray et al. involved 4,996 HIV-negative uncircumcised men aged 15–49, randomized to circumcision or control with 24-month follow-up ending September 2007.60313-4/fulltext) This yielded 67 HIV seroconversions (20 circumcised, 47 control), a 51% reduction in incidence (95% CI: 16–71%; adjusted HR 0.49, 95% CI: 0.29–0.82), again without increased risk behavior or sexual activity in the intervention group.60313-4/fulltext) Surgical complications were low (0.7% severe).60313-4/fulltext) The consistency across trials—each demonstrating approximately 50–60% efficacy against heterosexual HIV acquisition in high-prevalence sub-Saharan African settings—provided robust causal evidence from RCTs, surpassing prior ecological and observational associations. [95]

These results prompted rapid policy shifts. In March 2007, the World Health Organization (WHO) and Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS), following an expert consultation reviewing the trials, issued guidelines recognizing voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) as an efficacious additional intervention to reduce heterosexually transmitted HIV in men, particularly in 13 priority countries with HIV prevalence exceeding 15% in men aged 15+. [96] The recommendations emphasized integration with condom promotion, testing, and counseling, targeting adult uptake without mandating neonatal procedures, and called for safe, voluntary implementation to avoid coercion or unsafe traditional practices. [96] National programs in countries like South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda began scaling up VMMC shortly thereafter, informed by trial data on feasibility and safety. [95]

### Post-2007 Developments and Updates

![Celebration of the 1 millionth Voluntary Medical Male Circumcision in South Africa][float-right] Following the 2007 World Health Organization (WHO) and Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) recommendations, voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) programs expanded rapidly in 15 priority countries in eastern and southern Africa with high HIV prevalence and low male circumcision rates. [2] By October 2023, approximately 35 million men and adolescent boys had undergone VMMC through these initiatives, representing substantial progress toward averting new infections. [57]

Observational studies and long-term follow-ups from the original randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in Kenya, Uganda, and South Africa have corroborated the approximately 60% reduction in heterosexual HIV acquisition risk for men observed in the 2005–2007 trials. [12] [7] For instance, a 2020 analysis of follow-up data from a Zimbabwean cohort reported an HIV incidence of 1.2 per 100 person-years among circumcised men compared to higher rates in uncircumcised controls, with no evidence of waning protection over time. [7] Population-level data from sub-Saharan Africa have similarly associated increased VMMC coverage with reduced HIV incidence, though effects vary by region due to factors like program saturation and concurrent interventions. [97]

WHO reaffirmed VMMC as a core HIV prevention strategy in subsequent guidelines, including a 2020 operational manual emphasizing safe procedures and integration with other services. [56] Economic evaluations have consistently deemed VMMC highly cost-effective, with models projecting millions of infections averted at costs below per-infection treatment thresholds, prompting calls in 2023 for intensified scale-up amid stagnating coverage in some areas. [11] [57] Emerging data have explored VMMC's potential benefits for men who have sex with men (MSM), with a 2024 study indicating up to 91% risk reduction for predominantly insertive partners, though evidence remains limited outside heterosexual epidemics. [22]

Challenges in sustaining VMMC programs include operational hurdles, funding constraints, and heterogeneous progress, with coverage reaching over 90% adult male targets in countries like Ethiopia and Rwanda but lagging in others like Zimbabwe. [78] Despite these, meta-analyses and real-world assessments through 2023 continue to support VMMC's role in combination prevention, without indications of policy reversal. [98]

## Controversies and Criticisms

### Methodological Debates on Trial Design

The three randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating adult male circumcision for HIV prevention—conducted by Auvert et al. in South Africa (2005), Bailey et al. in Kenya (2007), and Gray et al. in Uganda (2007)—employed parallel-group designs randomizing uncircumcised heterosexual men aged 18–24 to immediate circumcision or delayed circumcision after 24 months of follow-up, with HIV incidence as the primary endpoint assessed via quarterly counseling and testing. [99] These trials reported relative risk reductions of 60% (Auvert), 51% (Bailey), and 56% (Gray), leading to early termination upon interim analyses demonstrating statistical significance. [1] However, methodological critiques have centered on inherent design limitations, including the impossibility of participant blinding, which introduces risks of performance bias (e.g., differential sexual risk behaviors due to knowledge of group assignment) and detection bias (e.g., influenced self-reporting or assessor expectations in HIV testing). [99] Proponents counter that objective HIV seroconversion outcomes and comparable self-reported behaviors across arms mitigate these concerns, as confirmed by subsequent Cochrane reviews assessing overall low risk of bias. [1]

Early stopping rules, applied after interim analyses at approximately 20–30% of planned enrollment, have drawn scrutiny for potentially overstating efficacy, as such decisions amplify type I errors and may capture transient effects not sustained long-term; for instance, the Auvert trial halted after 1,200 infections despite a planned sample exceeding 3,000. [100] Defenses highlight prespecified statistical boundaries (e.g., Haybittle-Peto) and consistency with post-trial observational data showing persistent protection up to 5–7 years in Rakai, Uganda. [1] Selection bias arises from volunteer participants, who exhibited lower baseline HIV prevalence (e.g., 2–6% in trial cohorts versus 10–20% community rates) and potentially safer behaviors due to motivation for trial entry, questioning internal validity and raising doubts about randomization balancing unmeasured confounders like partner HIV status or concurrent STI treatment. [101] Trial protocols addressed this via stratified randomization and intention-to-treat analyses, though attrition rates of 10–15% (higher in control arms awaiting circumcision) introduced further imbalance. [99]

Generalizability debates underscore the trials' restriction to high-prevalence (3–20% adult HIV), low-circumcision (<10%) sub-Saharan African settings among monogamous or low-partner-count men, limiting extrapolation to low-prevalence regions, female-to-male transmission assumptions, or populations with diverse sexual networks; observational data from diverse ecologies often yield weaker or null associations, prompting arguments that RCTs overestimate population-level causal effects absent real-world confounders like variable surgical quality or uptake barriers. [100] Critics also note inadequate pre-randomization counseling equipoise, with 70–80% of enrollees believing circumcision protective, potentially skewing compliance and endpoint ascertainment. [101] Rebuttals emphasize ecological consistency with pre-trial hypotheses and modeling validations, asserting that unblinded designs, while imperfect, represent pragmatic superiority over non-randomized alternatives prone to confounding. [102] Overall, while RCTs provide causal evidence under ideal conditions, debates persist on whether their design flaws necessitate supplementary evidence hierarchies for policy, with some analyses favoring integrated RCT-observational frameworks to capture temporal and contextual dynamics. [99]

### Ethical Concerns and Consent Issues

Ethical concerns surrounding voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) for HIV prevention primarily revolve around the adequacy of informed consent processes and the potential erosion of individual autonomy in implementation settings. International guidelines from organizations like WHO and PEPFAR mandate that VMMC be offered only with voluntary informed consent, involving pre-procedure counseling on the procedure's approximately 60% efficacy in reducing heterosexual HIV acquisition in men, surgical risks such as infection or excessive bleeding (occurring in 1-2% of cases), alternatives like condom use, and post-operative care requirements. [103] However, critics argue that in high-prevalence African contexts, low literacy rates, cultural norms, and program incentives can impair full comprehension and free choice, potentially transforming a preventive intervention into a coercive public health measure. [67]

Consent challenges are particularly acute for adolescents, who comprise a growing target group in VMMC scale-up efforts. In countries like Tanzania and Eswatini, national policies often require parental or guardian consent for minors under 18, alongside adolescent assent, yet studies reveal frequent lapses: up to 10% of procedures on Kenyan youth aged 15-19 occurred without documented parental approval, including instances of forged signatures or absent forms. [104] Social dynamics exacerbate this, as cultural deference to parental authority can override adolescent preferences, with reports of boys undergoing circumcision despite personal reluctance due to familial pressure (e.g., "my mother said I must circumcise"). [105] Counseling sessions often emphasize HIV prevention benefits while downplaying risks like pain or complications, leading to incomplete risk understanding; for instance, some adolescents mistakenly believed HIV testing was mandatory rather than optional. [105]

Economic and operational incentives introduce further risks of undue inducement, where compensation for time lost (e.g., transport reimbursements or small items like food) may disproportionately influence decisions among low-income participants. Ethical analyses contend that such payments, if exceeding actual economic losses, can act as "substantially controlling" factors, especially for economically vulnerable men, thereby compromising voluntariness and resembling coercion rather than reimbursement. [106] In Kenya, performance targets set by donors like PEPFAR—aiming for millions of procedures annually—have incentivized mobilizers to use shaming tactics against uncircumcised males or offer extraneous perks (e.g., sodas), while rushing intake to meet quotas, resulting in abbreviated counseling and circumvention of consent protocols. [104]

Broader autonomy debates highlight the procedure's irreversibility and the ethical tension between individual bodily integrity and population-level HIV reduction goals. Opponents, including bioethicists, question whether proxy interventions like VMMC adequately balance personal rights against aggregated benefits, particularly when social pressures from partners or communities—fearing HIV stigma—prompt uptake without fully autonomous deliberation. [67] Program evaluations acknowledge these issues, recommending enhanced training for providers and independent oversight to safeguard consent, though persistent target-driven metrics continue to raise doubts about prioritization of numbers over ethical rigor. [104]

### Behavioral Risk Compensation Claims

Critics of voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) for HIV prevention have claimed that the procedure could induce behavioral risk compensation, whereby circumcised men perceive reduced personal risk and subsequently increase sexual risk-taking, such as engaging in more unprotected sex or having multiple partners, potentially offsetting protective benefits. [107] This concern draws from broader theories of risk homeostasis in public health interventions, where perceived safety gains lead to compensatory behaviors, as observed in some contexts like helmet laws or condom promotion. [108] Such claims were raised early in the debate following the 2005–2007 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), with arguments that trial designs inadequately monitored or adjusted for post-circumcision behavioral shifts, particularly resumption of sex before full healing. [107]

However, longitudinal data from the pivotal RCTs in South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda found no significant evidence of risk compensation. In the ANRS 1265 trial, circumcised participants showed no increase in sexual activity or partner numbers compared to controls over 21 months, with even slight reductions in reported unprotected sex in some subgroups. [109] Similarly, the Rakai trial reported stable or decreased risky behaviors post-circumcision, with no rise in HIV incidence attributable to behavioral changes. [110] Post-trial surveillance in implementation settings, including over 20 million VMMCs by 2020, confirmed that circumcised men did not exhibit higher rates of multiple partners or condomless sex relative to uncircumcised peers. [111]

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reinforced these findings, analyzing data from RCTs and observational studies across high-prevalence regions. A 2021 meta-analysis of 13 studies involving heterosexual men concluded no association between medical male circumcision and increased risky behaviors, such as condomless sex (odds ratio 0.94, 95% CI 0.82–1.07) or multiple partners (OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.92–1.20). [112] Another review of VMMC rollout data found minimal early resumption of sex post-procedure (median 26–40 days, aligning with healing guidelines) and no sustained uptick in risk compensation, attributing any minor perceptions of invulnerability to counseling failures rather than inherent effects. [113] These results hold despite potential underreporting biases in self-reported behaviors, as HIV incidence trends in circumcised cohorts remained 50–60% lower than expected without compensation.00360-0/fulltext)

While isolated observational studies have suggested possible localized increases in partner numbers among newly circumcised youth, these lack causal controls and are outweighed by RCT evidence; critics' emphasis on hypothetical compensation has not been substantiated empirically, with modeling indicating that even moderate risk increases would erode observed HIV reductions, which persist in population data. [114] Overall, the absence of verifiable risk compensation supports VMMC's net protective role, though ongoing behavioral counseling remains recommended to mitigate any perceptual risks. [115]

### Opposition Narratives and Rebuttals

Opponents of voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) for HIV prevention have argued that the randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in Africa between 2005 and 2007 overstated efficacy due to methodological flaws, such as inadequate randomization, short follow-up periods, and failure to account for confounding factors like sexual behavior or concurrent interventions. [116] These critics, including some public health advocates, contend that observational data prior to the RCTs already showed associations but lacked causation, and that the trials' early termination upon interim efficacy findings undermined full assessment of risks. [117] Additionally, claims persist that VMMC promotes a false sense of security, potentially diverting resources from proven strategies like condom promotion and antiretroviral therapy (ART). [118]

A prominent opposition narrative centers on behavioral risk compensation, positing that circumcised men increase sexual risk-taking—such as more partners, less condom use, or earlier resumption of sex—offsetting any biological protection. [119] Some analyses of post-trial data in Uganda suggested circumcised men were less likely to abstain, interpreting this as disinhibition. [120] Ethical critiques further highlight consent issues in resource-limited settings, where community pressures or inadequate counseling may coerce participation, and argue that promoting surgery ignores bodily autonomy and potential complications like infection or pain, especially when alternatives exist. [121] Groups opposing non-therapeutic circumcision extend this to VMMC, framing it as cultural imperialism rather than evidence-based policy. [10]

Rebuttals emphasize that the three pivotal RCTs (in South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda) demonstrated consistent 50-60% reduction in HIV acquisition among heterosexual men, with pooled analyses confirming biological causality via mechanisms like reduced viral entry through the foreskin. [111] Long-term follow-up from the Orange Farm trial in South Africa showed sustained 58% efficacy at 72 months, aligning with initial findings and refuting decay claims. [122] Methodological critiques are dismissed as denialism, given the trials' rigorous design, including intention-to-treat analyses and adjustments for behavior, which found no evidence of bias invalidating results. [116] [123]

On risk compensation, systematic reviews and meta-analyses of post-circumcision cohorts report no substantial increase in risky behaviors; a 2021 analysis of 28 studies found higher-quality evidence showing neutral or reduced risk profiles, with circumcised men often reporting safer practices due to counseling. [112] [124] Real-world programs in 15 African countries, scaling to over 30 million procedures by 2023, correlate with declining HIV incidence without offsetting surges in transmission attributable to disinhibition. [125] Ethical defenses note VMMC's voluntary adult nature, informed consent protocols, and WHO endorsement as a complement—not replacement—to ABC (abstinence, behavior change, condoms) strategies, with complication rates under 1% in scaled programs. [10] Critics' reliance on selective or low-quality data is contrasted with the robustness of RCT evidence, underscoring VMMC's role in causal reduction of female-to-male transmission in high-prevalence heterosexual epidemics. [126]

## Impact and Cost-Effectiveness

### Estimated Prevented Infections and Modeling

Mathematical models of voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) for HIV prevention integrate randomized controlled trial data indicating a 60% reduction in heterosexual HIV acquisition risk among circumcised men, projecting population-level impacts based on coverage rates, HIV prevalence, sexual behavior patterns, and transmission dynamics. [2] [127] These dynamic models, such as the Decision Makers' Program Planning Tool (DMPPT 2.0), simulate averted infections by estimating reduced male-to-female and subsequent transmissions over time horizons of 10–30 years. [128] Early projections from 2009 indicated that one HIV infection could be averted for every 5–15 VMMCs in high-prevalence settings over a 10-year period. [129]

Retrospective modeling attributes specific averted infections to implemented VMMC programs since 2007, when over 27 million procedures were performed across 15 priority countries in eastern and southern Africa. [2] By 2017, these efforts were estimated to have prevented 230,000 new infections, with forecasts anticipating over 1 million averted by 2030 under sustained scale-up. [130] [28] WHO and UNAIDS analyses projected 340,000 (range: 260,000–440,000) infections averted by 2019, including benefits to female partners through reduced prevalence. [69] A 2022 evaluation using multiple models (Goals-ASM, Thembisa, EMOD) estimated up to 83,000 infections prevented from 2010–2020 in select contexts, highlighting variability due to local epidemiology and program reach. [11]

Forward-looking simulations emphasize scale-up potential: achieving 80% circumcision coverage among HIV-negative men aged 15–49 over 15 years could avert 3.4 million infections in low- and middle-income countries. [112] Country-specific models, such as in Malawi, predict that targeting males aged 10–29 for circumcision over 15 years would prevent 75% of projected infections in that cohort, rising to 88% if extended to age 34. [131] These estimates assume lifelong protection efficacy of 58–60% against female-to-male transmission and incorporate indirect effects on women, though outcomes depend on adherence to modeling parameters like minimal risk compensation. [11] [98]

### Economic Evaluations and Long-Term Projections

Economic evaluations of voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) programs for HIV prevention in sub-Saharan Africa have predominantly concluded that the intervention is cost-effective, particularly in settings with moderate to high heterosexual HIV transmission. A 2022 modeling analysis across 13 countries found VMMC cost-effective (defined as less than three times the gross domestic product per capita per disability-adjusted life year averted) in 62% of scenarios with adult HIV incidence exceeding 1.5 per 100 person-years, with effectiveness increasing in higher-incidence areas due to greater absolute risk reduction.00515-0/fulltext) The World Health Organization's 2023 review of programmatic data from priority countries affirmed VMMC as highly cost-effective or cost-saving in nearly all evaluated scenarios, attributing this to low per-procedure costs (typically $50–150) and the intervention's one-time nature, which averts lifelong treatment expenses estimated at $1,000–2,000 annually per person on antiretrovirals. [57] Variations in cost per HIV infection averted are substantial, ranging from $78 in optimized Kenyan programs with high uptake to over $22,000 in lower-incidence Rwandan contexts, influenced by factors like procedure volume, adverse event rates (under 2%), and baseline prevalence. [132]

Comparisons with alternative HIV prevention strategies, such as antiretroviral therapy scale-up, position VMMC favorably in cost metrics. One analysis estimated VMMC's cost per infection averted at $1,096 versus $6,790 for treatment-as-prevention, reflecting VMMC's upstream preventive impact on incident cases without ongoing drug requirements. [133] In South Africa, where over 3 million VMMCs were performed by 2016, program costs averaged $120 per procedure, yielding net financial returns through reduced HIV-related healthcare expenditures. [61]

Long-term projections from dynamic epidemic models underscore VMMC's potential for sustained impact if coverage reaches 90% among non-circumcised adult males in priority countries. UNAIDS-supported simulations forecast that maintaining VMMC through 2030 could avert 2–5 million infections across eastern and southern Africa, with cumulative savings exceeding program costs by factors of 2–10 under 3–5% discount rates, assuming stable heterosexual transmission dynamics. [134] A South African cohort model projected $617 net savings per VMMC performed at age 20 (discounted at 5%), escalating with earlier implementation due to extended protection periods spanning decades. [61] However, projections indicate diminishing returns in scenarios of rapid incidence decline from antiretrovirals and testing (projected 20–50% drops by 2030 in some models), potentially rendering VMMC less cost-effective below incidence thresholds of 0.8–1.0 per 100 person-years without targeted prioritization of high-risk groups.00001-3/fulltext) Recent disruptions, including funding reductions post-2023, threaten these gains, with models estimating 10–20% fewer averts if scale-up stalls. [135]
