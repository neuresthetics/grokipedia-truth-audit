# Female genital mutilation in the United States

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in the United States refers to procedures that intentionally alter or injure the female genital organs for non-medical reasons, including partial or total removal of the external genitalia, a practice prohibited by federal statute since 1996 and occurring chiefly among certain immigrant groups from high-prevalence regions in Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. [1] [2] The federal law, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 116, criminalizes performing, assisting, or facilitating FGM, with penalties up to five years imprisonment, and explicitly rejects religious, cultural, or traditional justifications as defenses. [1] While the procedure offers no health benefits and inflicts immediate and long-term physical and psychological harm, including pain, infection, urinary issues, sexual dysfunction, and increased childbirth complications, enforcement remains challenging due to underreporting, victim reluctance, and cultural secrecy. [2] [3]

Estimates from U.S. government analyses indicate that over 500,000 women and girls in the United States have either undergone FGM abroad or face risk of undergoing it domestically or during travel, derived from demographic data on immigrants and their descendants from countries with FGM rates exceeding 20 percent. [4] This figure underscores a persistent public health and human rights issue, concentrated in urban areas with large diasporas from nations like Somalia, Egypt, and Nigeria, where prevalence can approach 90 percent in origin populations. [5] Federal agencies, including the Department of Justice and Immigration and Customs Enforcement, have pursued prosecutions in notable cases involving practitioners and transporters of minors, though convictions are infrequent owing to evidentiary hurdles and judicial scrutiny of the law's scope. [6] [7]

State-level responses vary, with some enacting specific bans reinforcing federal prohibitions and others relying on general child abuse or assault statutes, reflecting uneven legislative attention to the problem. [8] Ongoing federal initiatives, such as the STOP FGM Act of 2019, mandate interagency coordination for prevention, education, and reporting, aiming to address data gaps and community outreach amid criticisms of inadequate surveillance and cultural sensitivity debates that sometimes impede intervention. [9] [10]

## Prevalence and Demographics

### Current Estimates

A 2016 analysis by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), drawing on data from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and Demographic and Health Surveys, estimated that 513,000 women and girls in the United States were either affected by female genital mutilation (FGM) or at risk of undergoing it. [5] This figure represented a tripling of prior estimates from 1990, largely attributable to increased immigration from countries where FGM prevalence exceeds 50 percent, such as Somalia, Egypt, Sudan, and Ethiopia. [11] The at-risk population primarily consists of girls under 18 from immigrant families, with the highest concentrations in communities originating from sub-Saharan Africa and parts of the Middle East. [5]

No comprehensive national estimates have been updated since 2016, creating significant data gaps amid continued immigration from high-prevalence regions. [2] Globally, UNICEF reported in 2024 that over 230 million girls and women alive today have undergone FGM, with Africa accounting for more than 144 million cases, underscoring the potential for elevated risks in the U.S. tied to demographic shifts. [12] Without routine tracking or mandatory reporting in all states, actual prevalence likely exceeds documented figures, as procedures often occur covertly to evade legal scrutiny. [13]

Underreporting is exacerbated by clandestine practices, including "cutting parties" organized within immigrant enclaves in urban areas like Minneapolis, Seattle, and New York, where girls are subjected to FGM by traditional practitioners or family members. [14] Medicalized procedures, performed by health workers in clinics or hospitals to mimic legitimacy, further obscure detection, with some surveys indicating support for such approaches among affected communities to reduce perceived harms while preserving cultural norms. [15] These factors contribute to hidden caseloads, as victims rarely seek care due to stigma, fear of reprisal, or distrust of authorities, limiting empirical verification. [2]

### Affected and At-Risk Populations

The primary victims of female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) in the United States are girls under 18 years old from first- or second-generation immigrant families originating from countries with high FGM/C prevalence rates, where the practice involves non-consensual procedures on minors typically aligned with World Health Organization classifications of Types I (clitoridectomy), II (excision), or III (infibulation). [16] In 2012, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates indicated that 513,000 women and girls were at risk, with 169,000 (about one-third) being girls under 18, reflecting a 314% increase in the minor at-risk population since 1990 due to immigration-driven demographic shifts rather than rising origin-country rates. [16] [5]

These at-risk groups are overwhelmingly from African nations, comprising 97% of the total, with the largest shares from Egypt (20%), Ethiopia (18%), and Somalia (12%), where national FGM/C rates exceed 70-90% among females. [5] [16] Second-generation U.S.-born daughters form a substantial portion of the younger at-risk cohort, as parents may perpetuate origin-country customs despite acculturation pressures, though empirical data show no reliable measurement of actual U.S.-performed incidences due to underreporting and clandestine nature. [5] Risk is amplified by "cutting vacations," where families transport prepubescent girls abroad for the procedure to evade U.S. scrutiny, or through domestic underground practitioners, linking causal persistence directly to familial ties with high-prevalence diasporas. [16]

Claims minimizing FGM/C as voluntary cultural choice lack empirical support, as procedures target non-consenting minors—often infants or young girls—causing immediate risks like hemorrhage and infection alongside long-term complications such as urinary issues, sexual dysfunction, and psychological trauma, corroborated by clinical outcomes in affected communities. [2] [16] Hospital data from immigrant-heavy regions reveal elevated presentations for FGM/C-related complications, underscoring non-voluntary imposition without evidence of affirmative minor consent in documented cases. [5]

### Geographic Concentrations

Estimates indicate that female genital mutilation (FGM) risks and occurrences in the United States are disproportionately concentrated in metropolitan areas and states with substantial immigrant populations from countries where the practice is culturally entrenched, such as Somalia, Ethiopia, Egypt, and Sudan. A 2013 analysis using U.S. Census data identified the highest numbers of women and girls at risk in the New York-Newark-Jersey City metro area (65,893), followed by Washington-Arlington-Alexandria (51,411) and Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington (37,417), with these five largest metros accounting for about 40% of the national total of approximately 507,000 at risk. [5] These patterns reflect immigration-driven demographics, as foreign-born individuals from the 27 WHO-designated FGM-prevalent countries comprise over 90% of those estimated at risk. [16]

In Minnesota, home to one of the largest Somali diaspora communities in the U.S.—with over 80,000 Somalis resettled since the 1990s—FGM risks are elevated due to the practice's near-universal prevalence (98%) in Somalia. State health data highlight ongoing concerns in the Minneapolis-St. Paul area, where underground networks persist despite awareness campaigns, and an estimated 44,000 women and girls are at risk statewide. [17] [5] Similarly, Washington state, including Seattle's Somali population, shows concentrations linked to refugee resettlement, with broader state estimates contributing to the top-eight at-risk tally. [5] [18]

Georgia's Atlanta metro area emerges as a hotspot through refugee inflows from Ethiopia and Eritrea (FGM rates 74% and 89%, respectively), with state-level estimates placing around 58,000 women and girls with ancestral ties from practicing countries. [19] In Michigan, the 2017 federal prosecution of a Detroit-area doctor for performing FGM on at least seven girls aged 6 to 8—primarily from the Dawoodi Bohra Muslim sect, which maintains the practice despite origins outside traditional African contexts—underscored localized underground operations in immigrant enclaves. [20]

State-level variations align with immigration gateways: California (57,000 at risk), New York (48,000), and Minnesota lead, comprising over half of national figures alongside Maryland, New Jersey, Texas, Virginia, and Washington. [5] Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) tracking, derived from census and health surveys, notes under-detection in rural areas and non-gateway states due to clandestine practices, limited reporting, and cultural stigma, potentially understating true incidence beyond urban clusters. [2] [16]

## Historical Development

### Early Instances and Awareness

Sporadic reports of female genital mutilation (FGM) among small immigrant communities from African countries appeared in the United States during the 1970s and 1980s, coinciding with initial waves of refugees from nations such as Ethiopia and Somalia where the practice is culturally entrenched. [21] Medical documentation first emerged in refugee health clinics, where providers identified complications like infections and urinary issues in affected women, marking an initial shift from general ignorance of the procedure's presence domestically to empirical recognition of its physical harms. [22]

By the early 1990s, awareness intensified through refugee resettlement data compiled by health agencies, revealing patterns of FGM-related health risks among arrivals from high-prevalence regions. This documentation prompted heightened scrutiny, culminating in congressional proposals to address the issue. In 1994, Senator Bill Frist introduced S. 2501, the Federal Prohibition of Female Genital Mutilation Act, signaling formal acknowledgment of FGM as a domestic concern tied to immigrant health vulnerabilities. [23] A companion bill, H.R. 941, followed in 1995 under Representative Barbara-Rose Collins, further elevating the practice in policy discussions prior to enactment. [24]

The World Health Organization's 1996 classification of FGM into four types—emphasizing its non-therapeutic nature and severe consequences—reinforced U.S. discourse by providing a standardized framework for viewing the procedure as medically harmful rather than culturally neutral. [25] This international benchmark, combined with domestic refugee health findings, facilitated a transition from anecdotal clinic reports to structured recognition of FGM's causality in chronic pain, obstetric complications, and psychological trauma among affected populations. [26]

### Rise with Immigration

![2020 Global FGM prevalence map showing high-risk immigration source countries][float-right]

The increase in female genital mutilation in the United States since the 1990s correlates directly with waves of immigration from countries with high prevalence rates, particularly in Africa. The Somali civil war, beginning in 1991, prompted significant refugee resettlement in the US, with Somalis arriving in Minnesota starting in 1992 and forming concentrated communities by the early 2000s. [27] Overall African-born immigrants in the US rose from approximately 881,000 in 2000 to 1.8 million in 2013, predominantly entering between 1990 and 2000, including from FGM-prevalent nations like Somalia (98% prevalence), Egypt (91%), and Ethiopia. [28] [5]

Estimates of women and girls affected or at risk reflect this demographic shift: approximately 168,000 in 1990, increasing to 228,000 by 2000 and 507,000–545,000 by 2012–2013, a tripling overall and quadrupling for those under 18. [4] [5] This expansion stems from immigration volumes rather than rising rates in origin countries, with 97% of at-risk individuals tracing origins to Africa; top sources included Egypt (over 100,000), Ethiopia (around 90,000), and Somalia (64,000–75,000). [4] [5]

In unassimilated enclaves, such as Somali communities in Minnesota and Ohio, the practice persists across generations, with studies indicating continued support and transmission despite US residence. [29] Family reunification policies have further amplified inflows from high-risk regions, sustaining cultural norms that embed FGM as a rite of passage, challenging assumptions of automatic cessation upon migration. [4] Empirical data from immigrant cohorts show that geographic isolation and community cohesion maintain prevalence levels akin to homelands, rather than inevitable assimilation-driven decline. [29]

## Legal Prohibitions

### Federal Laws and Evolution

The Federal Prohibition of Female Genital Mutilation Act of 1996 established the first nationwide ban on FGM, criminalizing the knowing performance, attempt, or conspiracy to perform any clitoridectomy, excision, or infibulation on the labia majora, labia minora, or clitoris of a female under 18 years of age, classifying it as a felony punishable by fines and up to five years imprisonment. [24] [1] Codified at 18 U.S.C. § 116, the statute included a narrow affirmative defense for procedures deemed medically necessary to the person's health and performed by a licensed medical professional in that jurisdiction, but no allowances for cultural, religious, or customary practices. [1] This legislation framed FGM as a form of child abuse under federal criminal law, targeting acts regardless of parental consent or perpetrator intent beyond knowledge of the procedure. [16]

In 2013, Congress enacted the Transport for Female Genital Mutilation Act as an amendment, extending prohibitions to include knowingly transporting or attempting to transport any person across a U.S. state line or international border with intent to subject them to FGM, thereby addressing "vacation cutting" practices where minors are taken abroad for the procedure. [30] This expansion reinforced federal jurisdiction over interstate and foreign elements, maintaining the focus on protecting minors without cultural exemptions. [31]

The Strengthening the Opposition to Female Genital Mutilation Act of 2020 (STOP FGM Act), signed into law on January 5, 2021, further amended 18 U.S.C. § 116 to increase the maximum penalty to 10 years imprisonment, explicitly affirm prosecution viability through links to interstate or foreign commerce, and clarify that the offense applies irrespective of the victim's consent or the perpetrator's beliefs about cultural or religious requirements. [9] [31] These enhancements marked a shift toward stricter enforcement mechanisms and zero-tolerance policy, emphasizing FGM's classification as non-consensual child harm without health-based justifications beyond the existing medical exception. [32] The federal prohibition remains limited to acts on minors under 18, with no direct criminalization of procedures on consenting adults, though transportation offenses apply more broadly. [1]

### State-Level Regulations

As of 2025, more than 40 states have enacted explicit statutes criminalizing female genital mutilation (FGM), often as standalone offenses or integrated into child abuse and endangerment laws. [33] Early legislation emerged in the mid-1990s, with Texas passing the first state ban in 1995, followed by New York in 1996. [34] These laws typically prohibit the knowing performance of FGM on minors, with some extending protections to adults. [33]

State laws exhibit significant variations in scope and penalties. For instance, Minnesota's statute includes a "vacation cutting" provision that criminalizes transporting a minor out of state or abroad for FGM, addressing circumvention via international travel. [33] Penalties range from misdemeanors for lesser procedures to felonies carrying sentences of up to life imprisonment in cases involving severe harm, death, or repeat offenses, as seen in statutes like California's, which escalates based on injury extent. [33] Several states hold parents or guardians liable alongside performers, enhancing deterrence. [34]

Approximately nine states lack dedicated FGM prohibitions, depending instead on general assault, battery, or child maltreatment statutes, which may not adequately address the procedure's non-therapeutic nature or cultural drivers. [34] Equality Now's assessments highlight these gaps as weakening enforcement, as generic laws often fail to signal zero tolerance for FGM specifically, potentially allowing persistence through prosecutorial reluctance or evidentiary challenges in culturally insular communities. [33] Comprehensive bans correlate with higher reporting incentives and specialized training for officials, underscoring the need for uniform specificity to close loopholes. [33]

### International Treaties and Influences

The United States has not ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) or the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), despite signing CEDAW in 1980, limiting direct binding obligations under these instruments that condemn female genital mutilation (FGM) via general recommendations such as CEDAW General Recommendation No. 14 and CRC General Comment No. 14. [35] [36] However, U.S. policy aligns with broader UN frameworks, including General Assembly resolutions since 2012 annually calling for global elimination of FGM by 2030, which reinforce domestic legislation like the 1996 Federal Prohibition of Female Genital Mutilation Act by framing the practice as a human rights violation incompatible with non-discrimination and child protection norms. [37] [38]

UNHCR guidelines treat FGM as persecution under the 1951 Refugee Convention, to which the U.S. acceded via the 1968 Protocol, enabling asylum claims for those fearing the procedure; the 2002 Guidelines on Gender-Related Persecution Within the Context of Article 1A(2) explicitly classify FGM as inflicting grave physical and mental harm qualifying as refugee status, influencing U.S. adjudications under the 1980 Refugee Act where over 100 FGM-related grants occurred by 2015. [39] [40] This recognition prioritizes protection from state-tolerated or familial enforcement of FGM, overriding cultural exemptions in refugee definitions.

U.S. State Department advisories and human rights reports since the 1990s document FGM prevalence in origin countries, tying international condemnation to immigration scrutiny, including inadmissibility under 8 U.S.C. § 1182 for health-related grounds or child exploitation, though explicit visa denials for FGM intent rely on case-specific evidence rather than blanket policy. [3] [41]

The 2003 Maputo Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights, prohibiting FGM under Article 5, exerts indirect influence through African diaspora networks in the U.S., where advocacy groups invoke its standards to challenge community norms and press for federal enforcement, highlighting tensions between treaty-driven universalism and localized multicultural persistence. [42] [43]

Collectively, these instruments bolster U.S. legal prohibitions by establishing FGM as non-negotiable persecution, yet expose enforcement shortfalls where immigration from high-prevalence regions—estimated at 513,000 affected or at-risk girls and women—tests commitments against diplomatic deference to source-country sovereignty. [44] [38]

## Enforcement Mechanisms

### Prosecution Cases

In April 2017, federal authorities charged Jumana Nagarwala, a Detroit-area emergency room physician, with performing female genital mutilation (FGM) on at least six girls aged 6 to 8, marking the first federal prosecution under the 1996 FGM prohibition; the case involved girls from the Dawoodi Bohra Muslim community, with allegations of a network performing procedures disguised as medical exams. [45] [46] Nagarwala and associates faced additional counts of conspiracy and aiding, but in November 2018, a federal judge dismissed the core FGM charges, ruling the law exceeded Congress's Commerce Clause authority as it regulated intrastate activity without sufficient interstate nexus. [47] Remaining charges were dismissed in September 2021 after legislative amendments failed to revive the case, though the proceedings established judicial rejection of religious exemptions, as defendants' claims of a non-mutilative "khatna" rite did not alter the procedures' classification as criminal harm. [48]

Earlier, in 2006, Khalid Adem became the first individual convicted in the U.S. for FGM-related offenses, sentenced in Georgia state court to 10 years for aggravated battery and cruelty to children after cutting his 2-year-old daughter's genitals with a razor blade; Adem, an Ethiopian national, was deported in November 2024 following his release. [49] This state-level case predated federal efforts but highlighted prosecutorial reliance on general child abuse statutes when FGM-specific charges were unavailable.

In January 2021, Zahra Badri was federally indicted in Texas for transporting a minor across state lines to Minnesota for FGM, under the Mann Act as amended to cover such acts; the case underscored enforcement against facilitators but proceeded without public disclosure of a conviction or trial outcome by 2025. [6]

Federal prosecutions remain infrequent, with no successful convictions under the post-2020 amended FGM statute by October 2025, despite ICE and HSI awareness campaigns; this scarcity, amid estimates of hundreds of annual U.S. cases, suggests limited deterrent impact, as evidentiary hurdles like victim reluctance and community secrecy persist, though precedents affirm FGM's criminality irrespective of cultural or religious rationales. [7] [50]

### Investigative Challenges

Underreporting of female genital mutilation (FGM) in the United States stems primarily from victims' reluctance to disclose due to familial coercion, fear of reprisal, and ingrained cultural normalization within practicing communities, resulting in few cases surfacing through official channels. [51] The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated in 2016 that approximately 513,000 women and girls were either living with or at risk of FGM, a figure derived from demographic and immigration data rather than confirmed incidents, underscoring the gap between potential prevalence and documented reports. [52] Health professionals often fail to detect FGM during routine examinations absent specific suspicion, as standardized gynecological protocols do not mandate screening for such procedures, allowing many cases to remain hidden. [53]

Evidentiary barriers further complicate investigations, as FGM is typically performed clandestinely—either domestically in private settings or abroad during family visits—leaving scant forensic traces by the time authorities might intervene. [51] The practice's increasing "medicalization," whereby licensed health workers conduct procedures under the guise of clinical intervention, evades traditional child abuse detection frameworks, as such acts may mimic legitimate medical care and lack overt signs of coercion upon later review. [54] This shift, observed globally and applicable to U.S. diaspora contexts, demands specialized expertise to differentiate harmful cuttings from permissible treatments, yet federal data collection remains fragmented, hindering pattern recognition for prosecutions. [55]

Institutional hesitancy exacerbates these issues, particularly in jurisdictions with dense immigrant populations from FGM-prevalent regions, where law enforcement and social services prioritize community outreach over intrusive probes to avoid alienating groups amid cultural sensitivity mandates. [10] U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) assessments of federal efforts reveal coordination shortfalls with local agencies, including inadequate training on navigating cultural barriers without compromising enforcement, leading to underutilized reporting mechanisms. [51] Department of Justice (DOJ) reports under the STOP FGM Act emphasize the need for enhanced stakeholder education in these communities, implicitly acknowledging that deference to multicultural norms can delay or deter investigations despite federal prohibitions. [56]

### Asylum Protections

In 1996, the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) established a key precedent in Matter of Kasinga, granting asylum to Fauziya Kasinga, a 17-year-old girl from Togo who fled her country to avoid female genital mutilation (FGM) arranged by her family as part of a tribal tradition. [57] The BIA ruled that FGM constitutes persecution on account of membership in a particular social group—specifically, young women of her ethnic group who have not undergone the procedure and oppose it—provided the applicant demonstrates a well-founded fear of the practice. [58] This decision marked the first formal recognition by U.S. immigration authorities that fear of FGM qualifies as a basis for asylum under the Immigration and Nationality Act, emphasizing the procedure's severe physical and psychological harm as meeting the persecution threshold. [59]

Subsequent BIA and federal court rulings have upheld FGM-related asylum eligibility for applicants, particularly uncircumcised girls and women from countries where the practice is prevalent and enforced through family, community, or state mechanisms. [60] U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) adjudicators evaluate claims based on evidence of past persecution or a well-founded fear of future FGM, often requiring documentation of cultural prevalence, family intent, and lack of internal relocation options within the home country. [61] Successful grants typically involve minors at imminent risk, with precedents prioritizing protection for those who can show the procedure's inevitability absent U.S. intervention. [62]

Limitations persist in FGM asylum adjudications, particularly for adult applicants who have already undergone the procedure, as the BIA has held in Matter of A-T-, 24 I&N Dec. 296 (BIA 2007), that FGM is generally a one-time harm, precluding claims based solely on fear of repetition unless accompanied by risks to female children or other ongoing persecution. [63] Denials are common for unsubstantiated claims lacking corroborating evidence, such as country condition reports or affidavits from family members, and for cases where applicants fail to establish nexus to a protected ground beyond general cultural practices. [64] While exact figures on FGM-specific grants remain confidential and limited, available data indicate relatively few approvals compared to broader asylum categories, reflecting stringent evidentiary standards and the one-time nature of the harm. [65]

## Health Consequences

### Physical Harms

Female genital mutilation (FGM) encompasses procedures that cause immediate physical trauma, including severe pain, excessive bleeding, and hemorrhage leading to shock, as classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) into types I, II, and III. Type I (clitoridectomy) involves partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and/or prepuce, resulting in acute tissue damage and risk of infection from unsterile instruments. Type II (excision) extends removal to the labia minora, with or without partial or total excision of the labia majora, amplifying blood loss and open wound exposure. These harms arise from cutting healthy genital tissue without anesthesia or medical oversight, often using rudimentary tools. [25] [66]

Type III (infibulation) inflicts the most severe procedural damage by narrowing the vaginal orifice through cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without clitoridectomy, frequently causing urinary retention, genital ulcers, and delayed wound healing due to stitched or scarred tissue. Immediate complications include sepsis, tetanus, and abscess formation from bacterial entry in non-sterile conditions, as evidenced by clinical observations of post-procedural infections. In the United States, where FGM persists among certain immigrant groups, these risks are exacerbated in clandestine home settings lacking hygiene protocols, leading to documented cases of severe infections requiring hospitalization. [25] [66] [2]

Longitudinal health data refute purported hygienic benefits of FGM, revealing instead heightened vulnerability to recurrent urinary tract infections and chronic pelvic inflammation attributable to altered anatomy and scarring. Studies tracking affected women demonstrate no reduction in genital infections and elevated complication rates compared to uncircumcised peers, underscoring the procedure's net physical detriment without empirical support for preventive claims. In U.S. clinical contexts, immigrant patients present with sepsis and wound dehiscence linked to Type III procedures performed domestically or abroad, per hospital management reports. [66] [67] [68]

### Long-Term Medical Outcomes

Women affected by female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) in the United States face chronic reproductive health challenges, including increased requirements for cesarean sections and heightened risks of infertility, with severity linked to procedure type. Type III FGM/C (infibulation), prevalent among immigrants from countries like Somalia and Sudan, causes extensive scarring that obstructs vaginal delivery, elevating cesarean delivery odds by approximately 55% according to systematic reviews of obstetric outcomes applicable to affected U.S. populations. [69] The 2021 Women's Health Needs Study (WHNS), surveying communities with high FGM/C prevalence, found affected women reported significantly more childbirth-related health concerns compared to unaffected peers. [70] Among Somali women in the U.S., FGM/C correlates with adverse reproductive sequelae, including complications from scarred tissue impeding natural labor. [29]

Long-term urogenital issues persist, such as vesicovaginal and rectovaginal fistulas resulting from prolonged obstructed labor, alongside keloid formation from unhealed excision sites, particularly in Types II and III. These conditions contribute to recurrent urinary tract infections and chronic pelvic pain, documented in U.S. healthcare encounters among immigrant women from FGM/C-practicing regions. [4] Sexual dysfunction, including dyspareunia, reduced arousal, and lubrication deficits, is prevalent; WHNS data indicate affected U.S. women experience more sexual health problems, causally tied to clitoral removal and vaginal narrowing in higher-type procedures. [70]

Mental health sequelae include elevated rates of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depressive symptoms, with U.S. studies linking FGM/C trauma to memory impairments and psychological distress among survivors. [71] WHNS participants with FGM/C were more likely to report persistent sadness, reflecting ongoing emotional impacts. [70] While comprehensive U.S.-specific emergency room visit data for 2016–2024 remains limited due to underreporting, inpatient analyses show FGM/C patients require interventions for genital complications, underscoring the procedure's enduring toll. [72] Causal mechanisms stem from anatomical disruption and associated trauma, independent of confounding social factors in migrant contexts. [2]

## Cultural and Ideological Drivers

### Justifications in Practicing Communities

In Somali immigrant communities in cities like Minneapolis, female genital mutilation (FGM) is frequently justified as a mechanism to regulate female sexuality by diminishing sexual desire and deterring premarital or extramarital relations. [73] Participants in a phenomenological study of Somalian women residing in Minneapolis described the procedure as removing the clitoris to prevent "uncontrolled sexual behavior and prostitution," thereby promoting fidelity and safeguarding family reputation within tightly knit enclaves. [73]

The practice is also tied to enhancing marriage eligibility, with uncircumcised girls reportedly facing rejection by potential suitors and social ostracism, as it is perceived as a prerequisite for acceptable unions and securing bride prices that uphold family status. [73] This rationale persists through intergenerational transmission, where mothers view FGM as essential preparation for adulthood and integration into community norms, even amid U.S. legal prohibitions enacted in 1996. [4] Empirical evidence of continuation includes reports of families arranging "vacation cutting" abroad or seeking clandestine local performers to evade detection. [4]

Variations in form, such as the "Sunna" type involving partial clitoral excision, are defended in these groups as milder alternatives that align with cultural expectations without the severity of infibulation, though community members frame it as a non-harmful rite comparable to male circumcision. [73] Surveys of African immigrant women in the U.S., including Somalis, reveal approval rates for some form of the practice ranging from 60-80% in certain subgroups, reflecting entrenched social pressures despite exposure to American laws and education. [29] These attitudes underscore the role of enclave dynamics in sustaining the custom for perceived preservation of honor and eligibility. [74]

### Religious and Traditional Rationales

In certain Muslim communities practicing female genital mutilation (FGM), the procedure is rationalized through interpretations of hadith collections rather than explicit Quranic mandates, with prevalence entrenched across more than 30 countries primarily in Africa and the Middle East where Sunni Islam predominates. [75] Specific narrations, such as those in Sunan Abi Dawud attributed to Umm Atiyyah describing a form of female cutting as recommended (mustahabb), are invoked by proponents to frame FGM as a sunnah practice promoting ritual purity and restraint of sexual desire. [76] These interpretations persist despite classifications of the hadiths as weak (da'if) by many Islamic jurists and the absence of any health-related empirical foundation, prioritizing communal tradition over verifiable causal evidence of benefits. [77]

Among U.S. diaspora populations from FGM-prevalent regions like Somalia and Sudan, community elders and some religious figures endorse the practice as a purifying rite (khitan al-banat) aligned with prophetic tradition, countering broader Islamic scholarly consensus that rejects it as non-obligatory and potentially harmful. [78] [79] In these unassimilated enclaves, such rationales emphasize spiritual cleanliness and fidelity, overriding empirical data showing no physiological advantages and associating the rite with pre-Islamic tribal customs syncretized into local Islamic observance. [80] Traditional tribal justifications, evident in African immigrant groups, portray FGM as an initiatory passage conferring social legitimacy and averting promiscuity, with continuity maintained through elder authority despite migration. [81] This adherence reflects causal primacy of inherited norms over host-country evidence, as documented in studies of post-migration persistence. [82]

## Debates and Criticisms

### Medical Professional Positions

In 2010, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) issued a policy statement suggesting that a "ritual genital nick" of minimal extent might serve as a culturally sensitive compromise to discourage more severe forms of female genital cutting among immigrant families, framing it as a potential harm-reduction strategy. [83] This position drew immediate backlash from critics, including medical ethicists and advocacy groups, who argued it undermined opposition to any non-therapeutic genital alteration and risked normalizing harm under the guise of cultural accommodation. [84] Within weeks, the AAP retracted the suggestion, clarifying that it did not endorse any form of female genital cutting and reaffirming its opposition to procedures lacking medical justification. [83]

Subsequent AAP guidance, including a 2020 clinical report, classifies female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) as medically unnecessary and harmful, emphasizing the need for pediatricians to educate against it and report suspected cases as child abuse. [68] The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) maintains a firm stance against all forms of FGM/C, defining it as procedures involving partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury for non-medical reasons, with no health benefits and significant risks including infection, hemorrhage, and psychological trauma. [85] ACOG explicitly opposes medicalization or accommodation, supporting federal and state laws criminalizing the practice and urging providers to advocate for prevention. [86]

The American Medical Association (AMA) condemns FGM/C as a form of child sexual abuse and gender-based violence, rejecting any medical role in its performance and calling for enhanced education, research, and enforcement to eradicate it. [87] In the 2020s, these organizations have endorsed legislative efforts like the STOP FGM Act expansions, aligning with empirical evidence from systematic reviews showing that even Type IV procedures (e.g., pricking or nicking) carry risks of immediate complications such as pain, bleeding, and infection, alongside long-term issues like scarring and sexual dysfunction. [67] [25] Internal debates within pediatrics and obstetrics have centered on rejecting harm-reduction rationales, with consensus that no level of cutting yields benefits outweighing harms, prioritizing evidence-based opposition over cultural relativism. [54]

### Multicultural Relativism Critiques

Critiques of multicultural relativism argue that elevating cultural traditions above assessments of harm undermines fundamental protections for children, allowing practices like female genital mutilation (FGM) to persist under the pretext of tolerance. Proponents of relativism often frame FGM as a private cultural rite, such as informal "cutting ceremonies" conducted in immigrant enclaves, thereby excusing non-consensual procedures on minors as beyond external judgment. [88] [89] This approach fails on first-principles grounds by subordinating observable physical and psychological damage to girls—evidenced by immediate risks of hemorrhage and infection—to unexamined communal norms, effectively normalizing irreversible bodily alteration without the child's agency. [90] [91]

Empirical data counters relativist tolerance by demonstrating that greater integration into host societies correlates with reduced FGM prevalence and support. Post-migration studies of communities from high-prevalence countries, such as Somalia and Sudan, show significant declines in endorsement of FGM among immigrants in Western settings, with second-generation individuals in integrated environments exhibiting lower continuation rates compared to those in isolated enclaves. [92] [82] For instance, surveys of Somali refugees in the United States reveal shifting attitudes away from the practice as exposure to legal norms and education increases, suggesting assimilation disrupts transmission rather than cultural preservation sustaining it. [93] This pattern holds across diaspora analyses, where enclave isolation perpetuates FGM at rates mirroring origin countries, while dispersal fosters abandonment. [16]

Relativism's claim of cultural equivalence debunks under scrutiny, as no parental "right" extends to mutilating minors, paralleling the historical rejection of other traditions like Chinese foot-binding or Indian sati, which were prohibited despite entrenched rationales of honor and purity. [88] [89] These precedents affirm that universal thresholds of harm—rooted in child autonomy and bodily integrity—override relativist defenses, with FGM similarly lacking consent or medical necessity. [90] Sources advancing relativism, often from anthropological circles, exhibit selective application by condemning equivalent male practices less rigorously, revealing inconsistency rather than principled neutrality. [89]

### Enforcement and Assimilation Debates

Debates surrounding the enforcement of anti-female genital mutilation (FGM) laws in the United States center on the tension between rigorous legal application and arguments for cultural accommodation. Proponents of strict enforcement, including child protection advocates and organizations such as the AHA Foundation, contend that consistent prosecution under the federal statute (18 U.S.C. § 116, enacted in 1996) and varying state laws serves as a deterrent, particularly following high-profile cases like the 2017 federal indictment of physician Jumana Nagarwala in Michigan—the first such prosecution—which exposed a network performing procedures on minor girls across multiple states. [94] Despite an estimated at-risk population exceeding 500,000 girls and women from high-prevalence immigrant communities, the paucity of documented cases post-2017 suggests potential deterrence effects from heightened awareness and legal risks, though underreporting remains a challenge. [2] Critics of leniency, including some legal scholars, highlight risks in jurisdictions with "sanctuary" policies or prosecutorial discretion favoring non-enforcement, arguing these undermine child safeguarding by signaling tolerance for cultural exceptions. [7]

In contrast, certain immigrant rights groups and multicultural relativists advocate for de-emphasizing criminal enforcement in favor of community-led education to avoid stigmatizing ethnic enclaves, positing that heavy-handed approaches may drive the practice underground without addressing root beliefs. [89] However, empirical evidence indicates voluntary abandonment of FGM in U.S. immigrant communities is rare absent sustained external pressure, with studies of diaspora groups showing persistent support or covert continuation without legal mandates and social stigma. [95] Child protection perspectives prioritize universal application of U.S. law over relativist exemptions, noting that accommodations erode assimilation imperatives and expose minors to irreversible harm, as cultural justifications lack empirical health benefits and conflict with constitutional protections for children. [96]

Assimilation debates underscore the need for immigration vetting to identify risks from origin countries with FGM prevalence exceeding 70% (e.g., Somalia, Guinea), incorporating explicit pledges against the practice and mandatory orientation on prohibitions. [12] U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) promotes zero-tolerance messaging in refugee and visa processes, yet lacks formal risk-based screening, prompting calls from enforcement advocates for enhanced measures to condition entry or residency on verifiable rejection of FGM intent. [97] Without such imperatives, assimilation falters, as evidenced by ongoing clandestine networks, reinforcing arguments that education alone insufficiently supplants entrenched norms without legal compulsion and cultural integration requirements. [98] DOJ reports affirm active federal investigations but few convictions, attributing gaps to evidentiary hurdles rather than policy leniency, though advocates urge broader prosecutorial vigor to signal unequivocal intolerance. [10]

## Prevention Strategies

### Government and Law Enforcement Efforts

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) have coordinated multi-agency campaigns to combat female genital mutilation (FGM), emphasizing awareness and prevention at entry points. In 2025, ICE's Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) partnered with the Federal Bureau of Investigation's (FBI) International Human Rights Unit (IHRU) and the Department of Justice's (DOJ) Human Rights and Special Prosecutions Section (HRSP) for initiatives like Operation Limelight USA, which conducts outreach at U.S. international airports to inform travelers of FGM's health, criminal, and immigration consequences. [32] ICE's Human Rights Violators and War Crimes Center (HRVWCC) leads investigations into FGM perpetrators, focusing on cases involving the transport of minors abroad for the procedure, prohibited under 18 U.S.C. § 116. [32] The FBI provides specialized training to law enforcement agents on recognizing and investigating FGM as a federal crime. [32]

Implementation of the STOP FGM Act of 2020 has directed federal resources toward victim support and targeted awareness. The DOJ's Office for Victims of Crime (OVC) allocated over $5 million in grants during fiscal years 2020 and 2021 for survivor services and prevention programs, including the Minnesota Female Genital Mutilation Prevention and Outreach Project, which engages stakeholders for policy recommendations and community interventions. [99] Additional funding supported awareness efforts in high-risk states such as Minnesota, New York, California, Texas, Virginia, and Washington, through trainings, social media campaigns, and community dialogues. [10] These initiatives promote public reporting via ICE tip lines (866-347-2423) and online forms to facilitate investigations. [32]

Federal outcomes reveal persistent enforcement challenges despite heightened post-2020 activities. Annual STOP FGM Act reports document only one federal indictment in 2021—for transporting a minor to Kenya for FGM—amid estimates of over 500,000 at-risk women and girls in the U.S. [99] No additional convictions or investigation tallies were detailed in subsequent reports, underscoring low prosecution rates relative to prevalence data from sources like the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which indicate gaps in translating awareness into actionable cases. [10] [52] This disparity suggests that while tip submissions and trainings have expanded, cultural barriers and evidentiary hurdles limit deterrence effectiveness. [10]

### Community Education Initiatives

Non-governmental organizations in the United States have implemented community-based education programs targeting immigrant groups from FGM-practicing regions, such as Somali and Ethiopian communities, to promote awareness of health risks and cultural alternatives to the practice. The International Institute of Minnesota, for instance, organized events in August 2024 focused on raising awareness and prevention among Somali and Ethiopian women, emphasizing community dialogue and support services. [100] Similarly, Safe Hands for Girls, a survivor-led organization based in Atlanta, Georgia, conducts workshops and advocacy programs to educate communities on the harms of FGM and encourage abandonment through survivor testimonies and discussions of non-cutting rites of passage.

School-based initiatives in states with significant at-risk populations, like Minnesota, incorporate FGM education into health curricula to inform students about legal prohibitions and health consequences, often featuring survivor stories to foster understanding. Community recommendations in Minnesota advocate for age-appropriate inclusion of FGM topics in school health education, aiming to equip youth with knowledge of risks and reporting mechanisms. [74] The U.S. End FGM/C Network supports such efforts through outreach to education professionals, providing resources on identification and prevention roles. [101]

Evaluations of these non-coercive programs reveal mixed evidence on behavior change, with participant surveys indicating shifts toward negative attitudes regarding FGM, yet persistence of the practice in some communities absent stronger enforcement measures. [102] For example, community workshops have demonstrated increased knowledge and opposition among attendees, but long-term prevalence reduction requires sustained engagement beyond education alone. [103]
