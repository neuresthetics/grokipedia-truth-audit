# Female genital mutilation in Nigeria

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in Nigeria refers to the culturally entrenched procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia, or other injury thereto, performed on girls and women for non-medical reasons, resulting in Nigeria hosting the world's highest absolute number of affected individuals, estimated at around 20 million. The practice predominates among ethnic groups like the Yoruba, Igbo, and certain Hausa subgroups, primarily in southern and central states such as Osun, Ebonyi, and Ekiti, where prevalence rates among women aged 15-49 exceed 50% in some areas. [1] [2] [3]

Common forms include Type I (clitoridectomy, removing the clitoral prepuce and/or glans) and Type II (excision, removing the clitoris and labia minora, sometimes with labia majora trimming), with rarer instances of Type IV variants like symbolic nicking or scraping; these are typically conducted by traditional practitioners on infants or girls under 15 using unsterilized tools, leading to immediate risks of hemorrhage, infection, and shock, alongside long-term complications such as chronic pain, urinary and menstrual disorders, sexual dysfunction, and obstructed labor increasing maternal and neonatal mortality. [4] [5] [6] Rooted in beliefs about preserving virginity, enhancing marriageability, and enforcing social norms, FGM persists despite empirical evidence of zero health benefits and demonstrable causal harm from anatomical disruption and psychological trauma. [7] [8]

Federally criminalized in 2015 under the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act with penalties up to four years imprisonment, the ban's enforcement is uneven due to weak state-level adoption, prosecutorial reluctance, and community resistance, contributing to stalled progress toward elimination; national prevalence among adult women has declined from 25% in the early 2000s to about 20% per recent surveys, yet rates among girls aged 0-14 show stagnation or slight increases in rural, low-wealth areas, underscoring the need for intensified education, survivor-led advocacy, and UNFPA-UNICEF joint programs targeting root cultural drivers. [9] [10] [1]

## Historical Background

### Origins and Evolution

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in Nigeria originated in pre-colonial ethnic traditions among major groups such as the Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa-Fulani, Ijaw, and Kanuri, predating the influence of Islam and Christianity. These practices were embedded in animist cultural frameworks as rites of passage marking transition to womanhood, often performed by traditional healers or community elders using rudimentary tools for symbolic scarification or excision. Oral traditions and ethnographic accounts describe early forms tied to tribal identity and social maturation, without evidence of external diffusion from ancient Egyptian or other continental practices specific to Nigeria. [11]

Over time, FGM evolved from primarily ritualistic modifications to mechanisms for regulating female sexuality, with communities invoking beliefs in preserving premarital chastity and postmarital fidelity to justify continuation. This shift reflects adaptations within kinship systems amid internal migrations and trade networks that facilitated inter-ethnic exchange of customs across Nigeria's regions, embedding the practice in family lineages rather than centralized institutions. Historical narratives contain no verifiable endorsements of health, fertility, or hygienic benefits, attributing persistence instead to normative enforcement for social conformity. [11]

The absence of doctrinal causation is evident in FGM's uniform occurrence across animist, Muslim, and Christian populations, with no scriptural imperatives in Abrahamic faiths linking it to piety or salvation; attributions to religion often stem from conflation with cultural syncretism rather than causal origins. Colonial-era exposures to Western norms introduced sporadic resistance, particularly from Christian missionaries in the early 20th century, yet the practice endured through autonomous ethnic continuity, adapting forms (e.g., from infibulation to clitoridectomy) without altering core sociocultural rationales. [11]

### Pre-Colonial Practices and Colonial Influence

In pre-colonial Nigerian societies, particularly among ethnic groups such as the Yoruba, Igbo, and communities in the southeast and southwest, female genital mutilation served as a rite of passage embedded in initiation ceremonies marking the transition to womanhood. [12] These practices reinforced clan identity and marriage eligibility, with families voluntarily arranging the procedures to ensure daughters' perceived fidelity, chastity, and social integration, often justified by beliefs in controlling female sexuality and preventing promiscuity. [12] Ethnographic evidence from African contexts, including Nigeria, indicates that such rituals predated external influences, originating from ancient traditions possibly linked to trade routes and multi-ethnic customs, where the cutting symbolized purity and adulthood readiness without coercive state imposition but through kinship and communal enforcement. [12]

During the British colonial period from 1914 to 1960, administrators generally tolerated female genital mutilation as an entrenched cultural custom, conducting rare interventions amid indirect rule policies that prioritized local traditions to maintain stability. [13] Early 20th-century missionary and colonial medical critiques, which highlighted health risks and labeled the practice barbaric, faced dismissal by Nigerian communities as cultural imperialism, prompting authorities to retreat from prohibition efforts due to widespread local resistance. [13] Christian missions across Africa, including in Nigeria, campaigned against the practice in the early 1900s, yet empirical data show no significant reduction in prevalence during this era, as social norms and family-driven enforcement persisted unchecked by limited colonial penetration into rural areas. [14]

The continuity of these practices into the post-independence era stemmed from entrenched social mechanisms rather than colonial imposition, with weak central authority failing to disrupt voluntary familial participation and communal validation that sustained the rituals across generations. [13] No measurable decline occurred until the late 20th century, underscoring the causal primacy of internal cultural persistence over external administrative influences. [13]

## Prevalence and Demographic Patterns

### Current National and Regional Statistics

Nigeria accounts for approximately 20 million women and girls living with the effects of female genital mutilation, representing about 10 percent of the global total. [15] The 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) reported a national prevalence of 20 percent among women aged 15-49. [1] Among girls aged 0-14, prevalence rose to 19.2 percent in recent estimates, up from 16.9 percent in 2013, indicating stalled progress and potential reversal in younger cohorts. [16]

Prevalence exhibits sharp regional disparities, with the highest rates concentrated in southern zones: South West (around 54 percent), South East (around 35 percent), and South South (around 30 percent) per the 2018 NDHS. [17] Specific states like Osun (77 percent) and Ekiti (74 percent) report among the highest state-level figures for women 15-49. [18] Northern zones show much lower rates, typically under 5 percent in North West and North East, though urban-rural differences are narrowing as rural prevalence edges upward in some areas.

Cohort analysis reveals a historical decline, with prevalence dropping from 56.3 percent among women born in 1959-1963 to 25.5 percent among those born in 1994-1998, based on 2018 NDHS data. [19] However, the recent increase among infants and young girls suggests this downward trend may have plateaued, with ongoing risks concentrated in high-prevalence southern and rural populations. [16]

### Variations by Ethnicity, Age, and Generation

Female genital mutilation (FGM) prevalence and forms in Nigeria exhibit marked ethnic variations, with higher rates and more invasive procedures among southern groups such as the Yoruba and Igbo, where Type I (clitoridectomy) and Type II (excision) predominate, compared to lower prevalence and less severe practices like Type Ia (sunna, involving pricking or minimal removal) among northern Hausa-Fulani communities. [20] [8] These patterns persist despite ethnic migrations, with urban Yoruba and Igbo maintaining traditions through community networks, while Hausa practices correlate with Islamic interpretations emphasizing minimal intervention. [18] The practice transcends religious affiliations, occurring among Muslims, Christians, and adherents of traditional animist beliefs across ethnic lines, driven by shared cultural norms rather than doctrinal mandates. [21]

Age at cutting typically peaks between infancy and 15 years, with over 80% of cases in the 0-14 age group per 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) data, though urban shifts toward medicalized procedures by trained practitioners have increased infant cuttings (under 1 year) to evade scrutiny and reduce risks. [17] [16] Cohort analyses from NDHS and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) reveal a generational decline, from 56.3% prevalence in the 1959-1963 birth cohort to 25.5% in 1994-1998 cohorts, stabilizing at around 8-10% for post-1998 groups, yet recent MICS data indicate a reversal with prevalence among girls aged 0-14 rising from 16.9% in 2013 to 19.2% by 2017, attributed to intensified family enforcement amid awareness campaigns. [19] [3] [16]

Education levels inversely correlate with FGM continuation, with NDHS analyses showing women with secondary or higher education exhibiting 40-50% lower odds of subjecting daughters to the practice compared to those with no education, mediated by enhanced critical reasoning and exposure to anti-FGM messaging. [22] [23] Urbanization reinforces this through access to formal schooling and media, reducing prevalence by up to 30% in cities versus rural areas, though low socioeconomic status groups sustain transmission via intergenerational family pressures that prioritize conformity over individual literacy gains. [17] [8] In such contexts, even educated mothers in tight-knit ethnic enclaves face overrides from maternal kin, underscoring causal primacy of social enforcement over personal attainment alone. [24]

## Cultural and Social Drivers

### Traditional Justifications and Beliefs

Practitioners and communities in Nigeria often justify female genital mutilation (FGM) as a means to curb female promiscuity and ensure premarital virginity or marital fidelity, believing it reduces sexual desire and makes women more suitable wives. [18] [4] Uncut girls face social ostracism, labeled as unclean or promiscuous, which diminishes their marriage prospects and ties family honor to compliance with the practice. [18] Additional rationales include purported enhancements to fertility, hygiene, and social status, with some viewing FGM as essential to fulfilling womanhood or ritual purity. [18] [4]

These beliefs lack religious mandate, as neither the Quran nor the Bible prescribes FGM, and major Islamic and Christian doctrines remain silent on it, with some clerics issuing fatwas against the practice. [4] [18] Despite this, syncretism occurs in communities where FGM is integrated into Islamic or Christian rites, particularly among traditionalists (39% believing it religiously required) and Muslims (34%), though only 17% of women aged 15-49 overall endorse it as such, reflecting cultural rather than doctrinal origins in pre-colonial animist purity rituals. [18]

Empirical studies contradict these claims, finding no association between FGM and delayed sexual debut, preserved virginity, or increased marital fidelity, thus debunking causal links to reduced promiscuity. [25] Similarly, assertions of hygiene or fertility benefits hold no validation, as FGM damages healthy tissue without conferring advantages and instead introduces risks like infections and reproductive complications. [4] The practice persists through entrenched gender norms, where mothers often enforce it on daughters to secure conformity and avoid stigma, prioritizing social cohesion over verifiable outcomes. [18]

### Integration into Social Structures and Family Dynamics

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in Nigeria is deeply embedded within kinship systems as a rite of passage marking the transition to womanhood, conferring social status and eligibility for marriage, which in turn influences bride price negotiations and family economic prospects. In communities where the practice persists, such as among certain Yoruba, Igbo, and Hausa-Fulani groups, undergoing FGM signals adherence to cultural norms that ensure a girl's integration into extended family networks and potential inheritance rights, particularly in patrilineal structures where family honor is tied to female chastity. Non-compliance can lead to social ostracism, reduced marriage prospects, and economic isolation for families, as uncircumcised girls are often viewed as unmarriageable, thereby diminishing bride wealth and reinforcing communal enforcement through self-perpetuating social costs rather than isolated coercion. [11]

Intra-family transmission of FGM is predominantly driven by female relatives, with mothers and grandmothers serving as primary decision-makers, motivated by intergenerational norms and peer pressure within kin groups to uphold family reputation over paternal input, which remains secondary and often passive. Surveys indicate that while overall support has declined, a significant portion of mothers in high-prevalence areas continue the practice, reflecting internalized social expectations where deviation risks familial disapproval and exclusion from support networks. This dynamic underscores a causal loop wherein mothers, having themselves undergone FGM, replicate it to secure their daughters' future social and economic viability within the family unit, prioritizing collective conformity. [11] [26]

Urban migration has disrupted traditional FGM implementation but prompted adaptations, including less invasive "symbolic" forms or medicalized procedures to align with modern settings while maintaining extended family approval. Among educated urban elites, persistence rates hover around 30-40%, exemplified by 37.2% prevalence among women with secondary education, largely due to influence from rural-extended kin who exert pressure to preserve cultural continuity and avoid perceived familial dishonor. This resilience highlights how extended family networks sustain the practice amid socioeconomic shifts, imposing relational costs on non-adherents through withheld social capital and marriage alliances. [11] [27]

## Types and Implementation

### WHO Classifications Prevalent in Nigeria

The World Health Organization classifies female genital mutilation (FGM) into four types based on the extent of tissue removal or damage: Type I involves partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and/or prepuce (clitoridectomy); Type II entails partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora (excision); Type III comprises narrowing of the vaginal opening through cutting and repositioning of the labia, with or without clitoral excision (infibulation); and Type IV encompasses all other non-medical procedures, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, or cauterizing the genitalia. [4] In Nigeria, where approximately 20% of women aged 15-49 have undergone FGM as of 2018, Type II is the most prevalent form, affecting 41% of those cut, followed by Type I at 10% and Type III at 6%. [20] [28]

Type I and Type II predominate in southern regions, such as the South East and South West, where prevalence rates reach 35% and 30%, respectively, reflecting practices among ethnic groups like the Yoruba and Igbo. [20] Type III, though less common nationally, shows elevated incidence in northern states, comprising a higher share of cases in areas influenced by Hausa-Fulani traditions. [29] Type IV variants, accounting for the remainder of cases, include practices like angurya cuts (scraping of vaginal walls with a gishiri knife) and gishiri cuts, which together represent significant portions—up to 40% and 13%, respectively—in affected northern communities, often performed postnatally or for perceived therapeutic reasons and increasingly medicalized by health workers. [20]

Empirical data indicate severity gradients, with Type II and III associated with elevated complication risks compared to Type I; for instance, more extensive excisions correlate with 2-3 times higher incidence of immediate issues like hemorrhage and infection due to greater tissue trauma and blood loss potential. [4] [2] These patterns underscore Nigeria's diverse FGM landscape, where less invasive forms like Type I still constitute a minority despite their relative prevalence in certain southern contexts. [20]

### Procedures, Tools, and Practitioners

In Nigeria, traditional female genital mutilation is predominantly carried out by elderly women or designated traditional circumcisers, often in communal ceremonies that mark rites of passage, using rudimentary tools such as unsterilized razor blades, knives, or pieces of broken glass, without anesthesia or antiseptic measures. [30] [31] These practitioners, typically respected community elders, acquire their skills through matrilineal transmission across generations, deriving social status and modest economic incentives—ranging from approximately $5 to $20 per procedure—from families seeking to uphold cultural norms. [18]

The timing of the procedure varies by ethnic group and region: among some communities like the Yoruba, it occurs in infancy or within the first 7-10 days of life, while in others, such as certain northern groups, it aligns with puberty or pre-marital preparations. [32] Ceremonies may involve singing, feasting, and herbal applications post-cutting to staunch bleeding, reinforcing communal bonds but conducted in non-clinical settings with shared or reused blades. [33]

A partial shift toward medicalization has emerged, particularly in urban southern areas like the Southeast and Southwest, where 8.6% to 20% of procedures are now performed by nurses, midwives, or doctors in clinics or hospitals, employing scalpels, scissors, or other surgical instruments. [18] This trend, declining slightly from 12.7% in 2013 to 8.6% in 2018 per national surveys, is motivated by families' perceptions of greater hygiene and expertise, though instruments are frequently unsterilized, and anesthesia remains uncommon even in these settings. [18] Practitioners in medical roles cite financial gains similar to traditional counterparts, with the practice persisting despite professional ethical codes. [34]

## Health and Psychological Impacts

### Acute and Immediate Complications

Hemorrhage represents a primary acute risk of FGM procedures in Nigeria, occurring in 17% to 62% of cases due to vascular damage from cutting without hemostatic measures, with profuse bleeding documented in local case reports. [35] Severe pain, unmitigated by analgesia, affects over 50% of affected girls immediately post-procedure, as evidenced by surveys in Nigerian communities. [35] Urinary retention arises shortly after from edema, clitoral swelling, or obstructive tissue flaps, impacting up to 60% in comparable African datasets incorporating Nigerian experiences. [35] Shock may ensue from blood loss or acute pain, contributing to immediate morbidity. [35]

Infections, facilitated by unsterilized blades and razors commonly used by traditional practitioners, include sepsis at rates of 1.4% to 7.6% and tetanus at 0.18% to 2.3%, with Nigerian case series reporting fatalities such as tetanus deaths in infants and a septic adult woman. [35] These carry case fatality rates of approximately 1-2% in documented instances, underscoring the causal role of contamination in non-medical settings. [35] Wound healing delays, observed in 0% to 54% of cases per aggregated hospital and community data from Nigeria-inclusive studies, prolong vulnerability to secondary infections. [35] Southeast Nigerian surveys confirm practitioner and community awareness of bleeding and infection as frequent immediate sequelae, though underreporting persists due to home-based procedures. [36]

### Chronic Physical and Reproductive Effects

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in Nigeria leads to chronic physical complications primarily through fibrosis and scarring from tissue excision, resulting in keloid formation and clitoral cysts. These outcomes stem from non-sterile procedures using rudimentary tools, promoting excessive collagen deposition during healing. Nigerian clinical observations document keloid scars as a frequent long-term issue, causing chronic pain, itching, and functional impairment in the genital area. Clitoral neuromas or cysts, arising from buried nerve endings and residual tissue, further contribute to persistent discomfort and require surgical intervention in severe cases. [2]

Chronic urinary tract infections (UTIs) affect women with FGM at elevated rates, linked to urethral distortion and poor hygiene due to scarred, narrowed anatomy. In Nigeria, where Type I and II FGM predominate, anatomical changes impede complete bladder emptying, fostering bacterial retention and recurrent infections. Studies indicate UTI prevalence is significantly higher among circumcised women, with dysuria and vaginal discharge commonly reported as sequelae. Menstrual obstruction occurs particularly in Type II and III procedures, where labial excision or apposition creates vaginal narrowing, leading to hematocolpos, dysmenorrhea, and secondary pelvic infections. [7] [5]

Reproductively, FGM correlates with prolonged labor durations, often 1.5 to 2 times longer in affected Nigerian women, due to inelastic scar tissue resisting cervical dilation and fetal descent. This increases perineal tear risks and neonatal distress from hypoxia during extended parturition. A cross-sectional study at Federal Teaching Hospital Abakaliki found FGM independently raised odds of perineal trauma in laboring women. Obstetric hemorrhage odds ratios of 1.5-2.0 have been associated with FGM in Nigerian cohorts, reflecting vascular damage and coagulopathy from scarred tissues. Vesicovaginal fistulas, resulting from ischemic necrosis during obstructed labor, show 20-30% higher prevalence among Nigerian women with FGM, particularly in northern regions practicing more severe forms. Demographic and Health Survey analyses confirm this fistula-FGM link, attributing it to compounded pelvic floor trauma. [37] [38] [39]

### Sexual Functioning and Mental Health Consequences

Studies in Nigeria have documented impaired sexual functioning among women subjected to female genital mutilation (FGM), including reduced vaginal lubrication and dyspareunia during intercourse. In a 2024 cross-sectional study of 400 women in Ibadan, those with FGM reported significantly lower scores on sexual function indices, encompassing arousal, lubrication, and orgasm domains, compared to uncircumcised controls (p<0.05). [40] Prevalence of pain during intercourse ranged from 40% to 60% in affected cohorts, attributed to scar tissue formation and nerve damage, with overall sexual satisfaction markedly diminished and no supporting data for claims of heightened pleasure or behavioral control. [41] [40]

Mental health sequelae include elevated risks of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety, with systematic reviews indicating odds ratios of 2 to 4 times higher among FGM survivors relative to unaffected women across African populations. [42] [43] A 2023 narrative synthesis highlighted consistent associations with psychological distress, though causality remains influenced by confounding social factors. In Nigerian surveys, FGM correlates with 25-35% increased anxiety prevalence, exacerbated by perceptions of familial betrayal and normalized violence, fostering intergenerational transmission of trauma through perpetuated acceptance of ritual harm. [42] [44] No empirical evidence supports psychological benefits, underscoring the practice's net adverse impact on psyche and intimacy. [43]

## Legal and Policy Responses

### Federal and State Legislation

The federal Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act (VAPP) of 2015 explicitly criminalizes female genital mutilation (FGM), defining it as the excision, infibulation, or mutilation of any part of the labia minora, labia majora, clitoris, or vulva, with penalties including imprisonment for a minimum of four years, a fine of 200,000 naira (approximately $480 USD as of 2015 exchange rates), or both. [45] [20] The Act, signed into law on May 25, 2015, by then-President Goodluck Jonathan, applies directly to the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) and provides a framework for states to adopt, though it is not retroactive and does not punish prior offenses. Complementing this, the Child Rights Act of 2003, domesticated in 24 states and the FCT by 2023, prohibits subjecting children under 18 to harmful traditional practices such as FGM, interpreting it as a violation of rights to health, dignity, and freedom from degrading treatment, with penalties up to five years imprisonment in adopting jurisdictions. [10] [20]

At the state level, only 11 states had enacted specific anti-FGM laws by the end of 2021: Bayelsa, Cross River, Delta, Ebonyi, Edo, Ekiti, Lagos (2007), Osun (2003), Oyo, Ondo, and Rivers, often mirroring federal provisions with imprisonment terms of four to five years. [20] [46] In the remaining states, particularly the 12 northern states operating under Sharia penal codes, FGM lacks explicit prohibition and is rarely addressed as a prosecutable offense, despite its comparatively lower prevalence (under 5% in northern regions per 2018 data), with Sharia courts prioritizing other hudud crimes over such practices. [18] [20]

Nigeria ratified the Maputo Protocol (Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa) on December 16, 2004, obligating states under Article 5 to prohibit all forms of FGM and condemn its practice through legislative and other measures, yet domestication remains partial, with federal laws like VAPP partially fulfilling but not fully integrating the protocol's requirements across all 36 states due to federalism constraints and varying state adoption. [11] [20]

### Implementation Challenges and Case Studies

Despite federal legislation under the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act of 2015 criminalizing female genital mutilation (FGM) and its adoption in multiple states, enforcement has yielded fewer than 10 prosecutions nationwide since enactment, with reports documenting zero convictions through at least 2021. [18] This paucity arises largely from entrenched cultural tolerance, whereby law enforcement authorities frequently classify FGM as an internal family or customary affair, resulting in police hesitation to pursue cases even amid complaints from affected parties or communities. [20] Such reluctance is compounded by inadequate training on anti-FGM provisions and occasional susceptibility to bribery, prioritizing social harmony over legal accountability. [18]

Victim and witness recantation further hampers proceedings, often motivated by apprehension of familial reprisal or communal exclusion, while pervasive underreporting obscures the scale of violations given that cutters and enablers are predominantly kin. [20] Rural locales exacerbate impunity through diminished policing infrastructure and resilient traditional norms, fostering de facto exemption compared to urban settings where greater scrutiny occasionally prompts investigations, though successful adjudications remain exceptional. [18]

Illustrative cases underscore these impediments. In Ebonyi State, civil society monitoring identified at least 10 instances of FGM on schoolgirls in mid-2025 alone, yet subsequent legal follow-through faltered, reflecting persistent gaps between detection and deterrence amid familial pressures. [47] Osun State, with one of Nigeria's highest FGM prevalence rates exceeding 70% among women aged 15-49 as of 2018 surveys, has witnessed community sensitization drives fostering partial resistance, but analogous to broader patterns, these have translated into negligible prosecutions, demonstrating that grassroots engagement aids awareness yet falters without robust institutional backing. [48] In northern regions such as the North East, where prevalence hovers below 10% and often under 1% in select subgroups due to lesser cultural entrenchment, impunity endures through analogous barriers including insecurity and normative deference, sustaining low but unaddressed incidence absent prosecutorial precedents. [18]

## Eradication Efforts and Progress

### Governmental and Policy Initiatives

The Federal Ministry of Women Affairs and Social Development (FMWASD) leads Nigeria's national efforts against female genital mutilation (FGM), culminating in the adoption of the National Policy and Plan of Action for the Elimination of Female Genital Mutilation for 2021–2025, which builds on earlier frameworks to coordinate multisectoral responses. [49] This policy emphasizes integration of FGM prevention into health services, such as antenatal care and maternal health programs, and education curricula to raise awareness among school-aged girls and communities. [50] Complementing these, Nigeria has observed the annual International Day of Zero Tolerance to FGM since its inception on February 6, 2003, initiated by then-First Lady Stella Obasanjo to mobilize public campaigns and policy advocacy nationwide. [51]

At the state level, enforcement of FGM prohibitions under the 2015 Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act has involved targeted drives, including surveillance teams and prosecutions in high-prevalence areas like the South East and South West, though implementation remains inconsistent due to resource constraints. [9] Budget allocations for FGM surveillance and elimination efforts in 2023–2025 have been incorporated into broader gender and health frameworks, but funding constitutes a fraction of overall health expenditures, with persistent reports of under-resourcing limiting monitoring and enforcement capacity. [52] [53]

These initiatives have correlated with prevalence declines among older birth cohorts, dropping from 56.3% in the 1959–1963 cohort to 25.5% in the 1994–1998 cohort per Demographic and Health Survey data, reflecting heightened awareness from policy campaigns. [19] However, recent cohorts show stagnation or slight upticks to 30.2% in 2004–2008, alongside a policy lag in curbing shifts toward infant cuttings, which evade traditional monitoring focused on older girls and underscore gaps between awareness gains and actual incidence reductions. [19] [54]

### Activism, NGOs, and Community Interventions

Non-governmental organizations have played a significant role in combating female genital mutilation (FGM) in Nigeria through community-based education, advocacy, and alternative rite-of-passage programs. The UNFPA-UNICEF Joint Programme on the Elimination of Female Genital Mutilation, active since 2008 and extended into the 2020s, supports local partners in shifting social norms via awareness campaigns and survivor-led initiatives, emphasizing community dialogues to abandon the practice. [55] Local entities like the Civil Resource Development and Documentation Centre (CIRDDOC) have targeted high-prevalence southeastern states, implementing projects such as "Accelerating the Elimination of FGM in Imo and Ebonyi States," which include sensitization workshops, school-based education on FGM harms, and promotion of non-cutting coming-of-age rituals to replace traditional procedures. [56]

These interventions have demonstrated measurable shifts in attitudes and intent. In CIRDDOC's efforts in Imo and Ebonyi, baseline assessments highlighted persistent FGM support tied to cultural persistence, prompting targeted community engagements that aimed to reduce prevalence through local partnerships, though specific quantitative outcomes remain documented primarily in qualitative progress reports. [57] Complementary projects, such as the StopCut initiative in southwestern states like Oyo and Osun, reported significant reductions in intent to practice FGM following quasi-experimental interventions involving education on health risks and legal frameworks; for instance, perceptions of FGM benefits dropped from 52% in control groups to 9.4% in treatment groups, with 92.6% of participants expressing willingness to report violations compared to 54.8% in controls. [58]

Male engagement has emerged as a focused strategy within these NGO efforts. UNFPA and UNICEF have promoted campaigns partnering with men and boys to transform norms, including the 2024 launch of the Male Engagement Alliance, which trains male champions to advocate against FGM through community outreach and norm-shifting dialogues, building on broader joint programme activities to address patriarchal influences without relying on state enforcement. [59] [60]

Critiques of these NGO-led approaches highlight dependencies on foreign funding, which some analyses argue perpetuates a hierarchical dynamic by prioritizing Western-funded education over indigenous knowledge systems, potentially marginalizing local epistemologies. [61] Such efforts have provoked backlash in certain communities, where they are perceived as cultural impositions akin to colonial "civilizing" missions, leading to resistance and even heightened secrecy around FGM practices as a form of cultural preservation. [61] Surveys and decolonial critiques note that this framing can overshadow Nigerian-led voices, fostering stigma that complicates open dialogue and data accuracy. [61]

### Measured Outcomes and Recent Trends

Survey data from the Nigeria Demographic and Health Surveys indicate a modest decline in female genital mutilation (FGM) prevalence among women and girls aged 15-49, from 24.8% in 2013 to 19.5% in 2018, reflecting a roughly 20% relative reduction. [9] However, this progress masks reversals in younger cohorts, with prevalence among girls aged 0-14 rising from 16.9% to 19.2% over the same period, signaling persistent or intensifying practice in early childhood. [16] Attitudes have shifted modestly, with 67% of women and girls aged 15-49 expressing opposition to FGM in 2018, up from lower opposition rates in prior surveys, though approval persists in certain rural and low-education subgroups. [9]

Absolute numbers of affected individuals remain elevated due to Nigeria's rapid population growth, which outpaces prevalence declines; the country hosts an estimated 19.9 million FGM survivors, the highest absolute figure globally. [16] Empirical patterns reveal stronger correlations between reduced FGM rates and socioeconomic factors such as higher maternal education and urban residence—where prevalence drops to 13% versus 24% in rural areas—than with targeted campaigns alone, underscoring education and modernization as primary causal drivers. [9]

In recent years, UNICEF has highlighted stalled eradication efforts, with the uptick in younger girls attributed partly to pandemic-related disruptions like school closures and weakened monitoring, projecting potential additional cases without intensified intervention. [16] Nigeria, like many nations, is off-track for Sustainable Development Goal 5.3 to eliminate FGM by 2030, as global survivor estimates rose 15% to over 230 million by 2024, reflecting insufficient progress amid demographic pressures. [62] Medicalization rates have declined (from 26.9% to 7.1% among 0-14-year-olds), yet the persistence of early-age procedures suggests adaptive shifts rather than abandonment. [9]

## Controversies and Debates

### Cultural Defenses Versus Empirical Harms

Proponents of female genital mutilation (FGM) in Nigeria, particularly community elders in ethnic groups like the Yoruba, Igbo, and Hausa-Fulani, defend the practice as a vital tradition that upholds moral standards by curbing female promiscuity and ensuring chastity for marriage. These arguments frame FGM as a rite of passage fostering social cohesion and cultural identity, with surveys in practicing regions revealing that approximately 20-30% of respondents endorse it for preserving virginity or instilling discipline. [63] [21] However, such defenses lack empirical substantiation, as no controlled studies demonstrate causal reductions in sexual activity or moral lapses attributable to FGM; perceived benefits appear rooted in unverified social norms rather than measurable outcomes. [4]

Cultural relativist claims that FGM's harms are context-dependent overlook objective physiological data, including universal indicators of acute pain and tissue damage during procedures performed without anesthesia. The World Health Organization (WHO) explicitly states that FGM yields no health advantages, with Nigerian cohort studies confirming associations with heightened infertility risks—contradicting assertions of fertility enhancement—and no improvements in genital hygiene, as scarring often exacerbates bacterial infections rather than preventing them. [4] [64] Lifetime complication rates in affected Nigerian populations exceed 10-15%, encompassing urinary tract obstructions, keloid scarring, and dyspareunia, based on cross-sectional analyses of women aged 15-49. [65] [66]

Opposing viewpoints from FGM survivors and health researchers emphasize verifiable net harms, including elevated maternal mortality from obstructed labor, documented in prospective Nigerian hospital data showing cut women 1.3-2 times more likely to require emergency cesareans. [67] Elders' insistence on tradition contrasts with evidence that communities historically abandon injurious customs through internal reckoning, as seen in declining voluntary cessation rates predating formal bans in select Nigerian locales, prioritizing causal evidence of suffering over unsubstantiated cultural imperatives. [19] [68]

### Medicalization and Enforcement Dilemmas

In Nigeria, the medicalization of female genital mutilation (FGM)—where procedures are performed by health professionals such as nurses or midwives—has increased in urban areas due to perceptions of greater safety and hygiene, though overall rates have declined slightly from 12.7% in 2013 to 8.6% of affected women aged 15-49 in 2018. [20] [69] This shift reduces immediate risks like infection and hemorrhage compared to traditional methods, but empirical evidence indicates no elimination of long-term complications, including chronic pain, urinary issues, sexual dysfunction, and increased maternal mortality during childbirth, as these stem from the procedure's inherent tissue damage rather than the performer's training. [70] [71] Medicalization thus entrenches the practice by lending it a veneer of legitimacy through medical authority, complicating eradication efforts, as families view clinic-based FGM as a benign cultural rite rather than harm. [69]

Enforcement of anti-FGM laws, such as the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act of 2015 which criminalizes the practice with penalties up to life imprisonment, faces significant hurdles including low reporting rates—often because perpetrators are relatives—and limited police capacity, particularly in rural areas where cultural norms frame FGM as a private family matter. [9] [20] Between 2018 and 2021, only seven arrests and court cases were recorded nationwide, with zero convictions, reflecting prosecutorial gaps like inadequate evidence collection and judicial delays exacerbated by events such as COVID-19 lockdowns. [9] [20] While prosecutions deter overt traditional cuttings, they inadvertently drive the practice underground or toward medical settings, where detection is harder due to professional involvement and lack of specific monitoring. [20]

Debates surrounding these trends pit absolute prohibitions against pragmatic harm reduction, with critics arguing that strict enforcement without cultural engagement risks family backlash viewing laws as intrusions on parental autonomy, yet evidence from similar contexts shows exemptions or leniency fail to reduce prevalence and may normalize milder forms. [20] Proponents of absolutism emphasize causal links between FGM and irreversible harms, rejecting medicalization as a false safeguard that sustains demand, while enforcement advocates call for enhanced surveillance of clinics and community reporting incentives to avoid underground proliferation without excusing the practice's persistence. [70] [69]
