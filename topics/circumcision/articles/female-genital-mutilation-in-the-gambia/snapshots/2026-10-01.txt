# Female genital mutilation in the Gambia

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in the Gambia is a customary practice entailing the partial or total excision of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical purposes, most often conducted on girls before age five by traditional circumcisers using rudimentary tools. [1] The procedure, prevalent across ethnic groups including Mandinka, Fula, and Wolof, and irrespective of Muslim or Christian affiliation, serves purported social cohesion functions such as controlling female sexuality and marking rites of passage, with 72.6% of women aged 15–49 affected according to the 2019–20 Demographic and Health Survey, down marginally from 74.9% in 2013. [1] [2]

Enacted via the 2015 Women's (Amendment) Act, Gambian law criminalizes performing, aiding, or abetting FGM with penalties up to three years imprisonment or fines equivalent to $1,250, aligning with international obligations under treaties like the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women. [3] [2] Enforcement has been minimal, with only two prosecutions prior to the first convictions in 2023 for cutting infants, underscoring weak implementation amid cultural entrenchment. [3] In 2024, a parliamentary bill to repeal the ban advanced initially but was rejected following domestic activism and external advocacy, preserving the prohibition despite claims by some religious figures that it conflicts with tradition. [2] [3]

Predominantly Type I (clitoridectomy) or Type II (excision), with 16.6% involving infibulation (Type III), the practice correlates with elevated health risks including hemorrhage, urinary issues, and obstetric complications—studies indicate 77% of adverse neonatal outcomes link to FGM-affected mothers—compounded by a reported 34.3% lifetime health problems among cut women. [2] [1] Support for continuation has waned to 45.7% among women 15–49, particularly with rising education and wealth, fueling UNFPA-UNICEF initiatives like mothers' clubs reaching thousands via dialogues and alternative rites to shift norms. [2] [4] Cross-border practices with Senegal persist, but regional pacts and data systems aim to bolster monitoring, though abandonment hinges on addressing underlying social networks enforcing conformity. [4] [2]

## Cultural and Historical Context

### Origins and Traditional Significance

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in The Gambia traces its origins to ancient practices predating Islam and Christianity, with anthropological evidence suggesting roots extending over 2,000 years, possibly linked to early customs in regions like 5th-century BC Egypt or among Equatorial African herders for protective or sacrificial purposes. [5] In Gambian context, it emerged as a self-enforcing social convention upheld by community expectations across ethnic groups, transmitted generationally through families and elders, independent of religious mandates despite later associations with Islam by some adherents. [5] [6]

The practice holds profound traditional significance as a rite of passage marking the transition to womanhood, particularly among groups like the Mandinka, where it forms part of the nyaakaa initiation ritual performed between ages four and ten, transforming uninitiated girls (solima) into socially mature individuals prepared for marriage and motherhood. [5] During multi-week seclusion periods, initiates learn values such as respect, obedience, endurance, and secrecy—concepts encapsulated as "knowing the eye"—alongside practical skills, songs, and proverbs, reinforcing subservience and community roles. [5] It is justified culturally to ensure virginity, prevent promiscuity, control female sexuality, enhance cleanliness and aesthetic beauty, and uphold family honor, with uncut girls facing stigma as immature or unclean, often barring them from social ceremonies. [6] [7] Among the Sarahule, it occurs in infancy without elaborate ceremony but is viewed as essential for purity; Fula subgroups similarly emphasize sealing to guarantee fidelity. [5]

Prevalence and significance vary ethnically, with near-universal adoption among Mandinka (96.7%), Sarahule (97.8%), Fula (87.3%), and Jola/Karoninka (87%), where it signals ethnic identity and social integration, contrasted by low rates among Wolof (12.4%) and Serer (43-46%). [6] [5] These beliefs prioritize marriageability and communal acceptance, with grandmothers often deciding based on norms that equate FGM with a girl's status and her family's reputation, perpetuated by elder women circumcisers who derive authority from the tradition. [5] [7]

### Types and Procedures Practiced

In The Gambia, female genital mutilation (FGM) primarily consists of procedures classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as Type I and Type II, with Type I being the most common. [6] [8] Type I, or clitoridectomy, involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or its prepuce. [6] In a 2013 clinical examination of 570 Gambian women aged 15-49, Type I accounted for 57.2% of cases among those who had undergone FGM. [6] Type II, or excision, entails the partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora; this type represented 18.4% of cases in the same study. [6]

These procedures are traditionally carried out by elderly female excisers, known as traditional practitioners, who perform 97% of cuttings on girls and women aged 15-49. [8] The cutting occurs without anesthesia or sterile conditions, using rudimentary sharp instruments such as knives or razors, often leading to imprecise excision that may extend beyond the targeted tissues. [6] Girls typically undergo the procedure between birth and preadolescence, before menarche or marriage; among women aged 15-49, 55% were cut before age five, 28% between ages five and nine, and 7% between ages ten and 14. [8] Type III (infibulation), involving narrowing of the vaginal opening through apposition of the labia, is rare, observed in only 0.4% of the examined sample. [6] Type IV procedures, such as pricking or scraping, have been reported anecdotally but lack quantified prevalence in Gambian data. [9]

Variations in procedure extent occur across ethnic groups practicing FGM extensively, including Mandinka, Fula, Sarahule, and Jola, where prevalence within these groups exceeds 94% for women aged 15-49; these groups favor Types I and II without routine infibulation. [6] Post-procedure, wounds are sometimes bound or treated with herbal pastes to promote healing and adhesion, though medical intervention is absent in traditional settings. [6]

## Prevalence and Demographics

### National and Regional Statistics

According to the 2019-20 Gambia Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS), 73% of women aged 15-49 have undergone female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C). [10] This national prevalence reflects data from a representative sample of over 11,000 women, capturing self-reported experiences of the procedure. [10] Among girls aged 0-14, 46% had been subjected to FGM/C at the time of the survey, with higher rates (60%) among daughters of circumcised mothers. [10]

### Ethnic and Social Variations

In The Gambia, female genital mutilation (FGM) prevalence varies significantly by ethnic group, with the practice deeply embedded in the traditions of certain communities while largely absent in others. Among the major ethnic groups, Sarahuleh exhibit the highest rates, with 76% of girls undergoing FGM, followed by Mandinka/Jahanka at 54%, Bambara at 50%, Fula/Tukulor/Lorobo at 46%, and Jola/Karoninka at 43%. [11] In contrast, groups such as Wollof, Creole/Aku (19%), Serere (9%), and Manjago (4%) show much lower or negligible prevalence, reflecting cultural norms that do not incorporate the practice. [11] [9] Overall, for women aged 15-49, ethnic variations range from as low as 14% to as high as 95%, underscoring how FGM serves as a marker of group identity in practicing communities like Mandinka, Jola, Sarahule, and Fula, but is rejected by non-practicing ones such as Wolof and Serere. ## Health and Social Impacts

### Documented Physical and Psychological Effects

Physical effects of female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) in The Gambia include immediate complications such as hemorrhage, acute anemia, and infections like tetanus, cystitis, and septicaemia, reported in 12.6% of cases seeking prompt medical attention after the procedure. [12] Long-term physical consequences encompass chronic gynecological issues, with women subjected to type I FGM/C (clitoridectomy, comprising 66.2% of cases) experiencing dysmenorrhea in 58.2% of instances, recurrent urinary tract infections in 19.6%, and painful intercourse in 29.3%, while type II (excision, 26.3% of cases) shows elevated rates of 67.6%, 44.2%, and 57.1%, respectively, compared to 34.5%, 14.4%, and 12.9% in uncut women (all p < 0.001). [6] [12] Anomalous healing manifests as fibrosis (15.8–19.4%), keloid formation (13.3–35.6% on clitoris, up to 21.6% on labia), synechia (7.1–54.4%), and clitoral neuroma (8.1–20.6%), absent in uncut controls. [6]

Obstetric complications are markedly increased, with 39.0% of type I and 65.9% of type II cases facing delivery issues like perineal tears (27.8–48.8%), prolonged labor (15.6–36.6%), and episiotomy needs (20.0–30.5%), yielding odds ratios of 4.82–9.06 relative to uncut women (11.7% complications); fresh stillbirths occur in 2.9–6.1% of affected deliveries versus 0%. [6] Newborns of cut mothers experience higher rates of fetal distress (8.8–26.8%) and caput succedaneum (14.6–34.2%), with overall complications in 15.6–37.8% versus 5.3% (odds ratio 5.17). [6] Overall, 34.3% of Gambian women examined for FGM/C-related issues report either immediate or delayed physical effects, including persistent scarring and dyspareunia across all types. [12]

Psychological effects in Gambian girls post-FGC include probable post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) at 19.2% prevalence among 12-year-olds, though only 4.2% attribute it directly to FGC, with 95.8% linked to confounding serious life events like violence or bereavement (average 4.5 events per girl). [13] Girls citing FGC as most bothersome (16.8%) show lower PTSD rates (4.8%) and better functioning than those affected by other traumas (22.1%), indicating FGC's trauma may be overshadowed by broader adversities; physical complications like genital pain do not significantly correlate with PTSD attribution in this cohort. [13] General literature on FGM/C notes associations with anxiety, depression, and sexual dysfunction, potentially exacerbated by physical sequelae, but Gambia-specific data emphasize multifactorial mental health burdens rather than isolated FGC causation. [14]

### Perceived Cultural Benefits and Local Justifications

In The Gambia, female genital mutilation (FGM), locally termed "female circumcision," is often justified as a rite of passage marking a girl's transition to womanhood and social maturity, particularly among ethnic groups like the Mandinka, where it forms part of the nyaakaa initiation ritual for girls aged four to ten. This process, involving seclusion and instruction from elder women, is believed to instill essential virtues such as respect (horomo), secrecy (suturo), and endurance (sabati), collectively known as "knowing the eye," preparing girls for roles in marriage and motherhood while reinforcing community values of obedience and discretion. [15] Proponents argue that the pain endured during the procedure teaches resilience and discipline, with respondents in qualitative studies describing it as a "school" for proper behavior among elders, thereby enhancing a girl's social standing and eligibility for inclusion in communal events. [16]

Local beliefs emphasize FGM's role in controlling female sexuality to preserve purity and prevent promiscuity, with many Gambians asserting that uncut girls "like men too much" or risk premarital sex, pregnancy, or infidelity, thus safeguarding family honor and marital fidelity. [16] It is perceived to reduce excessive sexual desire, making women less demanding in marriage and averting risks like divorce or prostitution, while also ensuring physical "sealing" of virginity until wedlock in practices akin to Type III FGM among groups like the Sarahule and some Fula. [15] Hygiene and aesthetic ideals further underpin justifications, as the procedure is said to eliminate "bad smells," prevent abnormal clitoral growth, and promote cleanliness aligned with Islamic purity norms, rendering uncut women (solima in Mandinka) impure, disrespectful, or unfit for prayer and social interactions. [16] Social conformity drives adherence, with uncircumcised individuals facing stigma, ostracism, or exclusion from ceremonies, compelling families to comply to maintain ethnic and communal acceptance, especially in rural areas where prevalence exceeds 90% among Mandinka (96.7%) and Sarahule (97.8%) women aged 15-49. [15] [17]

Religious rationales, though not explicitly mandated by the Quran, draw on select hadiths referencing "female circumcision" as a virtuous Islamic practice (sunna), with leaders like Imam Abdoulie Fatty and the Gambia Supreme Islamic Council framing it as essential for piety and cultural preservation against external impositions. [18] Defenders, including lawmakers and practitioners, equate it to male circumcision and argue that bans infringe on constitutional rights to religious and ethnic traditions, viewing abandonment as a threat to societal norms upheld across generations. [17] Among ethnic minorities like the Wolof, where prevalence is lower (12.4%), justifications weaken, but in dominant groups, the practice sustains intergenerational transmission, with elders overriding individual choice to preserve group identity and economic roles for cutters. [15] These perceptions persist despite health risks, as cultural continuity is prioritized, with surveys indicating over 70% support in regions like Brikama and Mansakonko. [15]

## Legal Framework

### Historical Legislation

Prior to the enactment of specific legislation in 2015, female genital mutilation (FGM) in The Gambia lacked any dedicated legal prohibition under national law, despite its widespread practice across ethnic groups and regions. [19] The practice persisted without criminalization during the colonial period under British rule and in the post-independence era following 1965, reflecting its entrenched cultural status rather than formal legal endorsement or restriction. [20]

In December 2015, the Gambian government passed the Women's (Amendment) Act 2015, amending the 2010 Women's Act to explicitly criminalize FGM. [19] Sections 32A and 32B of the amended act define FGM as the partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to female genital organs for non-therapeutic reasons, imposing penalties of a fine of up to 50,000 Dalasi, imprisonment for a term of up to three years, or both, with life imprisonment if the procedure causes death. [21] This legislation marked the first national ban, prompted by President Yahya Jammeh's public declaration in November 2015 that FGM was "un-Islamic" and would no longer be tolerated, framing the prohibition in religious terms to align with the country's predominantly Muslim population. [20]

The 2015 act represented a shift from prior inaction, influenced by international pressure from organizations like UNICEF and the African Union, though enforcement remained limited in its initial years due to cultural resistance and weak institutional capacity. [22] No earlier Gambian statutes, such as those on child protection or general assault, were systematically applied to FGM cases prior to this amendment, allowing the practice to continue unchecked. [19]

### Enforcement, Penalties, and Compliance Issues

The Women's (Amendment) Act 2015 criminalizes performing female genital mutilation (FGM) under Section 32A(1), with penalties of up to three years' imprisonment, a fine of 50,000 Dalasi (approximately US$1,060), or both; if the procedure causes death, the punishment is life imprisonment. [21] Section 32B(1) imposes the same penalties on those who request, incite, or promote FGM by providing tools or other means, while failure to report known or impending FGM without good cause incurs a fine of 10,000 Dalasi (approximately US$212). [21] These provisions apply universally, including to traditional circumcisers, parents, and accomplices, supported by complementary laws such as the Children's Act 2005 and Criminal Code 1933, which address harm to children and grievous bodily injury. [21]

Enforcement relies on coordination among the Ministries of Women's Affairs, Justice, Health, and Social Welfare, alongside the National Steering Committee on FGM and community surveillance systems established with NGO support, such as GAMCOTRAP and UNFPA initiatives since 2021. [23] [21] Prosecutions remain rare; as of 2024, convictions have been secured only in the landmark 2023 case. In August 2023, the Kaur/Kuntaur Magistrates Court convicted three women for performing FGM on three infants (aged 4 months to 1 year) in Bakadagi-Mandinka village, fining each 15,000 Dalasi with a one-year prison alternative, following intervention that prevented the procedure on five other girls. [23] [24] An earlier 2016 case involving a child's death from FGM yielded no reported follow-through, highlighting inconsistent judicial follow-up. [21]

Compliance is undermined by persistent cultural entrenchment, with approximately 76% of women aged 15–49 having undergone FGM, often before age six, and widespread community resistance viewing the practice as a religious or traditional rite. [24] The procedure continues clandestinely, particularly in rural areas and across borders with high-prevalence neighbors like Senegal, evading detection due to underreporting and assurances from influential figures that perpetrators face no repercussions. [24] [21] A 2024 private bill to repeal the ban, rejected by the National Assembly in July after public debate, exposed ongoing pushback, with proponents citing cultural accommodations despite the law's preservation signaling nominal political commitment. [24] Gaps include inadequate training for law enforcers, limited victim protections, and absence of explicit provisions against medicalized or extraterritorial FGM, contributing to weak deterrence and sustained non-compliance. [21]

## Activism and Debates

### Anti-FGM Campaigns and International Involvement

Anti-FGM campaigns in The Gambia have intensified since the 2015 enactment of the Women's (Amendment) Act, which criminalized the practice with penalties of up to three years imprisonment and fines. Local organizations, including women's networks and religious leaders, have spearheaded community-based initiatives, such as advocacy through imams' sermons to challenge cultural and religious justifications for FGM. For instance, in rural areas, illiterate women like Amie Jatta Njie have mobilized peers via door-to-door education and village meetings to promote alternatives to the tradition, emphasizing health risks and legal consequences. These grassroots efforts often collaborate with national bodies like the Ministry of Women's Affairs, focusing on awareness in high-prevalence ethnic groups such as the Mandinka and Fula. [25]

International organizations have provided substantial support, funding, and technical assistance to bolster these campaigns. The UNFPA-UNICEF Joint Programme on the Elimination of Female Genital Mutilation, operational in The Gambia since 2008, has trained over 1,000 community influencers, including traditional cutters who renounce the practice, and supported public declarations against FGM in 15% of targeted villages by 2023. UNICEF has also engaged youth advocates, such as Nafisa Binte Jobe, who testified in parliament against repeal efforts, while UNFPA facilitated cross-border dialogues with Senegal and Guinea to curb FGM migration. In 2024, amid threats to repeal the ban, global entities like the World Health Organization and UN Women issued statements reinforcing The Gambia's commitments under the Maputo Protocol and Sustainable Development Goal 5.3, which aims to eliminate harmful practices by 2030. [26] [27] [28]

A pivotal campaign victory occurred on July 15, 2024, when parliament rejected a bill to decriminalize FGM, following intense lobbying by activists like Jaha Dukureh of Safe Hands for Girls, who credited international pressure from UN agencies and NGOs such as Amnesty International for swaying votes. This outcome preserved the ban amid religious opposition, with UN officials hailing it as a "monumental achievement" aligned with human rights treaties. However, Amnesty noted persistent root causes, urging sustained investment in education and economic alternatives for cutters, as campaigns have contributed to a slight decline in prevalence from 74.9% in 2013 to 72.6% in 2019–20 according to the Demographic and Health Survey, though enforcement remains uneven. [29] [30] [3] [1] International donors, including the European Union and U.S. Agency for International Development, have channeled over $5 million annually into prevention since 2015, prioritizing survivor-led advocacy to counter cultural resistance.

### Defense of the Practice and Cultural Resistance

Proponents of female genital mutilation (FGM) in The Gambia defend the practice as an integral component of cultural identity and social cohesion, viewing it as a rite of passage that marks a girl's transition to womanhood and ensures her eligibility for marriage within ethnic communities such as the Mandinka, Fula, and Jola. [18] Local beliefs hold that FGM preserves virginity, curbs promiscuity, and maintains family honor by controlling female sexuality, with uncut women often stigmatized as impure or capable of contaminating food, rendering it haram under traditional interpretations. [18] Cutters, typically respected elderly women, derive social status from performing the procedure, which is passed down generationally and reinforces community bonds, as evidenced by statements from practitioners who refuse to abandon it despite incentives. [31]

Religious justifications form a core defense, with many Gambians, including 53% of women surveyed in 2019-2020, believing FGM fulfills an Islamic obligation derived from certain hadiths, despite its absence from the Quran and pre-Islamic origins. [17] The Gambia Supreme Islamic Council issued a fatwa in September 2023 declaring female circumcision a "virtue of Islam," urging reconsideration of the 2015 ban, while Imam Abdoulie Fatty has campaigned to "restore" it, equating opposition to an assault on Islam and paying fines for convicted cutters in 2023. [18] Proponents like National Assembly Member Sulayman Saho distinguish it from mutilation, advocating only the removal of the clitoral tip—analogous to male circumcision—while questioning the relevance of global data to Gambian contexts. [18]

Cultural resistance manifests in political and communal pushback against the ban, including a 2024 Women's (Amendment) Bill introduced by MP Almameh Gibba to repeal it, which passed second reading with 43 of 47 votes before stalling amid international scrutiny. [31] Supporters frame the law as a Western imposition eroding sovereignty, with protests chanting "Gambia is not for sale" and decrying it as colonial interference, leading to legal challenges citing constitutional rights to religious and cultural freedoms under Sections 25 and 32. [31] Despite the ban's affirmation in July 2024, underground continuation persists, driven by fears that abandonment would dishonor ancestors and disrupt social norms, as articulated by community elders who prioritize tradition over external health critiques. [18]

## Recent Developments and Challenges

### Key Events from 2015 to 2024

In December 2015, President Yahya Jammeh signed the Women's (Amendment) Act, criminalizing female genital mutilation (FGM) with penalties of up to three years imprisonment and fines, marking the first national ban on the practice in The Gambia where prevalence exceeded 75% among women. [32] The legislation targeted all forms of FGM, including on minors and adults, amid international pressure and local advocacy, though enforcement remained limited in subsequent years due to cultural entrenchment. [33]

In 2016, The Gambia's parliament adopted the National Strategic and Action Plan for the Promotion of the Elimination of Female Genital Mutilation (2016-2020), outlining community sensitization, legal enforcement, and survivor support to reduce prevalence from an estimated 76.3% in adult women. [34] This followed a 2013 Demographic and Health Survey confirming high rates across ethnic groups, with the plan emphasizing partnerships with NGOs and religious leaders despite resistance from traditional cutters and communities viewing FGM as a rite of passage. [34]

From 2017 to 2022, no major prosecutions occurred under the ban, with surveys indicating persistent practice rates around 70-75%, often conducted clandestinely on infants to evade scrutiny, as reported by anti-FGM organizations tracking underground operations. [35] International funding supported awareness campaigns, but local compliance lagged, with cut cases rarely reported due to community complicity and fear of reprisal. [36]

In August 2023, authorities filed the first criminal charges for FGM since the ban, indicting three women in connection with performing the procedure, highlighting emerging enforcement efforts amid rising advocacy pressures. [35]

In March 2024, National Assembly Member Fatoumata Ceesay-Jallow introduced a private member's bill to repeal sections of the 2015 Act banning FGM, arguing it violated cultural and religious norms; the assembly voted 31-4 to advance it for debate despite committee recommendations against. [37] This sparked international condemnation from UNICEF, UNFPA, and Human Rights Watch, who cited risks to girls' health and rights, with prevalence data showing over 70% of women aged 15-49 still affected. [38] [33]

On July 15, 2024, The Gambia's National Assembly voted 31-18 to reject the repeal bill, upholding the 2015 ban following intense lobbying by activists like Jaha Dukureh and global bodies including the UN, which hailed it as a "monumental achievement" for protecting over 200,000 girls at risk annually. [30] [29] The decision underscored tensions between cultural preservation and human rights, with Amnesty International urging stronger root-cause interventions like education to sustain progress. [3]

### Ongoing Enforcement and Future Prospects

Enforcement of Gambia's 2015 ban on female genital mutilation (FGM) remains inconsistent, with reports indicating limited prosecutions and widespread non-compliance in rural areas. As of 2023, only a handful of cases have reached the courts since the law's enactment, including landmark convictions in August 2023 where three women were found guilty and fined D15,000 (or one year imprisonment in default) for performing FGM on infants, marking the first such convictions under the ban. [23] Despite this, human rights organizations note that enforcement is hampered by cultural entrenchment, lack of political will, and inadequate resources for monitoring, with a prevalence of 72.6% among women aged 15–49 as of the 2019–20 Demographic and Health Survey, reflecting high ongoing risk to girls. [1]

Challenges to ongoing enforcement include resistance from religious and community leaders who frame FGM as an Islamic obligation, despite scholarly rebuttals from Gambian imams in 2023 fatwas declaring it un-Islamic. Government initiatives, such as the National Action Plan Against FGM (2021-2025), aim to bolster community education and survivor support, but implementation is uneven, with funding shortfalls reported by NGOs in 2024. International partners like UNICEF and the EU have provided training for law enforcement, yet corruption and underreporting persist, as village-level excisions often evade detection.

Future prospects for eradication appear guarded, contingent on sustained advocacy and economic incentives to shift norms. Projections from 2023 studies suggest that without intensified enforcement, prevalence could decline only modestly to 60% by 2030, driven by urbanization and youth-led campaigns rather than legal measures alone. Optimistic scenarios hinge on expanding alternative rites of passage programs, which have shown success in select communities by preserving cultural elements without cutting, potentially accelerating abandonment if scaled nationally. However, political shifts, including potential Islamist influences, could undermine progress, as evidenced by a 2022 parliamentary push to repeal the ban that was narrowly defeated.
