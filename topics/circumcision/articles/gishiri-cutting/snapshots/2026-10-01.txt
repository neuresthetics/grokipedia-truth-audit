# Gishiri cutting

Gishiri cutting, also termed Yankan Gishiri or "salt cut" in Hausa, is a traditional therapeutic intervention practiced predominantly among rural Hausa-Fulani communities in northern Nigeria, whereby a longitudinal incision is made into the anterior or posterior vaginal wall using a knife or razor blade to address conditions such as perceived vaginal narrowing (stenosis), prolonged labor, infertility, genital infections, or prolapse. [1] The procedure is typically carried out by untrained traditional barbers (wanzami) or birth attendants (ungozoma) without anesthesia or sterile conditions, often involving a subsequent application of a paste made from local salt (gishiri) believed to promote healing and efficacy. [1] [2]

Classified by the World Health Organization as a Type IV form of female genital cutting—encompassing practices like pricking, piercing, or incision—Gishiri cutting differs from ritualistic excisions in its remedial intent, rooted in cultural beliefs that the cut enlarges the vaginal canal or expels pathological "blockages" to restore fertility and ease childbirth. [3] Empirical data from clinical studies indicate it accounts for 5 to 18 percent of vesicovaginal fistulas (VVF) in northern Nigerian fistula centers, with one analysis of 1,372 cases attributing 78 instances (5.68 percent) directly to the practice, often resulting from inadvertent damage to the urethra or bladder during incision. [1] [4] [5] These fistulas manifest as chronic urinary incontinence, severe pain, recurrent infections, and social isolation, with affected women frequently requiring complex reconstructive surgery. [6] [7]

Despite its persistence in rural areas due to limited access to modern healthcare and entrenched traditional healing systems, Gishiri cutting has drawn scrutiny for its iatrogenic harms, with peer-reviewed reports linking it to heightened obstetric risks, urethral erosion, and secondary complications like sepsis or hemorrhage from non-sterile tools. [8] [9] Public health initiatives, including community education and fistula repair programs, have sought to curb the practice by demonstrating its causal role in preventable disabilities, though cultural acceptance remains a barrier in Hausa-dominated regions. [10] [11]

## Definition and Etymology

### Definition

Gishiri cutting, also known as Yankan Gishiri or "salt cut" in Hausa, is a traditional form of female genital cutting involving the deliberate incision of the vaginal wall. [1] This procedure entails making one or more longitudinal cuts, typically 2–5 centimeters from the urethral opening, in the anterior or posterior vaginal wall using a sharp blade such as a knife or razor. [1] It is classified by the World Health Organization as Type IV female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C), a category encompassing all harmful procedures on female genitalia for non-medical purposes, including pricking, piercing, incising, and scraping. [12] The practice is predominantly carried out among Hausa-Fulani ethnic groups in rural northern Nigeria and southern Niger, often by traditional barbers (wanzami) or birth attendants (ungozoma). [1] [8]

The etymological term "gishiri" derives from the Hausa word for salt, reflecting cultural associations with salt's symbolic role in fertility and life, as well as historical practices involving the application of rock salt to the incision site post-procedure. [1] Unlike more excision-based forms of FGM/C, gishiri cutting targets the vaginal canal rather than the clitoris or labia, with incisions sometimes extending toward the urethra or bladder, increasing risks of complications such as vesicovaginal fistulas. [3] Medical studies indicate it accounts for 1–18% of genitourinary fistulas in affected regions, highlighting its iatrogenic potential despite traditional therapeutic intent. [8] [1]

### Etymology and Terminology

The term gishiri derives from the Hausa language spoken in northern Nigeria and parts of Niger, where it directly translates to "salt," reflecting the procedure's traditional use of a salt-based paste applied to the incision site for purported antiseptic and tightening effects. [13] [14] This etymological root underscores the practice's association with salt as a key element, historically traded by Arab caravans across the Sahara to West African regions, though the cutting itself is a localized Hausa-Fulani custom rather than a direct import. [15]

Gishiri cutting, alternatively termed Yankan Gishiri (meaning "salt cut" in Hausa), denotes a specific variant of female genital cutting involving incisions into the anterior vaginal wall, distinct from clitoridectomy or infibulation prevalent in other Nigerian ethnic groups. [16] It is classified under Type IV female genital mutilation/cutting by international health frameworks, encompassing pricking, scraping, or incising the genital or vaginal area without removal of tissue, often performed postpartum or to address perceived vaginal laxity. [3] [15] In neighboring Niger, a closely related practice is known as Gurya cutting, involving similar vaginal scraping, though it shares the regional Hausa linguistic and cultural context. [8] These terms are not interchangeable with broader categories like "female circumcision," which typically refer to clitoral excision in Nigeria's southern or central regions. [15]

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Origins and Historical Prevalence

Gishiri cutting, known in Hausa as yankan gishiri (literally "salt cutting"), emerged as a traditional vaginal incision practice among Hausa-Fulani communities in northern Nigeria, potentially originating in the mid-20th century as an imitation of hospital episiotomy procedures introduced during colonial or early post-colonial medical influences. [17] This adaptation involved using unsterilized tools or abrasive salt pastes to enlarge the vaginal opening, ostensibly to address obstetric issues, though its roots lack documentation predating modern medical contact in the region. [8]

Historically, the practice gained prevalence in rural Hausa-dominated areas of northern Nigeria and parts of Niger, where it was employed for perceived treatments of dyspareunia, infertility, uterine prolapse, and prolonged labor, often by traditional birth attendants. [8] By the late 20th century, epidemiological data from fistula repair centers indicated gishiri cutting contributed to 2-15% of vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) cases in northern Nigeria, with higher estimates in specific locales like Kano and Sokoto states. [18] [19] In a 1990 analysis of VVF etiologies, up to 15% of cases were attributed directly to gishiri incisions, underscoring its role in iatrogenic obstetric injuries amid limited access to formal healthcare. [19]

Prevalence persisted into the early 21st century, particularly among uneducated women in polygamous Hausa-Fulani households, with surveys in northern Nigeria reporting 13% of postpartum women having undergone gishiri cuts alongside other local modifications like angurya scraping. [20] Institutional records from facilities such as Ahmadu Bello University Teaching Hospital in Zaria documented ongoing cases, with gishiri accounting for a notable fraction of non-obstetric fistulas, though exact national incidence remains underreported due to cultural secrecy and rural isolation. [21] Efforts to quantify historical spread highlight its concentration in Muslim-majority northern states, where it intersected with traditional medicine systems predating but amplified by episodic medical interventions. [22]

### Cultural Role in Hausa-Fulani Societies

In Hausa-Fulani societies of northern Nigeria and southern Niger, Yankan Gishiri—literally "salt cut" in Hausa—serves as a traditional therapeutic intervention within local ethnomedical systems, addressing perceived reproductive and sexual health ailments rather than functioning as a initiatory rite. The practice embodies cultural conceptualizations of the female genitalia as the "salt of life," where "gishiri" (salt) metaphorically denotes vaginal conditions involving narrowing, dryness, or obstruction, believed to disrupt fertility, intercourse, or childbirth. [8]

Traditional beliefs hold that such conditions, termed "ba hanya" (no way) or "goriya" (a tumor-like blockage), arise from imbalances in bodily humors, infections, or even psychological factors like marital discord, necessitating incision to restore patency and harmony. [23] [8] It is invoked for symptoms including dyspareunia, infertility, prolonged labor, genital prolapse, or itching, with practitioners viewing the cut as a curative release of blockages, often followed by salt application to promote healing and lubrication. This reflects broader Hausa-Fulani emphases on women's reproductive roles, where easing sexual submission and ensuring progeny fulfillment social obligations within patrilineal kinship structures. [8]

The procedure is typically executed by wanzami (traditional barbers) or ungozoma (birth attendants), non-specialized healers integrated into community trust networks, underscoring its embedding in everyday customary medicine over formal Islamic or biomedical alternatives. [23] Performed ad hoc—squatting for chronic issues or supine during labor—it lacks elaborate rituals but aligns with gendered expectations of female compliance and vitality, persisting in rural areas despite health campaigns due to entrenched oral traditions and skepticism toward Western interventions. [8] In clinical reviews from 2007–2010 across Kano facilities, it accounted for 5.68% of obstetric fistula cases, highlighting its prevalence as a culturally sanctioned response to gynecological distress.

## Procedure and Practice

### Step-by-Step Description

The gishiri cutting procedure, known locally as yankan gishiri among the Hausa-Fulani, involves a therapeutic incision into the vaginal wall performed by non-medical practitioners to address perceived reproductive or sexual issues. [1] It is typically conducted in rural settings without anesthesia or sterile conditions, targeting symptoms such as vaginal narrowing, obstructed labor, dyspareunia, or genital discomfort. [8] Practitioners include traditional barbers (wanzami), who perform approximately 59% of cases, or birth attendants (unguzoma), accounting for 41%. [1]

The process begins with the woman presenting to the practitioner, often due to complaints like infertility, prolapse, or itching, which are attributed to vaginal "stenosis" or blockages in traditional beliefs. [1] The patient is positioned either lying supine or squatting to expose the genital area. [1]

Using a non-sterile sharp instrument such as a razor blade or knife, the practitioner makes a longitudinal incision, typically 2–5 cm in length, into the anterior vaginal wall a few centimeters from the urethral opening; posterior wall cuts occur less frequently. [8] [1] The cut aims to "widen" the vagina or relieve perceived obstructions, with no standardized depth or extent, leading to variable tissue damage including potential urethral involvement. [8]

Post-procedure, the incision is left to heal naturally without suturing, sometimes with application of local herbs or salt (gishiri), though documentation focuses primarily on the cutting itself rather than adjunctive treatments. [1] The entire intervention lasts minutes and is repeated if symptoms persist, exacerbating risks. [8]

### Practitioners and Tools Used

Gishiri cutting is predominantly carried out by traditional birth attendants (TBAs), often elderly women serving as informal healers within Hausa-Fulani communities in northern Nigeria. [24] These practitioners, lacking formal medical training, respond to perceived gynecological issues such as obstructed labor, infertility, or vaginal narrowing by performing the incision without anesthesia or sterile conditions. [25] In some cases, male barbers or traditional midwives also undertake the procedure, drawing on cultural knowledge passed through generations rather than evidence-based obstetrics. [1] [25]

The tools employed are rudimentary sharp instruments, including razor blades, knives, penknives, scissors, kitchen knives, or even fragments of glass, selected for their availability in household or local settings. [24] [26] These implements are typically unsterilized and reused without disinfection, heightening risks of infection due to the absence of hygienic protocols. [26] Razor blades are most commonly associated with TBAs, while knives predominate among barbers, reflecting the non-specialized nature of the practice. [1]

No formal certification or oversight governs these practitioners, who operate within a framework of customary medicine where efficacy is gauged by anecdotal cultural beliefs rather than clinical validation. [24] Interventions occur in non-clinical environments, such as homes, exacerbating procedural inconsistencies and potential for harm. [25]

## Traditional Rationale and Purported Benefits

### Cultural and Medicinal Beliefs

In Hausa-Fulani communities of northern Nigeria, gishiri cutting stems from the traditional belief in a gynecological condition termed gishiri, where a metaphorical layer of "salt" (the Hausa word gishiri literally meaning salt) accumulates inside the vagina, purportedly causing narrowing of the vaginal canal. [8] This buildup is thought to result from dietary or physiological factors, leading to symptoms such as infertility, dyspareunia (painful intercourse), and obstructed labor during childbirth. [8] Practitioners hold that the procedure removes this obstructive salt layer by applying a caustic paste of rock salt mixed with herbs or by scraping the vaginal walls until bleeding occurs, thereby widening the passage and restoring normal function. [8]

Medicinally, the practice is rationalized as a curative intervention for a range of reproductive ailments beyond the core gishiri condition, including amenorrhea (absence of menstruation), pruritus vulvae (vaginal itching), and chronic pelvic pain attributed to vaginal constriction. Traditional healers, often local barbers or midwives, perform it on women seeking relief from these issues, with the bleeding induced by the procedure viewed as evidence of purification and expulsion of impurities. Culturally, it reinforces notions of female bodily maintenance aligned with marital and procreative duties, as a narrow vagina is believed to hinder sexual satisfaction for husbands and successful pregnancies, potentially straining social bonds within patrilineal Hausa society. [27]

These beliefs persist despite lacking empirical validation, with the procedure's endorsement rooted in oral traditions and experiential anecdotes passed through generations rather than systematic medical inquiry. [8] In some cases, it is performed prophylactically on primiparous women to preempt labor complications, reflecting a broader cultural emphasis on enhancing fertility and easing delivery in resource-limited settings. [27]

### Reported Traditional Outcomes

In traditional Hausa-Fulani communities, practitioners of Yankan Gishiri report that the procedure alleviates symptoms attributed to vaginal narrowing or stenosis, such as painful intercourse (dyspareunia) and difficulty with penetration, by widening the vaginal canal. Traditional birth attendants and barbers performing the cut claim it resolves these issues, enabling improved sexual function and satisfaction for both partners, often described as unblocking a perceived barrier symbolized by "gishiri" (salt) in local idiom. [28]

Reports from practitioners also assert efficacy in treating infertility, with the cut purportedly facilitating conception by addressing supposed obstructions or tightness preventing sperm access or implantation. For obstetric concerns, traditional accounts describe successful outcomes in easing prolonged labor, where the incision is said to "unblock the presenting part" of the fetus, reducing delivery complications and prolapse risks. [15] These claims stem from anecdotal practitioner experiences and cultural beliefs, without controlled verification, and are applied to symptoms like itching, boils, or genital pain.

Such outcomes are culturally reinforced as curative for amenorrhea or other gynecological ailments, with women seeking the procedure postpartum or during reproductive challenges in northern Nigeria and southern Niger. [29] However, these reports lack quantitative data from traditional sources, relying instead on qualitative assertions by Wanzami (barbers) or Ungozoma (attendants) who perform the cut with unsterilized tools.

## Medical Risks and Evidence-Based Complications

### Immediate Health Risks

The Gishiri cut, involving a deep incision into the anterior or posterior vaginal wall using a knife or razor blade, frequently results in excessive bleeding due to the vascular nature of the tissue involved. [30] This hemorrhage can be profuse and, if uncontrolled, lead to hypovolemic shock, particularly in settings without access to medical intervention. [30] Studies on similar Type IV female genital cutting procedures report excessive bleeding as one of the most common immediate complications, occurring in a significant proportion of cases performed by untrained practitioners. [31]

Severe pain accompanies the procedure, stemming from the acute trauma to sensitive vaginal tissues, often exacerbated by the absence of anesthesia. [31] Swelling and edema in the affected area are also prevalent, potentially causing urinary retention or difficulty with micturition shortly after the cut. [31]

Infection poses an acute risk owing to the use of unsterilized instruments and non-aseptic conditions typical of traditional settings. [24] Reproductive tract infections can manifest rapidly, with symptoms including fever and localized inflammation, and may progress to systemic sepsis if untreated. [24] Tetanus infection is a further concern from contaminated blades, contributing to immediate morbidity in vulnerable populations. [31]

### Long-Term Physiological Impacts

Gishiri cutting, a procedure entailing incision into the anterior vaginal wall, is strongly associated with the development of vesico-vaginal fistulae (VVF), a chronic condition characterized by an abnormal opening between the bladder and vagina that results in continuous involuntary urinary leakage. [24] In a review of obstetric morbidity, 13% of 1,443 VVF cases among Hausa women in northern Nigeria were directly attributed to gishiri cuts, particularly in older patients seeking treatment for perceived vaginal tightness or obstructed labor. [24] These fistulae often manifest months after the procedure due to poor healing, tissue necrosis, or secondary infection, leading to lifelong incontinence unless surgically repaired, with success rates varying based on fistula size and location. [32]

Scarring from the incisions contributes to vaginal stenosis, narrowing the vaginal canal and impeding normal anatomy, which exacerbates dyspareunia (painful intercourse) and increases susceptibility to recurrent urinary tract and reproductive tract infections. [32] Fistulae arising from gishiri cuts tend to be larger than those from other causes, complicating surgical closure and heightening risks of residual incontinence or recurrent breakdown post-repair. [32] Chronic inflammation from unhealed tissues can further promote pelvic inflammatory disease, potentially leading to tubal adhesions and secondary infertility, though direct causation requires further longitudinal study. [24]

Long-term sequelae also include dermatological issues from perpetual moisture, such as perineal excoriation and ulceration, alongside heightened obstetric risks in subsequent pregnancies, including obstructed labor due to scar tissue distortion. [33] In northern Nigerian cohorts, gishiri-related VVF accounted for up to 6.2% of cases as the second most common etiology after prolonged labor, underscoring its role in sustained physiological disability. [34]

## Scientific Assessment

### Empirical Evidence on Efficacy

No peer-reviewed studies have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy for gishiri cutting in addressing its purported benefits, including easing obstructed labor, treating dyspareunia, infertility, or amenorrhea. [35] [36] The procedure, involving unsterile incision of the anterior vaginal wall, lacks controlled trials or longitudinal data showing improved reproductive or obstetric outcomes compared to standard medical interventions. [37]

Epidemiological analyses from northern Nigeria, where the practice is prevalent among Hausa communities, instead associate gishiri cutting with iatrogenic vesicovaginal fistulas, accounting for approximately 13% of cases in adult patients treated at regional fistula centers between 1971 and 1981. [35] In a cohort of 1,443 women with fistulas, 32.5% reported prior gishiri cuts, with the procedure performed using unsterilized blades or shards, often leading to immediate tissue necrosis and urinary incontinence rather than resolution of symptoms. [35] Among pediatric cases (under 13 years), 15% of 80 documented fistulas were directly attributed to gishiri, typically applied for perceived vaginal narrowing but resulting in permanent defects without evidence of preventive success against labor complications. [4] [9]

The World Health Organization classifies gishiri as a type IV female genital mutilation/cutting procedure, emphasizing that all such practices confer no health benefits and carry risks of infection, hemorrhage, and chronic morbidity, with empirical data underscoring harm over utility. [37] Local perceptions of "therapeutic" value persist anecdotally, but quantitative assessments reveal no causal link to fertility enhancement or labor facilitation, as fistulas exacerbate the very infertility and social isolation the practice claims to mitigate. [8] Absence of efficacy is further inferred from fistula repair success rates, where gishiri-induced lesions require surgical correction without prior functional gains. [4]

### Key Studies and Data on Harms

A retrospective analysis of 80 cases of vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) in children under 13 years old in northern Nigeria identified 12 instances (15%) directly resulting from gishiri cutting, where incisions into the anterior vaginal wall inadvertently damaged the bladder or urethra. [4] This practice, often performed by traditional barbers using unsterilized razors or broken glass to address perceived vaginal narrowing or infections, carries risks of immediate hemorrhage and sepsis, as the cuts penetrate sensitive mucosal tissues without anesthesia or aseptic conditions. [3]

In a 2006 study from Birnin Kudu, northern Nigeria, gishiri cuts were documented as a contributing factor to VVF among women seeking repair, highlighting the procedure's role in non-obstetric fistula formation through iatrogenic trauma during self-treatment for gynecological complaints. [5] Broader epidemiological data indicate that gishiri incisions account for 5.68% to 18% of urinary fistulas in affected regions, underscoring the causal link between the cutting and chronic incontinence, dyspareunia, and social isolation due to persistent urinary leakage. [3] Long-term complications include vaginal scarring and stenosis, which exacerbate obstetric risks in subsequent pregnancies, though specific longitudinal studies on gishiri alone remain limited compared to broader female genital cutting research. [8]

Immediate post-procedure harms, observed in case series, encompass excessive bleeding, wound dehiscence, and urinary retention, often compounded by application of unverified herbal poultices that introduce bacterial contamination. [31] These findings, drawn from hospital-based records in high-prevalence areas like northern Nigeria, demonstrate gishiri's empirical harms outweigh any anecdotal therapeutic claims, with no controlled trials validating benefits against documented fistula induction. [9]

## Controversies and Viewpoints

### Classification as Female Genital Mutilation

Gishiri cutting involves the incision of the anterior vaginal wall using a sharpened instrument, typically performed without anesthesia to treat perceived infertility or obstructed labor in certain Hausa-Fulani communities in northern Nigeria and southern Niger. [3] This procedure is classified as Type IV female genital mutilation (FGM) by the World Health Organization (WHO), which defines Type IV as encompassing all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, including pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterizing. [38] The WHO's broader definition of FGM includes any procedure that involves partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons, explicitly covering vaginal incisions like Gishiri that cause harm without therapeutic benefit. [12]

Peer-reviewed literature consistently categorizes Gishiri cutting as a form of FGM practiced in Nigeria, alongside clitoridectomy and hymenectomy, noting its role in contributing to complications such as vesicovaginal fistulas. [15] For instance, a 2014 analysis by the Center for Arab Women Training and Research (CAWTAR) describes Gishiri incision as a specific variant of Type IV FGM, highlighting its cultural rationale but underscoring the associated health risks that align with FGM's harmful classification. [3] International health organizations, including WHO, emphasize that such practices lack empirical evidence of benefits and are sustained by cultural beliefs rather than medical necessity, justifying their inclusion under FGM to facilitate global efforts against non-therapeutic genital injuries. [38] [8]

While proponents in affected communities may view Gishiri cutting as a remedial intervention rather than mutilation, the classification prioritizes anatomical injury and absence of evidence-based efficacy, as evidenced by studies linking it to severe outcomes like genital fistulas without proven preventive value. [8] This stance reflects a consensus among medical bodies that any intentional non-medical alteration causing genital harm constitutes FGM, irrespective of cultural intent. [12] No major health authority disputes this categorization, though enforcement and awareness vary regionally. [15]

### Traditional Defenses vs. Modern Critiques

In Hausa communities of northern Nigeria and southern Niger, traditional defenses of gishiri cutting portray it as a vital ethnomedical intervention to address "gishiri disease," a culturally recognized condition involving perceived vaginal narrowing that manifests as dyspareunia, infertility, amenorrhea, genital prolapse, or obstructed labor. [36] [28] Practitioners, often traditional birth attendants or barbers using unsterilized blades, incise the anterior vaginal wall to enlarge the canal, claiming it facilitates easier delivery and restores fertility based on observed short-term relief in symptoms among women post-procedure. [3] These views are rooted in generational oral traditions and anecdotal successes, with the practice sustained by social pressures from husbands or in-laws attributing relational or reproductive issues to the untreated condition. [8]

Modern critiques, drawn from clinical epidemiology, reject these claims for lacking controlled evidence of efficacy, attributing reported benefits to transient mechanical widening or misattribution of unrelated recoveries rather than addressing root causes like infections, malnutrition-induced pelvic deformities, or cephalopelvic disproportion from early childbearing. [36] [8] Peer-reviewed analyses consistently link the procedure to iatrogenic harms, including hemorrhage, infection, scarring, and vesicovaginal fistulas, with contributions to fistula cases ranging from 5.68% to 18% in northern Nigerian series. [3] A review of 360 fistula patients in Niger found 10 cases (2.8%) directly resulting from analogous gurya cuttings performed for similar indications, all requiring reconstructive surgery for continence restoration, highlighting the procedure's net morbidity without biomedical validation. [8] Critics advocate alternative treatments, such as antibiotics for infections or cesarean sections for obstructions, as causally effective and less destructive. [36]

## Legal and Social Responses

### Legislation and Enforcement

In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act of 2015 (VAPP Act) criminalizes the performance of female circumcision or genital mutilation on any female child or woman, encompassing practices such as Gishiri cutting, classified under Type IV female genital mutilation (other harmful procedures to the female genitalia). [39] The Act imposes penalties of up to four years' imprisonment, a fine of at least ₦500,000 (approximately £700 as of 2022 exchange rates), or both, with higher sentences possible in states that have domesticated the law, such as up to 14 years in Imo State if death results. [39] [40] While federally applicable in the Federal Capital Territory, the VAPP requires adoption by individual states for broader enforcement, with 19 of 36 states having done so by 2022. [39]

Enforcement of the VAPP Act against FGM, including Gishiri cutting, has been limited, particularly in northern states like Kano and Sokoto where the practice is culturally entrenched among Hausa communities. No prosecutions or convictions for FGM were reported between June 2018 and March 2021, attributed to low reporting rates—often due to perpetrators being family members—and police tendencies to treat cases as private domestic matters rather than criminal offenses. [39] [41] Challenges include insufficient police training and resources, cultural resistance in Muslim-majority northern regions where 42% of women in the North West supported FGM continuation per 2018 data, and conflicts with customary or Sharia practices that prioritize family reconciliation over prosecution. [39] [42]

In Niger, where Gishiri cutting (locally termed gurya cutting) occurs among Hausa populations in southern regions, the Penal Code (Article 232.1) prohibits female genital mutilation as any assault on the female genital organs by total or partial excision, incision, or other harmful procedures, with penalties of two to five years' imprisonment and fines. [43] Enforcement remains sporadic, with overall FGM prevalence low (around 2% nationally per 2012 data, higher in border areas), but no specific prosecutions for Gishiri cutting documented, hindered by rural inaccessibility and traditional acceptance. [43] [44]

### Eradication Efforts and Challenges

International organizations such as UNFPA and UNICEF have implemented the Joint Programme on the Elimination of Female Genital Mutilation since 2008, targeting Nigeria among 18 high-prevalence countries through community engagement, comprehensive sexuality education, and norm-shifting initiatives, resulting in over 304,820 girls aged 0–14 protected from FGM and 1,394,813 individuals participating in elimination discussions by 2024. [45] In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act of 2015 criminalizes all forms of FGM, including Gishiri cutting—a Type IV procedure involving incision of the vaginal walls— with penalties up to life imprisonment for performers and facilitators. [46] The World Health Organization has supported national policies, multi-sectoral technical working groups, and awareness campaigns targeting health workers, policymakers, and communities to promote abandonment of harmful traditional practices like Gishiri, which is prevalent in northern regions such as the North-West zone where Type IV FGM accounts for significant cases. [47]

Local efforts include sensitization programs, lectures, and community dialogues led by NGOs and government agencies, particularly in southeastern Nigeria, where advocacy has involved religious and traditional leaders to challenge practices sustained by misconceptions about fertility and childbirth. [48] These initiatives emphasize empirical evidence of harms, such as increased risks of infection, hemorrhage, and chronic vaginal issues from unsterile Gishiri incisions, countering beliefs in its therapeutic value for obstructed labor. [47]

Challenges persist due to entrenched cultural norms viewing Gishiri as essential for marital eligibility and treatment of perceived vaginal narrowing, with family elders enforcing compliance amid social stigma against uncut women. [47] Enforcement of the 2015 VAPP Act remains uneven, as not all 36 states have domesticated it into local laws, limiting prosecutions in rural northern areas where Gishiri is covertly performed by traditional healers or even medicalized by some practitioners despite WHO prohibitions. [41] Broader barriers include poverty-driven secrecy, resistance to external interventions perceived as cultural imperialism, and insufficient data on Gishiri-specific prevalence, complicating targeted interventions amid overall FGM rates declining from 24.8% in 2008 to lower figures but still affecting millions. [49] [46]

## Prevalence and Trends

### Current Distribution and Rates

Gishiri cutting, a procedure involving incisions into the anterior vaginal wall, is primarily confined to specific ethnic groups in Niger and northern Nigeria, including Hausa, Zarma, and Songhai communities, where it is traditionally performed to address perceived reproductive issues such as obstructed labor or infertility. [8] [50] The practice is documented as a form of Type IV female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) under World Health Organization classifications, though it differs from more prevalent external genital alterations by targeting internal vaginal tissue. [51]

Specific prevalence data for gishiri cutting remains limited and is often subsumed within broader FGM/C statistics, as national surveys like Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) focus primarily on external procedures and may underreport internal cuttings due to respondent reluctance or interviewer challenges. [51] In Nigeria, where overall FGM/C prevalence among women aged 15-49 stood at 19.5% as of the 2018 DHS (with similar rates in subsequent analyses up to 2021), gishiri cuts were reported by 13% of women who had undergone any FGM/C, equating to an estimated low single-digit national incidence when extrapolated. [52] [51] This figure derives from self-reported data among FGM/C-affected women, highlighting gishiri as a less common variant compared to clitoridectomy or excision, which dominate in southern and central regions. [41]

In Niger, FGM/C prevalence is notably lower, at under 2% nationally among women aged 15-49 per 2012 DHS data, with slightly higher rates (up to 7.6%) in the Tillabéri region bordering Nigeria, where cross-border ethnic ties may sustain practices like gishiri. [53] A 2017 clinical review of obstetric fistula cases in Niger identified ten instances directly attributable to gishiri (locally termed gurya cutting), performed by traditional practitioners, indicating persistence in rural southwestern areas but at rates insufficient for population-level quantification in recent surveys. [8] No updated national statistics post-2012 specifically isolate gishiri, though broader FGM/C eradication efforts, including UNICEF and WHO initiatives, have contributed to stagnant or declining trends in the region. [54] Rural residence and low maternal education correlate with higher risk, mirroring patterns in adjacent Nigerian states like Sokoto and Kebbi. [39]

### Factors Sustaining or Reducing Practice

The persistence of Gishiri cutting among Hausa-Fulani communities in northern Nigeria stems from deeply held cultural beliefs that attribute obstructed labor, dyspareunia, and perceived vaginal narrowing—a condition termed "gishiri"—to supernatural or anatomical strictures requiring incision of the anterior vaginal wall for relief. [8] This therapeutic rationale, distinct from ritualistic forms of female genital cutting, prompts women to undergo the procedure from traditional barbers or healers using unsterile knives, often during pregnancy or after failed deliveries, reinforcing intergenerational transmission through family endorsement and lack of viable alternatives in remote areas. [2] Socioeconomic factors, including poverty and low literacy rates, exacerbate sustenance by limiting access to formal obstetric education, where symptoms of cephalo-pelvic disproportion are misdiagnosed as treatable by cutting rather than requiring medical intervention. [27]

Conversely, reductions in the practice are driven by heightened awareness of its complications, particularly vesicovaginal fistulas (VVF), with data from northern Nigerian hospitals indicating that Gishiri cuts account for up to 15% of pediatric VVF cases among girls under 13, often resulting from hemorrhage, infection, or tissue necrosis post-procedure. [4] [9] Legal measures, such as Nigeria's Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act of 2015, explicitly ban all female genital mutilation types—including Type IV incisions like Gishiri—with penalties including fines up to 100,000 naira or imprisonment, supplemented by state-level prohibitions enacted as early as 2000 in some northern regions. [55]

International and national campaigns by WHO, UNICEF, and UNFPA since 1997 have promoted evidence-based alternatives, such as skilled antenatal monitoring, timely hospital referrals, and cesarean sections for obstructed labor, reducing reliance on traditional methods by demonstrating superior outcomes in preventing fistulas without invasive vaginal scarring. [56] [57] Demographic trends further contribute to decline, with successive Nigeria Demographic and Health Surveys (2003–2018) revealing an overall drop in female genital cutting prevalence from 25% to approximately 20% among women aged 15–49, correlated with rising female secondary education (from 12% to 25% attendance) and urbanization, though Gishiri-specific rates remain underreported and higher in rural Hausa enclaves.
