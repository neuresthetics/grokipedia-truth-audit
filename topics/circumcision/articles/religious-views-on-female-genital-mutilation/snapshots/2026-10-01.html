Religious views on female genital mutilation

Fact-checked by Grok · 8 months ago
Female genital mutilation (FGM), also termed female genital cutting, refers to procedures that intentionally alter or cause injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons.[1] Religious views on FGM are not uniform across traditions and lack direct mandates in foundational scriptures of major faiths, yet the practice is often rationalized through religious justifications in affected communities, particularly in regions with predominant Muslim or animist populations in sub-Saharan Africa.[2] Empirical studies indicate higher prevalence among Muslims compared to Christians or adherents of traditional religions in countries like Burkina Faso, where religious identity correlates with support for continuation, though causation remains tied to intertwined cultural and familial factors rather than doctrinal imperatives.[2][3] In Islam, the Quran contains no explicit reference to FGM, and while certain hadiths describe it as an honorable act or sunnah in mild forms, contemporary scholarly opinions diverge, with organizations like the UNFPA emphasizing its cultural origins over religious necessity, and some authorities outright condemning it as un-Islamic.[4][5] Christian perspectives similarly reject FGM, absent from biblical texts, though historical practices persist among Coptic Christians in Egypt and Ethiopian Orthodox communities, where it coexists with male circumcision traditions despite missionary-led campaigns against it in the early 20th century.[6][7] Indigenous African religions and syncretic beliefs also invoke spiritual purification or rite-of-passage rationales, underscoring FGM's pre-Islamic and pre-Christian roots as a cultural norm perpetuated across faiths without universal theological endorsement.[8] Controversies arise from efforts to disentangle religion from the practice amid data showing familial and communal pressures reinforcing it, with peer-reviewed analyses highlighting religion's role in attitudes toward discontinuation rather than as a primary causal driver.[9][10]

Terminology and Classification

Definitions and Types

Female genital mutilation (FGM), also referred to as female genital cutting (FGC) in some contexts, encompasses all procedures that involve partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons.[11] The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies FGM into four types based on the extent of tissue removal or damage, emphasizing procedural distinctions to facilitate medical and epidemiological analysis rather than subjective cultural interpretations.[12] Type I, known as clitoridectomy, involves partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and/or the prepuce.[11] Type II, or excision, entails partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.[11] Type III, infibulation, is the most severe form and involves narrowing of the vaginal opening by cutting and repositioning the inner or outer labia, with or without removal of the clitoris.[11] Type IV includes all other non-therapeutic procedures, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, or cauterizing the genital area.[11] Empirical studies document significant health risks across these types, including immediate complications like hemorrhage (affecting up to 15% of cases in some cohorts), severe pain, shock, tetanus, sepsis, and transmission of infections such as HIV due to unsterile tools.[12][13] Long-term effects, confirmed in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, encompass chronic urinary tract infections, menstrual difficulties, keloid scar formation, decreased sexual function, and obstetric risks such as prolonged labor and higher neonatal mortality (with odds ratios up to 1.55 for perinatal death in Type III cases).[14][15] No credible medical evidence supports health benefits, despite cultural assertions in practicing communities that procedures reduce promiscuity or enhance hygiene; such claims lack causal substantiation and are contradicted by physiological data showing impaired genital function without compensatory gains.[12][16] The term "mutilation" highlights the irreversible harm and lack of consent, particularly when performed on minors, and is standard in international health frameworks like those of WHO and UNFPA to underscore non-therapeutic injury.[12][16] Practitioners and some advocates prefer "cutting" or "circumcision" to evoke parallels with male procedures and reduce stigma, but this framing obscures empirical evidence of disproportionate risks without medical justification.[17][18] These terminological preferences often reflect cultural relativism rather than procedural neutrality, as the practices involve excision of nerve-rich tissue leading to documented sensory loss.[19]

Cultural vs. Religious Framing

Female genital mutilation (FGM) is predominantly driven by cultural imperatives such as rites of passage into womanhood, enforcement of premarital chastity, and enhancement of marriageability within ethnic communities, rather than explicit religious mandates.[20] These motivations emphasize social conformity, family honor, and control over female sexuality to preserve patriarchal structures and tribal identities, with practices varying by ethnic group independently of dominant faiths.[21] Empirical surveys indicate that FGM persists as a marker of cultural continuity, often performed by traditional practitioners in non-medical settings to signify maturity and eligibility for marriage, irrespective of religious affiliation.[1] Prevalence data underscore the practice's transcendence of religious boundaries, occurring among animists, Christians, and Muslims alike, with rates more closely tied to ethnic traditions than doctrinal adherence. In Mali, where 89% of women aged 15-49 have undergone FGM, the procedure aligns with cultural notions of purity and social integration, even in a society that is over 95% Muslim, yet it shows no uniform correlation with levels of religiosity.[22] Similarly, in Burkina Faso, while prevalence is higher among Muslims (around 75%) than Christians (50%) or animists, all groups engage in it, primarily as an ethnic custom reinforcing community bonds rather than fulfilling scriptural requirements.[2] This variation highlights causal factors rooted in longstanding tribal norms and gender hierarchies, predating monotheistic influences and persisting where social pressures outweigh religious interpretations.[2] The notion that FGM is intrinsically linked to Islam lacks empirical support, as the practice shows higher rates among animist and Christian populations in certain African regions compared to many Muslim-majority areas. For instance, negligible FGM occurs in orthodox Islamic societies like Saudi Arabia or Pakistan, while it remains entrenched among non-Muslim ethnic groups in countries such as Ethiopia, where Orthodox Christians constitute the majority and prevalence exceeds 70% in some communities due to customary rites.[23] Such disparities reveal that FGM's persistence stems from embedded cultural mechanisms for regulating female autonomy and ensuring lineage purity, rather than any universal religious imperative, allowing rates to fluctuate with socioeconomic and ethnic dynamics over doctrinal consistency.[24]

Historical Origins

Ancient and Pre-Abrahamic Practices

Archaeological examinations of mummified remains from ancient Egypt, dating to approximately 2000 BCE, reveal evidence of clitoral excision and labial removal in prepubescent females, indicating the practice's antiquity prior to the emergence of Abrahamic faiths.[25] The Greek historian Herodotus, in his Histories composed around 440 BCE, explicitly described female genital cutting among Egyptians as a customary excision of the clitoris and sometimes labia, performed on girls before puberty to curb perceived excessive sexual desire.[26][6] This account, based on Herodotus's direct observations during his travels, corroborates the procedure's prevalence in Pharaonic society, where it functioned as a rite of passage tied to notions of purity and fertility control rather than theological mandate.[27] The custom's diffusion into sub-Saharan Africa occurred through pre-Islamic trade networks, migrations, and cultural exchanges along the Nile Valley and Red Sea corridors, as evidenced by ethnographic continuities in Nilotic and Cushitic groups predating Arab conquests.[28] Practices akin to Type II and III cuttings—removal of clitoris and labia, or infibulation—appear in East African pastoralist societies by the first millennium BCE, independent of Semitic religious influences, with linguistic and ritual parallels suggesting indigenous evolution from control mechanisms over female reproduction to maintain patrilineal inheritance and clan stability.[29] Empirical patterns link the rite to agrarian and nomadic economies where ensuring female fidelity reduced paternity uncertainty, a causal driver observable in non-religious contexts across prehistoric Eurasian and African analogs, though direct paleontological traces remain sparse.[30] In non-Abrahamic contexts, such as among ancient Ethiopian and Phoenician peoples referenced by Herodotus, the procedure aligned with broader initiation rituals emphasizing bodily modification for social cohesion, without scriptural endorsement, underscoring its secular origins in managing intergroup alliances and resource allocation via regulated sexuality.[16] This cultural persistence decoupled from divine imperatives allowed adaptation across diverse polities, from Egyptian temple economies to Saharan caravan trades, where the rite reinforced hierarchical gender norms empirically tied to survival in patrilocal systems.[31]

Spread in Africa and Middle East

Female genital mutilation (FGM) is concentrated in 30 African countries, where over 144 million girls and women alive today have undergone the procedure, alongside limited prevalence in Middle Eastern countries including Yemen, Oman, and Iraqi Kurdistan.[24][12] The practice's diffusion in these regions predates colonial eras and major Abrahamic influences, with archaeological and historical indications tracing it to ancient Egyptian civilizations, where ritual excision appears in temple reliefs and mummified remains from as early as 2000 BCE.[25][32] In sub-Saharan Africa, it spread via ethnic migrations and trade networks among indigenous groups, independent of Islam or Christianity, as evidenced by 19th-century European explorer accounts documenting the procedure among animist tribes in East Africa, such as the Maasai and Kikuyu.[33] In northeastern Africa, colonial records from British administrators in Egypt and Sudan in the mid-1800s noted FGM, including infibulation, among Coptic Christian communities and pagan Nilotic peoples, underscoring its embedding in local customs rather than religious doctrine.[34] Similarly, pre-Islamic Arabian societies on the peninsula exhibited forms of genital cutting, referenced in non-scriptural historical ethnographies, suggesting diffusion through Red Sea trade corridors linking Africa and the Middle East.[30] These patterns highlight non-religious drivers, with the practice persisting across faiths within ethnic enclaves. Empirical analyses reveal a stronger correlation between FGM and ethnic group membership than religious affiliation alone; for instance, in Burkina Faso, prevalence exceeds 70% among Mossi and Fulani ethnicities irrespective of Muslim, Christian, or animist dominance, pointing to inherited cultural norms over doctrinal imperatives.[2][35] This ethnic specificity facilitated its geographic entrenchment before external influences, as seen in higher rates along historical migration belts from the Nile Valley southward and eastward.

Views in Islam

Scriptural Basis in Quran and Hadith

The Quran contains no explicit references to female genital mutilation (FGM), nor does it issue any command, recommendation, or prohibition regarding the procedure.[36] This absence contrasts with the Quran's mentions of male circumcision in contexts such as ritual purity and covenant, as in Surah An-Nahl 16:123, which alludes to Abrahamic practices but specifies no female equivalent. Scholarly analyses confirm that primary Quranic exegesis, including tafsir works by classical commentators like Al-Tabari, yields no derivations supporting FGM as a religious obligation.[37] In Hadith literature, a narration attributed to Umm Atiyyah in Sunan Abi Dawud (5271) reports that a woman performing circumcisions in Medina was instructed by the Prophet Muhammad: "Do not cut severely as that is better for a woman and more desirable for a husband."[38] Some interpreters extend this to endorse a mild form of FGM (Type I, involving partial or total removal of the clitoral prepuce) as permissible or recommended for females, analogizing it to male khitan (circumcision), particularly since the Hadith uses the term "khitan" without gender restriction in related contexts.[39] However, this Hadith is classified as ahad (singularly narrated) and lacks the mutawatir (mass-transmitted) status that elevates male circumcision to a confirmed Sunnah practice.[40] The authenticity of such Hadiths is contested among Hadith scholars; for instance, Muhammad Nasiruddin al-Albani deemed the chain of narration in the Umm Atiyyah report weak (da'if) due to interruptions and unreliable transmitters, rendering it insufficient for deriving binding religious rulings.[41] Empirical review of major collections like Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim reveals no sahih (authentic) Hadiths explicitly mandating FGM, unlike the robust chains supporting male circumcision as fitrah (innate disposition) in Sahih Muslim 257. This disparity underscores that any scriptural endorsement for FGM remains tentative and non-obligatory, reliant on debated narrations rather than core prophetic tradition.[42]

Scholarly Endorsements and Oppositions

Some Islamic scholars, particularly from the Shafi'i and Hanbali schools of jurisprudence, have endorsed mild forms of female genital modification, classifying Type Ia—typically involving a symbolic prick or removal of the prepuce—as sunnah (recommended) or makrumah (honorable) to promote modesty and control desire, drawing on hadiths such as the narration attributed to Umm Atiyyah where the Prophet Muhammad reportedly advised a minor procedure on girls.[36] These endorsements emphasize that such practices must avoid harm and are not obligatory, distinguishing them from more invasive procedures deemed bid'ah (innovation) or impermissible due to potential mutilation.[43] Egypt's Dar al-Ifta, in its 2007 international conference proceedings, ruled that female circumcision is permissible and recommended (mustaḥabb) when limited to non-harmful forms, aligning with traditional views on purification and chastity.[44] Similarly, Indonesia's Majelis Ulama Indonesia (MUI) issued Fatwa No. 28/DSN-MUI/III/2003 and reaffirmed in subsequent statements that mild khitan perempuan (female circumcision), such as a superficial cut without removal, is makrumah and not forbidden, provided it adheres to health guidelines and avoids excess.[45] These positions reflect interpretations prioritizing prophetic traditions over explicit Quranic mandates, though reliant on hadiths of debated authenticity. In opposition, mainstream Islamic scholars and institutions, such as Dar al-Ifta and Al-Azhar, have declared FGM prohibited (haram) due to lack of explicit basis in the Quran and reliable Hadith, viewing it as a pre-Islamic tribal custom that violates the Islamic principle of no harm to the body.[41] Many Muslim countries, including Egypt and Sudan, have enacted laws opposing or banning the practice, reflecting this scholarly consensus against it.[46] Numerous contemporary fatwas reject all forms of female genital modification as lacking firm basis in the Quran or authentic Sunnah, classifying them as culturally driven rather than religiously mandated. The Fiqh Council of North America, in its June 2022 fatwa, declared all variants impermissible, citing absence of definitive textual evidence and potential violation of Islamic principles against harm (la darar wa la dirar).[47] Egypt's Dar al-Ifta reversed its earlier stance in a May 2018 ruling, deeming female genital mutilation (khitan al-untha) religiously forbidden (haram) due to documented health risks and incompatibility with Sharia's preservation of life and lineage.[48] Opposition often highlights the weak chain of transmission (isnad) for supporting hadiths and prioritizes maqasid al-Sharia (objectives of Islamic law), such as averting bodily harm, leading scholars like Yusuf al-Qaradawi to advocate abandonment of the practice entirely. While endorsements remain confined to Type Ia and explicitly exclude Types II and III (excision or infibulation) as excessive or un-Islamic, global fatwa bodies increasingly align against any procedure, reflecting empirical evidence of complications over traditional rationales.[49][37]

Sectarian and Regional Differences

Within Sunni Islam, endorsement of female genital mutilation varies by jurisprudential school, with higher support in traditions following the Shafi'i and Maliki schools, where it is often classified as recommended or obligatory, compared to the Hanafi school, which views it as honorable but not mandatory. In Southeast Asia, predominantly Shafi'i regions like Indonesia exhibit prevalence rates of approximately 49% among girls aged 0-14, typically involving mild Type I or IV procedures integrated with local adat customs rather than strict religious mandates.[50][51] In contrast, Shia Twelver jurisprudence generally does not prescribe female genital mutilation, viewing it as extraneous to sharia requirements, which correlates with lower overall prevalence in Shia-dominant areas.[52] For instance, while regional practices occur in parts of Shia-majority Iran, such as western provinces with rates up to 50% in specific communities, national figures remain far below those in Sunni-majority Sudan, where 87% of women aged 15-49 have undergone the procedure, often including severe infibulation (Type III).[53][54] Ismaili Shia communities show variation, with some subgroups like Dawoodi Bohras practicing a form of clitoral hood cutting, though broader Ismaili leadership emphasizes rejection of harmful traditions. Regional disparities further highlight cultural overlays on religious frameworks; in Sunni Hanafi-majority Turkey, prevalence is negligible despite Islamic predominance, absent from national health surveys and not entrenched in local customs.[55] This contrasts with high rates in adat-influenced Indonesia or infibulation-prevalent Sudan, underscoring that practice persistence owes more to ethnic and tribal norms than uniform doctrinal endorsement across Islam.[24]

Views in Christianity

Absence in Canonical Texts

The canonical texts of Christianity, consisting of the Old and New Testaments, contain no references to or endorsements of female genital mutilation (FGM) or any form of female genital alteration as a religious rite.[56] While the Old Testament prescribes male circumcision as a covenantal sign exclusively for males—detailed in Genesis 17:10–14, where God commands Abraham that every male among his descendants must be circumcised on the eighth day, and reiterated in Leviticus 12:3—there is no parallel directive for females. This specificity underscores a doctrinal focus on male-only physical circumcision, with female inclusion in the covenant achieved through familial or tribal affiliation rather than bodily modification.[6] In the New Testament, circumcision is further spiritualized, emphasizing inner transformation over external rites. Passages such as Romans 2:29 describe true circumcision as "of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the written code," and Colossians 2:11 refers to a "circumcision not performed by human hands," rejecting physical alterations as insufficient for spiritual purity. This shift aligns with Christianity's core theology, which prioritizes baptism and faith as initiatory sacraments, rendering additional genital modifications unnecessary and extraneous to salvation. No New Testament text addresses female genital practices, reinforcing the absence of any scriptural basis for FGM.[56] Early Church Fathers extended this scriptural silence by explicitly opposing voluntary bodily mutilations, viewing them as violations of the body's divine creation. For instance, Origen's self-castration, though an extreme personal interpretation of Matthew 19:12, was broadly condemned by subsequent church authorities as contrary to the integrity of the human form, with councils like the Council of Nicaea (325 CE) implicitly upholding the body's wholeness against such acts.[57] This patristic stance, rooted in the canonical emphasis on spiritual rather than corporeal purity, doctrinally precludes FGM, distinguishing it sharply from the limited endorsement of male circumcision under the old covenant.[58]

Historical Practices Among Christian Communities

In regions of Africa where Christianity took root amid longstanding indigenous customs, female genital mutilation (FGM) persisted among some Christian communities through cultural syncretism, independent of theological mandates. Among Coptic Christians in Egypt, FGM was practiced at high rates comparable to those among Muslims, embedded in familial and communal identity as a rite of passage predating modern scrutiny; ethnographic data from Upper Egypt, such as Minya, indicate its prevalence among Christians until at least the late 20th century, though exact pre-1900 figures are sparse due to limited records.[3][59] Similarly, in Ethiopia, Ethiopian Orthodox Christians in certain highland and pastoralist areas incorporated FGM into traditional initiation ceremonies, with historical accounts noting its continuity as a tribal norm rather than a liturgical requirement, affecting up to 74% of women in some Orthodox-dominated regions by the mid-20th century before church-led reforms.[60][61] Christian missionary efforts in the early 20th century actively opposed FGM, framing it as incompatible with biblical teachings on the body. In colonial Kenya, Protestant missionaries, particularly from the Church of Scotland and Anglican missions, initiated campaigns against Kikuyu female circumcision starting in 1929, denouncing it as pagan and harmful; this sparked the "female circumcision controversy," where missionaries withheld sacraments from participants, leading to schisms but contributing to a measurable decline—subsequent analyses estimate mission exposure reduced FGM prevalence by 10-20% in affected areas over decades through education and alternative rites.[7][62] In Christian-majority South Sudan, FGM endures in select ethnic groups like the Bari and Murle due to entrenched tribal customs emphasizing purity and marriageability, overriding denominational doctrines; prevalence remains low nationally (under 5% per limited surveys), but higher in rural Christian communities where syncretic practices blend ancestral rites with catechism, unaffected by scriptural absence of endorsement.[63][64] These instances underscore FGM's roots in pre-colonial ethnic traditions, with Christianity serving variably as a vector for resistance rather than propagation.[65]

Modern Ecclesiastical Positions

In April 2021, Coptic Pope Tawadros II of the [Coptic Orthodox Church](/page/Coptic_Orthodox Church) issued a formal declaration rejecting female genital mutilation (FGM), affirming a zero-tolerance policy toward the practice as part of broader commitments to combat violence against women.[66] This stance aligns with the church's emphasis on protecting human dignity, viewing FGM as incompatible with Christian teachings on the sanctity of the body.[67] Pope Francis of the Roman Catholic Church has repeatedly condemned FGM as an affront to human dignity and a violation of women's rights, describing it in 2022 as a practice that must be eradicated due to its harmful effects on the female body, which Catholic doctrine regards as a temple of the Holy Spirit.[68] In a November 2022 address, he explicitly labeled FGM a "crime" that undermines equality and opportunity, urging global action rooted in moral imperatives over cultural traditions.[69] The Catholic Church's position frames opposition as essential to upholding the inherent value of persons, independent of non-therapeutic mutilation. Anglican leaders have similarly opposed FGM through ecumenical efforts, with churches in regions like Kenya and Tanzania collaborating on campaigns to eradicate it, citing biblical principles of wholeness and justice.[70] Official Anglican synods and bishops' statements integrate anti-FGM advocacy into broader initiatives against gender-based violence, prioritizing scriptural calls to protect the vulnerable.[71] Evangelical missions have contributed to FGM's decline in parts of Africa, including through doctrinal prohibitions that emphasize bodily integrity as per New Testament teachings, with historical missionary activities correlating with reduced prevalence in mission-influenced areas.[7] In Chad, evangelical churches have highlighted FGM's health risks in official agreements, promoting alternatives aligned with Christian ethics.[72] While some African Pentecostal communities exhibit cultural tolerance for milder forms due to syncretism, major Pentecostal denominations officially denounce all FGM variants as contrary to doctrines of creation and redemption.[73]

Views in Judaism

References in Jewish Texts

Rabbinic Jewish texts, including the Torah and Talmud, make no reference to female genital mutilation (FGM) as a religious obligation or ritual practice comparable to male circumcision (brit milah). The biblical covenant of circumcision is explicitly mandated for males as a perpetual sign of the Abrahamic covenant (Genesis 17:10–14), with no parallel procedure prescribed for females. This male-specific emphasis persists throughout rabbinic literature, where extensive discussions of circumcision rituals, timing, and exemptions apply solely to boys, underscoring the absence of any normative female equivalent. The Talmud's tractate Yevamot (60b) addresses virginity verification in the context of priestly marriage eligibility, describing a non-invasive test involving exposure to wine vapors to detect prior intercourse via scent absorption differences, but it neither endorses nor describes any form of genital cutting as a ritual or proof mechanism.[74] Such discussions focus on legal status and halakhic fitness rather than mutilatory interventions, reflecting a broader rabbinic silence on FGM absent in Egyptian or other cultural precedents occasionally noted by ancient commentators.[6] Medieval codifiers like Maimonides (Rambam) in Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Milah) elaborate on male circumcision as the covenantal marker, detailing procedures, ages, and spiritual rationales—such as curbing excessive lust—while remaining entirely silent on any female genital modification. This omission aligns with the gendered covenantal framework in Jewish law, where female inclusion in the covenant occurs through other means, such as immersion or familial status, without physical alteration of genitalia.[75] Empirically, the lack of textual endorsement correlates with zero documented prevalence of FGM in mainstream Ashkenazi and Sephardi Jewish communities across history, from medieval Europe to modern Israel and diaspora, where practices remain confined to male ritual circumcision.[6] Scholarly analyses confirm that rabbinic Judaism rejects FGM as extraneous to halakhah, viewing it as incompatible with principles of bodily integrity outside explicit commandments.[76]

Practices Among Specific Jewish Groups

Among the Beta Israel (Ethiopian Jews), ritual female genital surgery—typically involving partial or total clitoridectomy classified as Type I or II FGM—was a normative cultural practice in Ethiopia prior to mass immigration to Israel. A 1997 study examining 113 immigrant Beta Israel women found physical evidence of such surgery in approximately one-third, with 27% showing total or partial clitoral amputation and varied lesions indicating heterogeneous procedures.[77] This rite, performed around puberty or earlier, stemmed from ethnic traditions emphasizing sexual restraint and marriageability, not from interpretations of Jewish religious texts.[6] Post-immigration via Operations Moses (1984–1985, airlifting about 8,000) and Solomon (1991, airlifting over 14,000), the practice ceased rapidly among Beta Israel in Israel. A 2012 analysis confirmed that while severe FGM was universal among Ethiopian-born women, every interviewed Beta Israel woman planned to forgo it for daughters, attributing abandonment to societal integration, education, and exposure to mainstream Jewish norms rejecting mutilation.[78] This cultural shift underscores the import of Ethiopian customs rather than any enduring religious imperative, with no resurgence reported in subsequent data. Rabbinic authorities regard FGM as incompatible with halakha, prohibiting unnecessary bodily harm (e.g., akin to Deuteronomy 23:2's restrictions on mutilation) absent scriptural mandate, which is entirely lacking for females.[6] Israel's Chief Rabbinate, overseeing religious standards, has issued no endorsements and aligns with broader Jewish condemnation of the practice as non-halakhic, reinforcing its opposition through general rulings against elective genital alterations beyond male brit milah.[6] Isolated Type IV procedures (e.g., symbolic pricking) have occasionally surfaced in fringe communities influenced by adjacent Arab Bedouin customs, but these remain rare, culturally driven, and disavowed within Judaism.[78]

Views in Indigenous and Other Religions

African Traditional Religions

In many African traditional religions, particularly animist belief systems prevalent in West and East Africa prior to widespread Abrahamic influences, female genital mutilation (FGM) serves as a foundational rite of passage integrating girls into womanhood and communal spiritual frameworks. Among the Maasai of Kenya and Tanzania, excision—typically Type II involving removal of the clitoris and labia minora—is conducted on prepubescent girls during elaborate initiation ceremonies known as Emuratare, symbolizing maturity, eligibility for marriage, and alignment with ancestral and fertility spirits.[79] [80] This practice, documented ethnographically since the early 20th century, underscores endurance of ritual pain as a test of character essential for social and spiritual roles within matrilineal clan structures.[33] Traditional rationales for FGM in these animist contexts emphasize preservation of chastity, enhancement of fertility, and safeguarding clan purity against spiritual impurities associated with unaltered genitalia. Beliefs hold that the procedure curbs excessive sexual desire, thereby ensuring marital fidelity and appeasing deities tied to reproduction and lineage continuity; for instance, uncut females are viewed as susceptible to malevolent spirits that could disrupt communal harmony or agricultural yields linked to fertility cults.[81] [82] These convictions reinforce hierarchical gender dynamics, where the ritual's infliction of permanent alteration causally binds women to patriarchal oversight through embodied markers of compliance and group identity.[33] Prevalence data from UNICEF surveys reveal that in regions with strong adherence to indigenous animist practices, such as parts of Kenya and Tanzania inhabited by pastoralist groups like the Maasai, FGM rates historically approached universality, with over 90% of women in affected communities reporting undergoing the procedure before anti-FGM interventions gained traction in the 21st century.[83] Similarly, in West African traditional settings, ethnographic accounts from the early 2000s indicate 80-98% adherence among non-converted populations, tying the rite directly to seasonal initiation cycles synchronized with lunar or harvest fertility observances.[24] [84] These empirical patterns highlight FGM's embedded role in sustaining cosmological worldviews where bodily modification ritually mediates human-spirit interactions and enforces endogamous purity.[33]

Animist and Tribal Contexts

In non-African animist and tribal societies, female genital cutting remains exceedingly rare, with ethnographic records documenting only minor variants such as symbolic pricking or nicking of the clitoral hood, often classified as WHO Type IV procedures, rather than excision or infibulation. Among indigenous groups like the Dayak of Borneo, Indonesia, no widespread evidence exists for ritual female genital alteration independent of Islamic influences, though isolated reports describe pre-Islamic symbolic rituals tied to animist beliefs in spiritual purification and transition to womanhood. Similarly, Australian Aboriginal communities exhibit no documented tradition of female genital cutting; male subincision rituals predominate, emphasizing totemic and initiatory symbolism without female analogs.[85][86] Syncretic contexts, where animist cosmologies merge with Islamic or Christian elements, occasionally elevate these minor rituals into markers of communal identity, framing them as bulwarks against erosion by modernity rather than harmful mutilation. In such blends, practitioners rationalize the act as safeguarding ancestral purity, fertility rites, or social harmony, attributing spiritual efficacy to bloodletting for appeasing animist spirits or aligning with monotheistic hygiene norms. For example, in Indonesia's diverse ethnic tapestry, animist-influenced communities in regions like Kalimantan integrate pricking ceremonies with Islamic sunat perempuan, viewing persistence as resistance to Western secularism and preservation of ethnic cohesion.[87][88] This preservationist ethos underscores a causal logic among adherents: unperformed rituals risk spiritual disequilibrium or social ostracism, embedding the practice in intergenerational transmission of worldview against globalizing pressures. Empirical surveys in syncretic Asian settings reveal motivations centered on cultural continuity, with 86% of Indonesian respondents in one study citing tradition as primary, independent of doctrinal mandates. Such views contrast external characterizations of mutilation, prioritizing empirical continuity of lineage beliefs over biomedical critiques.[50]

Contemporary Religious Debates

Reform Efforts and Fatwas

In Islamic contexts, reform efforts have included fatwas explicitly prohibiting all forms of female genital mutilation (FGM). In Indonesia, female religious leaders issued declarations in the early 2020s deeming non-medical FGM haram (forbidden), emphasizing its lack of basis in Islamic texts and calling for its abandonment.[89] The Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) highlighted FGM as a form of violence against women in its 2018 Women and Development Report, urging member states to address it through education and legal measures, with ongoing advocacy under the OIC-2025 Programme of Action.[90][91] However, positions vary; while global fatwas post-2020 increasingly reject FGM outright, some scholars, including earlier Al-Azhar interpretations, have permitted minimal procedures as cultural rather than religious obligations, though institutional shifts toward prohibition have intensified amid international pressure.[92] Christian initiatives have featured interfaith collaborations against FGM. In Egypt, Coptic Pope Tawadros II signed declarations on April 16, 2021, committing to zero tolerance for FGM, alongside support for family planning, in partnership with national efforts to combat the practice prevalent among both Muslim and Christian communities.[93] These statements framed FGM as incompatible with religious teachings on human dignity, contributing to broader religious mobilization. In Judaism, Israeli programs have targeted FGM prevention among Ethiopian Jewish immigrants, where the practice was historically reported in originating communities. Government submissions to the UN in 2024 affirmed Israel's zero-tolerance policy, with awareness campaigns and legal protections integrated into immigrant integration, leading to reported discontinuation among these groups post-immigration.[76][94] Empirical data show declines linked to religious leader involvement. In Kenya and Tanzania, FGM prevalence among women aged 15-49 fell from 32.1% to 21.0% in Kenya and from 10.0% to 7.5% in Tanzania between 2008 and 2022, attributed partly to community sensitization by faith leaders challenging cultural-religious justifications, though resistance persists in rural areas where endorsements of "mild" forms linger.[95][96] These efforts reflect partial success, as religious endorsements of FGM continue in some sects despite fatwas, underscoring the tension between doctrinal reform and entrenched traditions.[97]

International Religious Campaigns Against FGM

Since the 2010s, the UNFPA-UNICEF Joint Programme on the Elimination of Female Genital Mutilation has collaborated with religious leaders across Africa and the Middle East to frame opposition to the practice as consistent with faith teachings, establishing networks of imams, priests, and traditional influencers who issue public declarations against it.[98][99] These efforts, active in at least 18 focus countries as of recent phases targeting Sustainable Development Goal 5.3 by 2030, emphasize zero-tolerance stances through interfaith dialogues and mosque/church-based sensitization, with over 1,000 religious figures trained annually in select regions by 2022.[100][101] In Yemen, religious and community leaders convened in 2023 workshops to debunk myths linking FGM to Islamic requirements, resulting in unified fatwas and youth pledges against the practice in high-prevalence governorates.[102] Similar ecumenical initiatives in Djibouti mobilized imams for municipal declarations abandoning FGM entirely, integrating anti-practice messaging into sermons and reaching thousands via radio broadcasts.[103] These campaigns prioritize local faith authority to counter entrenched norms, with documented shifts in attitudes among adherents where religious endorsements explicitly denounce all forms. Verifiable impacts include prevalence reductions among younger cohorts; in Egypt, the share of girls aged 0-14 undergoing FGM fell from 74% in 2008 to under 10% in projections by 2020, with UNICEF attributing part of this 20-30% generational drop to religious leader advocacy amplifying legal bans and health education.[104][105] However, such outcomes face critiques from anthropologists and local voices who contend the campaigns impose Western biomedical standards, sidelining culturally defended claims of hygiene or modesty benefits in milder forms (Type I or II), despite empirical evidence from WHO classifications showing no net health advantages and elevated risks of infection, hemorrhage, and psychological trauma.[106][107] These perspectives highlight tensions between universal human rights framing and contextual relativism, though longitudinal studies confirm sustained harm profiles across procedure types.[7]
