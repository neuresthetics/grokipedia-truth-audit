# Prevalence of female genital mutilation

Female genital mutilation (FGM), defined as the partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to female genital organs for non-therapeutic reasons, affects over 230 million girls and women alive today, according to household survey data compiled by UNICEF and WHO. [1] [2] This figure represents a 15% rise from 2016 estimates, driven primarily by population growth in high-prevalence regions rather than accelerating incidence rates. [1] The practice is concentrated in 30 countries where prevalence among women aged 15-49 exceeds 1%, predominantly across sub-Saharan Africa (over 144 million cases), North Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia (over 80 million cases), though evidence of occurrence extends to 94 countries when including lower-prevalence or diaspora communities. [3] [4] Prevalence rates vary sharply by country, exceeding 90% in nations like Guinea, Somalia, Djibouti, and Sierra Leone based on Demographic and Health Surveys, while urban-rural divides and generational declines—such as daughters of cut women being 30% less likely to undergo the procedure—indicate uneven progress amid persistent cultural entrenchment. [1] [2] Despite international bans and campaigns, FGM persists due to social norms enforcing conformity, with migration introducing risks to low-prevalence areas in Europe, North America, and beyond, where underreporting hampers precise tracking. [2] Empirical data from repeated cross-sectional surveys underscore that while absolute numbers grow with demographics, targeted interventions have reduced type III procedures (infibulation) in some cohorts, highlighting causal links between education, legal enforcement, and attitude shifts over blanket prohibitions. [5]

## Definitions and Classifications

### WHO Types of FGM

The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies female genital mutilation (FGM) into four types based on the extent of tissue removal or other injury to the external female genitalia, providing a standardized framework for data collection and analysis across regions. This classification, developed to facilitate consistent reporting and assessment of prevalence, distinguishes procedures by anatomical severity, though variations exist in practice and documentation. [6]

Type I (clitoridectomy) involves the partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and/or the prepuce (clitoral hood). Subtype Ia entails removal of the prepuce only, while Ib includes the clitoral glans with the prepuce. This form is among the less extensive cuttings but still results in permanent alteration of sensitive tissue. [6]

Type II (excision) comprises partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. Subtypes include IIa (labia minora only), IIb (clitoral glans and labia minora, possibly affecting prepuce), and IIc (adding labia majora). Type II represents a more invasive procedure than Type I and predominates in many practicing communities alongside Type I. [6]

Type III (infibulation or pharaonic circumcision) is the most severe, involving narrowing of the vaginal opening through cutting and repositioning of the labia minora (IIIa) or majora (IIIb), often with removal of the clitoral glans; the raw edges are then stitched or held together to form scar tissue over the opening, leaving only a small aperture for urine and menstrual flow. Globally, Type III accounts for a minority of cases but reaches 80-90% prevalence in countries of the Horn of Africa, such as Somalia and Djibouti, where it is the dominant form. [6] [3]

Type IV encompasses all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical reasons, including pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, or cauterization of the clitoris or surrounding tissue. These forms are often less invasive and may involve symbolic nicking without significant tissue removal, contributing to challenges in data collection as they are frequently underreported or not recognized as FGM due to cultural normalization of minimal interventions. The WHO classification thus aids in capturing such variations, though reliance on self-reported surveys can lead to inconsistencies, particularly for Type IV, where severity thresholds influence disclosure. [6] [2]

### Variations in Practice and Reporting

Practices of female genital mutilation (FGM) exhibit significant regional variations in severity and form, ranging from extensive excision and infibulation (WHO Type III) to symbolic pricking or nicking (Type IV), which directly impacts how prevalence is documented and compared across contexts. In sub-Saharan Africa, where the majority of severe cases occur, Type III procedures— involving narrowing of the vaginal opening through stitching—account for a substantial proportion, particularly in countries like Somalia (up to 98% of cases) and Sudan, complicating eradication efforts due to associated health risks such as obstetric complications. [7] In contrast, Asian practices, such as those in Indonesia and among the Dawoodi Bohra community in India, predominantly involve milder interventions classified as Type IV or limited Type I, including superficial incisions or clitoral hood nicks without significant tissue removal, which are often performed in infancy with minimal immediate harm but still fall under international FGM definitions. [8]

These procedural differences contribute to reporting inconsistencies, as broad definitional inclusion of minimal forms can elevate prevalence figures in surveys while understating comparative severity. For instance, in Indonesia, national data indicate that approximately 49% of girls aged 0-14 have undergone FGC, yet ethnographic studies describe most as symbolic rituals involving a drop of blood via pricking, leading critics to argue that such inclusions inflate global estimates without reflecting equivalent harm levels found in African excisional practices. [8] [9] Similarly, in the Bohra khatna practice, which entails prepuce removal akin to Type Ia, community members frequently describe it as a harmless "sunna" rite rather than mutilation, resulting in underreporting in self-administered surveys due to cultural normalization. [10]

Self-reporting further exacerbates discrepancies, as respondents often distinguish "sunna" (milder cuttings) from more invasive forms, misaligning with clinical classifications and leading to underestimation of severity. A cross-sectional study in Sudan found that while 71% of women self-reported "sunna" FGM, clinical examinations revealed that 18% had undergone Type III infibulation, highlighting how perceptual biases toward less harmful self-views distort data reliability. [11] Such gaps hinder cross-regional comparisons, as Asian prevalence rates—dominated by non-excisional types—appear high under inclusive metrics but mask lower complication rates relative to Africa's higher incidence of Types I-III, where systematic reviews confirm over 80% of cases involve excision or infibulation in endemic zones. [7] [11] This variability underscores the need for standardized verification methods beyond self-reports to align reported figures with actual procedural impacts.

## Global Estimates

### Current Worldwide Figures

As of 2024, UNICEF estimates that more than 230 million girls and women alive today have undergone female genital mutilation (FGM), based on data from nationally representative household surveys in 94 countries. [1] This figure marks a 15% increase from the 200 million estimated in 2016, driven largely by population growth in affected regions rather than higher rates of the practice. [12]

Africa bears the heaviest burden, with over 144 million girls and women affected, accounting for the largest share of global cases. [3] Asia follows with more than 80 million cases, predominantly in countries like Indonesia and Malaysia, while the Middle East contributes a smaller but notable portion; prevalence in other continents remains below 1% of the total. [3] [2]

The vast majority of cases—over 90%—are concentrated in approximately 30 countries where prevalence among women aged 15-49 exceeds 1%, with data derived primarily from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) conducted between 2010 and 2023. [1] These core countries include Egypt, Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Sudan in Africa, as well as Indonesia in Asia, where rates range from 20% to over 90% in certain populations. [2]

### Geographic Distribution

Female genital mutilation exhibits extreme concentration in sub-Saharan Africa, where over 144 million girls and women aged 15-49 have undergone the procedure, accounting for the majority of the global total of more than 230 million cases reported in 2024. [3] The highest national prevalence rates, exceeding 90%, are recorded in Somalia at 99%, Guinea at 95%, and Djibouti at 90%, based on demographic health surveys from 2020 and earlier data integrated into UNICEF's 2024 estimates. [13] [1] These hotspots reflect entrenched regional practices, with prevalence often approaching universality in affected ethnic groups and communities. [14]

## Trends and Projections

### Historical Changes in Prevalence Rates

Empirical data from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) indicate gradual declines in female genital mutilation (FGM) prevalence rates in several high-prevalence countries since the late 1990s, though the pace has been slow and uneven. In Ethiopia, rates among women aged 15-49 fell from 79.9% in 2000 to 74.3% in 2005 and 65.2% in 2016, reflecting a modest overall reduction of approximately 15 percentage points over 16 years. [17] Similarly, in Kenya, prevalence dropped from 37.6% in 1998 to 32.2% in 2003, 27.1% in 2008-09, and 21% in 2014, with further decline to 15% by 2022. [17] [18] These trends align with broader regional patterns; for instance, in East Africa, FGM prevalence among girls aged 0-14 decreased from 71.4% in 1995 to 8.0% in 2016, signaling sharper intergenerational shifts compared to overall adult rates. [19]

Such rate reductions are primarily linked to socioeconomic factors including urbanization and expanded female education, which correlate with lower support for the practice. Urban areas consistently report lower prevalence—13.8% versus 25.9% rural in Kenya (2014)—while maternal education beyond none reduces odds significantly, as seen in Ethiopia where higher-educated women show decreased transmission to daughters (from 56.2% in 2000 to 16.7% in 2016). [17] Anti-FGM initiatives, intensified post-2000 through UN-led campaigns involving community dialogues, legal prohibitions (e.g., Kenya's 2011 Act), and alternative rites, have contributed to attitude shifts, particularly among younger cohorts and in peri-urban settings. [17] Pre-2000 baselines, drawn from early DHS data, often hovered near 80% in core Ethiopian regions, with minimal change until sustained interventions.

Despite these rate declines, absolute numbers of FGM cases have not decreased proportionally due to rapid population growth, especially Africa's youth bulge, which expands the at-risk cohort. In high-prevalence nations, slow per-capita reductions are offset by demographic expansion, resulting in stable or rising totals; for example, while Ethiopian rates edged down, the affected female population grew alongside national demographics. [20] Globally, this dynamic has driven an estimated 15% increase in lifetime FGM cases since earlier benchmarks, underscoring that prevalence metrics alone understate ongoing scale. [1]

### Future Risks and Population Impacts

Projections from the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) estimate that 68 million girls will undergo female genital mutilation (FGM) by 2030 if current practice levels continue unchanged, reflecting the inertia of entrenched cultural norms amid demographic expansion. [21] This figure accounts for birth cohorts in high-prevalence countries, where FGM persists across 30+ nations primarily in Africa and the Middle East, with additional risks in Asia. [1]

Causal drivers of these risks include rapid population growth and elevated fertility rates in FGM-practicing regions, which generate millions of girls annually susceptible to the procedure and offset any localized rate declines. [1] Sub-Saharan Africa's population is projected to increase by over 50% by 2050, concentrating new cases in high-burden areas like Somalia (prevalence exceeding 95%) and Guinea (96%), as well as Mali (~89%) and Sudan (~87%), where total affected individuals have risen 15% since 2016 estimates due to cohort effects and expansion. In these countries, 2023 World Bank data indicate total fertility rates of 6.1 for Somalia, 4.2 for Guinea, 5.6 for Mali, and approximately 4.3 for Sudan births per woman, contributing to population growth that sustains FGM cases despite prevalence reductions. [22] [23] Such dynamics imply millions of incident cases yearly, sustaining absolute prevalence despite per-capita reductions in select communities. [7]

Optimistic narratives of near-eradication by 2030 are critiqued for underestimating risks through incomplete accounting, particularly the exclusion of Type IV FGM—encompassing symbolic acts like genital pricking or scraping—from many projections and surveys. [2] WHO classifies Type IV as FGM, yet household-based data collection often focuses on excision (Types I-III), underreporting milder forms that comprise up to 10-20% of practices in regions like Indonesia and Malaysia, thereby inflating apparent progress and masking cultural persistence. [7] [24] This omission perpetuates intergenerational transmission, with population-level health impacts including heightened maternal mortality and chronic complications projected to burden healthcare systems in growing demographics. [25]

## Influencing Factors

### Cultural and Social Drivers

Female genital mutilation (FGM) persists in certain communities as a mechanism to enforce social norms around female sexuality, particularly by promoting ideals of purity and chastity that enhance marriage prospects in patriarchal structures. Ethnographic research in African societies reveals that the procedure is often framed as a prerequisite for marriageability, with uncut women stigmatized as promiscuous or undesirable, thereby reducing their eligibility for unions and associated bride wealth exchanges. [26] This control over female sexuality aligns with broader gender hierarchies where male lineage security depends on perceived fidelity, as documented in qualitative studies from regions like Somalia and Kenya. [27] [28]

Intergenerational transmission reinforces these norms, with mothers—who comprise the primary decision-makers in many households—overwhelmingly subjecting daughters to FGM to maintain family honor and avoid social ostracism. Surveys in high-prevalence settings, such as Somalia, indicate that among women aged 15-49 who underwent FGM, a substantial majority (often exceeding 80%) favor its continuation for their daughters, driven by peer expectations and the fear of daughters being excluded from community rites or marriage markets. [29] This pattern, observed in longitudinal data from Demographic and Health Surveys, underscores how maternal agency, shaped by lived experience, perpetuates the practice despite external pressures for abandonment. [30]

Economic incentives further entrench FGM through the specialized role of traditional cutters, typically elderly women who derive income, status, and ritual authority from performing the procedure. In communities like those in Cameroon's Ejagham region, bans encounter resistance from practitioners who view eradication as a threat to their livelihoods, with ethnographic accounts highlighting how cutters mobilize social networks to preserve their economic niche. [31] Economic shocks, such as droughts in sub-Saharan Africa, have been shown to temporarily reduce FGM incidence by straining household resources for ceremonies, illustrating the practice's embeddedness in resource-dependent social economies. [32]

### Religious and Traditional Justifications

In certain Islamic traditions, particularly among Sunni scholars, hadiths are invoked to justify female circumcision as a sunnah or honorable practice for women, typically described as a mild removal of the prepuce without severe mutilation. For example, a narration in Sunan Abu Dawud states that the Prophet Muhammad recommended circumcision for women as an "honor" but cautioned against excess, aligning with interpretations in the Shafi'i school of jurisprudence that view it as obligatory or recommended to promote chastity and hygiene. These texts, while not prescribing the infibulation or clitoridectomy prevalent in regions like the Horn of Africa, provide a religious rationale cited by practitioners to endorse cutting as fulfilling prophetic tradition, despite debates over hadith authenticity and chains of transmission in sources like Sahih Muslim. [33]

Empirical patterns reinforce a correlation between Sunni Islamic adherence and higher FGM prevalence in Africa, where rates exceed 80% in Muslim-majority areas of the Sahel and Horn, such as Mali (91% overall, predominantly Sunni) and Sudan (87%), compared to under 20% among coexisting Christian or animist groups in the same countries. [34] In Burkina Faso, for instance, FGM prevalence stands at 75% among Muslims versus 18% among Catholics, suggesting religious endorsement sustains the practice beyond cultural inertia, even as Christian missions historically reduced it through opposition campaigns. [35] [36] Shia Islam, by contrast, lacks such hadith endorsements and explicitly rejects FGM, with negligible prevalence in Shia-dominant regions like Iran (under 1% nationally, though pockets exist in Sunni-influenced provinces), highlighting doctrinal variance as a causal factor in distribution. [37]

Traditional justifications predate Islam, tracing to ancient Egyptian or tribal African rites symbolizing purity and maturity, as evidenced by mummification artifacts suggesting ritual cutting around 2000 BCE. [38] However, in the Sahel, where animist practices once prevailed, FGM persistence aligns with historical Islamization from the 8th century onward, as Islamic norms integrated and amplified pre-existing customs, evidenced by elevated rates in Islamized ethnic groups like the Fulani (over 90% in northern Nigeria) versus lower persistence among non-Islamized neighbors. [34] This fusion underscores how religious frameworks have causally entrenched the rite, countering claims of pure cultural autonomy. [39]

## Data Reliability

### Survey Methodologies

Primary data on the prevalence of female genital mutilation (FGM) are derived from large-scale, nationally representative household surveys, particularly the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), which have been conducted periodically since the 1990s in approximately 31 countries where the practice is concentrated, mainly in Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia. [40] [3] These surveys target ever-married women aged 15-49 years, querying them directly about whether they themselves have undergone FGM, the type performed (if any), and the circumcision status of their living daughters under age 15, enabling estimates of both current prevalence among adult women and the extent of ongoing transmission to younger generations. [41] [42] Standardized questionnaires across surveys facilitate cross-country and temporal comparisons, with sample sizes often exceeding 5,000-10,000 households per country to ensure statistical robustness and subnational breakdowns where feasible. [40]

While reliant on self-reports, which introduce potential underreporting due to social desirability bias, the methodologies incorporate interviewer training on sensitive topics and proxy reporting for daughters to capture intergenerational data without direct examination. [41] Validation efforts, though infrequent, have included clinical examinations to corroborate self-reports; for instance, a 2006 cross-sectional study in Sudan involving 2,565 women found that self-reported absence of FGM aligned with clinical findings in 89% of cases, while agreement on the specific type among those reporting cutting was 66%, indicating moderate to high reliability for detecting presence overall. [11]

Advancements in survey implementation have included the adoption of digital tools, such as tablet-based computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI), which recent DHS and MICS rounds (post-2010, with continued refinement through 2023) use to enable real-time data validation, reduce transcription errors, and facilitate access to remote or nomadic populations by minimizing paper-based logistics and enhancing enumerator mobility. [42] These electronic systems also support audio-computer-assisted self-interviewing (ACASI) options for highly sensitive questions, potentially lowering underreporting by allowing private responses, though their impact on FGM-specific data quality requires further empirical assessment. [42]

### Limitations and Criticisms

Self-reported data, the primary basis for most female genital mutilation (FGM) prevalence estimates from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), are vulnerable to social desirability bias, where respondents deny or minimize experiences due to associated stigma, particularly in urban settings with heightened anti-FGM campaigns. [43] This underreporting is estimated to affect 20-30% of cases in such areas, as women may conceal procedures performed in infancy or classify minor interventions as non-FGM. [11] Type IV FGM, encompassing symbolic pricking or nicking without excision, is frequently omitted from disclosures, as it is often not perceived or recalled as mutilation by respondents or interviewers. [44]

Sampling methodologies in national surveys often underrepresent rural high-prevalence zones due to logistical challenges and prioritize accessible clusters, while failing to capture transient migrant populations or diaspora communities where practices may persist covertly. [45] Critics have highlighted potential incentives in Western-funded surveys to amplify figures for advocacy and funding purposes, as seen in debates over UK hospital notifications extrapolated to claim widespread risk among girls, despite low verified incidence rates prompting concerns of methodological overreach. [46] [47]

Verification through physical examinations remains rare owing to ethical constraints, invasiveness, and high costs, confining most datasets to unconfirmed recall that exacerbates misclassification risks, especially for subtle or healed procedures. [48] While some clinic-based studies in Sudan verified self-reports with 100% concordance in small samples, broader reliance on unverified interviews perpetuates uncertainties in global extrapolations. [11] These flaws underscore the need for cautious interpretation, as unaddressed biases distort trends and policy prioritization. [49]

## Prevalence in Africa

### Sub-Saharan Africa Overview

Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for the majority of global female genital mutilation cases, with over 144 million girls and women affected as of 2024 estimates from UNICEF data. [3] This region hosts prevalence rates exceeding 50% among women aged 15-49 in more than 10 countries, including Guinea (96%), Djibouti (93%), Sierra Leone (83%), Mali (83%), and Egypt (87%), though the latter borders North Africa. [3] Type III FGM, or infibulation—the most severe form involving narrowing of the vaginal opening—is concentrated in hotspots such as Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Mali, Mauritania, and Somalia, where it constitutes a significant proportion of procedures. [2] A pooled analysis of 155 studies across Africa reported an overall FGM prevalence of 56.4%, underscoring the entrenched nature in Sub-Saharan contexts. [50]

Prevalence disparities persist between urban and rural areas, with urban rates generally lower due to greater exposure to education and anti-FGM campaigns; for instance, Demographic and Health Surveys indicate rural-urban gaps of 10-20 percentage points in countries like Nigeria and Kenya. [51] Recent 2024 UNICEF assessments show modest declines in prevalence rates—averaging 1-3% annually in surveyed nations—but these are offset by population growth, resulting in an estimated net increase of millions in absolute cases. [1] In West and Central Africa alone, nearly 50 million affected individuals represent 20% of the global total, highlighting subregional concentrations. [52]

Cross-border continuums exacerbate persistence, particularly along the Sahel belt spanning Burkina Faso, Mali, Niger, and neighboring states, where families evade national bans by traveling to permissive areas for procedures. [53] United Nations reports from 2024 note clandestine migrations undermining eradication efforts, with shared ethnic practices sustaining the continuum across porous borders. [54] Such dynamics illustrate how geographic and cultural proximity perpetuates FGM despite legislative progress in individual countries. [55]

### North Africa and Horn of Africa

In the North Africa and Horn of Africa region, female genital mutilation (FGM) remains nearly universal among women aged 15-49, with prevalence rates exceeding 80% in key countries and showing only marginal declines despite legal prohibitions. Egypt reports an overall prevalence of approximately 87% among ever-married women, though surveys indicate a drop to 37% among girls aged 15-17 in 2021, reflecting some generational shift amid medicalization and underground persistence. [56] [57] In Sudan, 87% of women aged 15-49 have undergone FGM, with rates reaching 97% in northern states like North Kordofan and North Darfur. [58] [59]

Somalia exhibits one of the highest global rates, at 98-99% among women aged 15-49, predominantly Type III (infibulation), which involves narrowing the vaginal opening through stitching. [3] [60] Similar patterns prevail in the Horn, where Eritrea has an estimated 83% prevalence and Djibouti 93%, with infibulation comprising 70-90% of cases across Sudan, Somalia, Eritrea, and Djibouti due to entrenched ethnic and clan traditions. [7] [61] UNICEF's 2024 global estimates, drawing from household surveys up to 2023, confirm minimal overall decline in these countries, as population growth offsets modest attitude shifts, leaving millions of girls at risk annually. [12]

Legal measures have failed to eradicate the practice; Egypt's 2008 ban and Sudan's 2020 criminalization under the Child Act have not curbed underground procedures, often performed by medical personnel, amid ongoing conflict in Sudan exacerbating access and enforcement gaps. [58] [62] In Somalia, where no comprehensive national ban exists as of 2024, 72% of women view FGM as religiously required, sustaining Type III dominance despite advocacy. [63] Regional migration patterns, including Sudanese displacement to Egypt, perpetuate FGM through cross-border practices and return visits for cutting, as documented in 2023-2025 studies. [64] These dynamics underscore causal persistence rooted in social conformity over statutory deterrence.

### West and Central Africa

In West and Central Africa, female genital mutilation (FGM) affects approximately 50 million women and girls, accounting for 20% of the global burden. [65] Prevalence rates exhibit marked variability across countries, often exceeding 80-90% in Guinea, Mali, and Sierra Leone among women aged 15-49, with Type II procedures (excision of the clitoris and labia minora) predominating in the region. [66] In Nigeria, national prevalence stands at around 20% for women aged 15-49, though rates surpass 50% in northern regions and among certain ethnic groups, contributing to the country's high absolute number of cases despite lower overall percentages. [67] Cross-border ethnic communities, such as the Fulani, sustain the practice through migration and shared traditions spanning multiple nations including Guinea, Mali, Nigeria, and Burkina Faso. [68]

In Chad and the Central African Republic, prevalence remains lower at approximately 38% and under 1% respectively among women aged 15-49 based on earlier surveys, though anecdotal reports and limited data suggest potential increases linked to ethnic practices and instability. [69] Type II FGM is the most common form in these areas, reflecting broader West African patterns rather than the infibulation (Type III) more typical in eastern regions. [66]

Recent trends show modest declines in prevalence in some countries, such as Burkina Faso where rates among women aged 15-49 fell from 75.8% to 56.1% between surveys up to 2021, attributed to legal enforcement and awareness campaigns. [70] However, population growth offsets these gains, leading to an estimated net increase in affected individuals across the region, with UNICEF projecting a need for accelerated efforts—up to 100-fold in Guinea—to meet elimination targets by 2030. [65] [71]

### Southern and Eastern Africa Periphery

In peripheral countries of southern and eastern Africa, such as Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, and South Africa, female genital mutilation exhibits lower prevalence rates among women aged 15-49 compared to core high-prevalence regions, ranging from negligible levels to around 15%, though persistent among certain ethnic groups and influenced by cross-border migration. [72] In Kenya, national prevalence stands at 15%, concentrated among communities like the Maasai and Somali, with adolescent girls aged 15-19 showing a decline from 26% three decades ago to 9% in recent surveys. [72] Tanzania reports 8% overall prevalence, with similar ethnic concentrations and a drop to 4% among 15-19-year-olds. [72] Ethiopia, at the eastern periphery, maintains higher rates of 65% nationally, driven by practices in regions like Afar and Somali, where 87% of cases involve infibulation. [72] [73]

Uganda and Rwanda represent the lowest end, with Uganda at 0.3% prevalence and stable below 1% for over 30 years, primarily among Sabiny communities in the east despite legal bans since 2010. [72] [74] Rwanda shows negligible prevalence, absent from concentrated-practice profiles, reflecting effective cultural and legal prohibitions without widespread documentation of cases. [75] In South Africa, the practice is sporadic and non-traditional, occurring mainly among migrant communities from high-prevalence countries like Liberia or Somalia, with isolated reports among Venda groups but no national surveys indicating measurable rates. [76] [77]

Recent declines in younger cohorts underscore campaign impacts, with under-15 prevalence below 5% in Uganda and Rwanda due to community-led initiatives, religious leader involvement, and enforcement of anti-FGM laws, though adult women retain higher legacy rates from prior generations. [72] [78] In Uganda, 95% of women in affected areas support abandonment, bolstered by police interventions and UNFPA-UNICEF programs since 2010. These efforts have sustained low transmission to minors, contrasting with adult cohorts where cultural norms linger. [74]

Projections indicate stable or further reduced low rates through 2025 absent disruptions, with Uganda expected at 0% and Kenya/Tanzania trending toward 7-2% by 2030 if current declines persist; however, porous borders with high-prevalence neighbors like Ethiopia or Somalia risk resurgence via migrant influences. [72] Regional data from 2004-2023 DHS and MICS surveys underpin these estimates, highlighting enforcement challenges in peripheral ethnic enclaves. [72]

## Prevalence in Asia and Middle East

### Southeast Asia Overview

Female genital mutilation (FGM) occurs in several Southeast Asian countries, primarily among Muslim communities, with practices typically classified as WHO Types I or IV, involving minimal excision or symbolic pricking rather than extensive removal seen elsewhere. [2] Indonesia reports the highest prevalence, with approximately 49% of girls aged 0-14 having undergone FGM, often as a ritual known as sunat perempuan performed shortly after birth or in childhood. [8] In Malaysia, estimates indicate a minimum prevalence of 53% among girls and women, concentrated among Malay Muslims, where the practice is culturally embedded and frequently medicalized by healthcare providers.

The Asia region accounts for over 80 million cases of FGM, representing about 35% of the global total, with Southeast Asia contributing significantly due to Indonesia's population of over 270 million, where more than 90% of Muslims practice some form. [3] In the Philippines, FGM is limited to minority ethnic groups such as the Tausug and Yakan in Mindanao, with no national prevalence data but anecdotal reports of ritual cutting among southern Muslims. Similarly, in southern Thailand, the practice persists among Malay Muslim communities, though at lower rates without comprehensive surveys. [79]

Recent data from 2024 indicate stable prevalence levels in these countries, with limited decline despite awareness campaigns, and a noted increase in medicalization, where procedures are conducted in clinical settings, potentially normalizing the practice. [80] Brunei and Singapore report sporadic cases among immigrant or minority populations, but lack systematic data. These forms, while less invasive, still constitute FGM under international definitions due to intentional harm to female genitalia for non-medical reasons. [2]

### Middle East Countries

Female genital mutilation in Middle Eastern countries primarily involves milder forms classified as Type I or symbolic pricking, often termed "sunna" circumcision, which some practitioners justify through references to certain hadith collections advocating female genital modification as a religious or cultural norm, though Islamic scholarship varies widely on its obligation or permissibility. [33] These practices occur predominantly among specific ethnic or tribal groups rather than uniformly across populations, with prevalence influenced by local customs intertwined with interpretations of Sunni traditions.

In Yemen, national prevalence among women aged 15-49 stands at 18.5%, with higher rates reported in eastern and coastal regions where up to 23% of girls undergo the procedure, often in infancy—83.8% within the first week of birth—typically involving excision of the clitoral prepuce. [81] A 2023 study in Yemeni coastal areas confirmed ongoing practice among youngest daughters, driven by social norms, though 75.4% of affected women express support for discontinuation, indicating potential urban attitudinal shifts amid advocacy efforts. [82] [81]

Iraq exhibits regional variation, particularly in the Kurdistan Region where prevalence among women 15-49 reaches 37.5%, ranging from 8% in urban Erbil to over 40% in rural or specific governorates like Sulaymaniyah, based on household surveys linking the practice to cross-border cultural exchanges with neighboring areas. [83] [84] Recent 2022-2023 data from primary health facilities in Erbil highlight persistent psychosocial impacts, with forms limited to Type I, though legal bans since 2011 have correlated with declining support in urban settings. [85] [86]

Prevalence in Gulf states like Oman and the United Arab Emirates remains low and underreported due to cultural privacy norms and lack of national surveys, with anecdotal evidence from southern Omani provinces like Dhofar indicating sporadic Type I practices among expatriate or tribal communities, while UAE studies from 2020 describe it as a health concern without quantified rates, suggesting incidence below 5% based on clinical observations. [87] [88] [89] Overall, UNICEF estimates contribute Middle Eastern cases to the broader Asian total exceeding 80 million affected women, underscoring underdocumentation in wealthier states where migration and modernization may suppress overt prevalence. [3]

### South Asia

In South Asia, female genital mutilation (FGM) occurs sporadically among specific ethnic and religious minority communities rather than as a generalized cultural norm, with practices often concealed due to social stigma and legal prohibitions. The Dawoodi Bohra Muslim sect, concentrated in India but also present in Pakistan and Bangladesh, maintains one of the region's most documented instances, where the ritual known as khatna—typically involving the removal of the clitoral hood (classified as Type Ia FGM)—is performed on girls aged 6-9. [90] A 2018 survey of Bohra women in India found a 75% prevalence rate for this procedure. [91] Estimates for the community overall range from 75% to 85%, affecting an estimated 80,000-100,000 girls in India alone, though exact figures remain uncertain due to the practice's secrecy and lack of national surveys. [92]

In Pakistan and Bangladesh, national prevalence remains below 5%, with UNICEF data indicating negligible rates among the general population aged 15-49. [93] [94] However, isolated enclaves of Dawoodi Bohra and other Shia Ismaili groups perpetuate the tradition, often through cross-border family networks from India, though documentation is sparse owing to underreporting and cultural reticence. [95] Broader Muslim communities in these countries largely abstain, distinguishing South Asian practices from more entrenched patterns elsewhere. [96]

Sri Lanka's Muslim population (approximately 8% of the total) includes pockets where FGM, referred to locally as sunat, is performed as a rite of passage, influenced by historical ties to Southeast Asian and Middle Eastern traditions. Small-scale studies from 2017-2021 confirm its occurrence but lack precise prevalence data, attributing persistence to familial and religious motivations despite growing opposition. [97] [98] In the Maldives, migrant labor from FGM-practicing regions and conservative Islamic interpretations have introduced limited cases, prompting UNICEF-led campaigns since 2020 to raise awareness and promote abandonment. [99]

Recent research from 2018-2024 highlights systematic undercounting across South Asia, as practices evade detection through private, non-medical settings and community enforcement of silence; a 2024 analysis notes that illegality under general child protection laws (e.g., India's POCSO Act) further drives concealment without specific bans. [100] [96] These studies, often from civil society networks, emphasize that while not comparable to African scales, South Asian FGM affects tens of thousands annually in hidden minority contexts, with medicalization trends emerging in urban areas. [80]

## Prevalence in Diaspora Communities

### Europe

In Europe, female genital mutilation is confined to diaspora communities from high-prevalence countries in Africa and the Middle East, driven by migration patterns that have brought significant populations from nations such as Somalia (prevalence exceeding 97% among women aged 15-49), Egypt (87%), and Sudan (87%). [1] Native European populations show no substantive historical or current practice of FGM, with prevalence effectively at zero outside immigrant groups. Immigration data from Eurostat indicate that between 2010 and 2023, the EU received over 1.5 million asylum seekers and migrants from top FGM-practicing countries including Somalia, Eritrea, and Nigeria, contributing to sustained risk in affected communities.

Estimates place the number of women and girls living with FGM across Europe at over 600,000, with approximately 190,000 more at risk of undergoing the procedure. [101] In France, around 125,000 adult women are affected, reflecting large-scale immigration from sub-Saharan Africa and the Maghreb. [102] Germany reports about 68,000 victims, a 44% increase from 2017 estimates, largely among refugees from Somalia and other high-risk origins. [103] The United Kingdom has identified over 37,000 cases through NHS records since 2015, though total affected women exceed 100,000 based on demographic modeling from migrant inflows. [104] Sweden, with substantial Somali and Eritrean diaspora, faces similar risks but lacks comprehensive national prevalence data. [105]

Annually, tens of thousands of girls in these communities remain at risk, with EU-wide extrapolations suggesting around 20,000 under 15 facing potential subjection, often via "vacation cutting" during school holidays or local arrangements. [106] FGM continues to be performed within Europe, including through "cutting parties" where multiple girls are mutilated simultaneously by imported or resident practitioners to reduce costs, as reported in UK investigations. [107] Despite universal criminalization—such as the UK's 1985 Prohibition of Female Circumcision Act and EU-wide directives—enforcement gaps persist, evidenced by minimal prosecutions (e.g., only a handful in the UK since 2015) due to underreporting, community secrecy, and jurisdictional challenges in diaspora settings. [108] These factors underscore ongoing transmission despite legal frameworks and awareness campaigns.

### North America

In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated in 2016 that approximately 513,000 women and girls were living with the effects of female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) or at risk of undergoing it. [109] This population largely consists of immigrants or daughters of immigrants from FGM/C-prevalent countries, with Egypt (109,205 at risk), Ethiopia (91,768), and Somalia (75,537) contributing over half of cases in 2013 analyses. [110] Prevalence has been sustained by immigration patterns, though underreporting persists in surveys due to stigma, privacy concerns, and reluctance to disclose in clinical settings. [111]

FGM/C has been federally prohibited in the US since 1996 under the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act. [112] Enforcement includes prosecutions, such as the 2017 case against a Michigan physician for performing procedures on girls from his community, marking the first federal charges under the law. [113] Despite bans, some families resort to medical tourism, traveling to origin countries for procedures on daughters, while domestic incidents continue in isolated, non-medical settings. [114]

In Canada, a 2023 Statistics Canada analysis estimated that 95,000 to 161,000 immigrant women and girls have undergone FGM/C or face risk, derived from immigration data cross-referenced with prevalence rates in countries of origin. [115] Concentrations occur in diaspora communities from high-prevalence nations like Somalia and Egypt, with trends tied to refugee and immigrant inflows rather than native-born transmission. [116] Underreporting affects official figures, as cultural normalization and fear of intervention deter self-disclosure in health or census data. [117]

Canada criminalized FGM/C in 1997 via amendments to the Criminal Code, prohibiting all forms regardless of consent or location. [118] The practice endures underground, including in private homes and by informal practitioners, as evidenced by 2025 reports of ongoing cases despite legal deterrents. [119] Medical tourism to countries of origin remains a concern for at-risk girls during family visits. [120]

### Other Regions (Latin America, Oceania)

In Latin America, female genital mutilation occurs sporadically among isolated indigenous populations rather than widespread immigrant enclaves. Among Colombia's Emberá communities, the practice involves partial removal of the clitoral hood on newborn girls and was first documented publicly in 2007 after hospital cases revealed complications during childbirth. [121] UNFPA-led initiatives since then have promoted abandonment through community dialogues, yet isolated incidents continue, including two cases among Emberá girls in Bogotá reported in July 2024. [122] In Peru, no national surveys exist, but reports indicate negligible occurrence confined to remote indigenous groups, with overall regional prevalence among women aged 15-49 below 1 percent as of recent World Bank aggregates. [123] [124] Verification remains difficult due to cultural secrecy, geographic isolation, and resistance to external inquiry in these small communities. [125]

In Oceania, female genital mutilation is marginal and primarily affects migrant women from high-prevalence regions, with no indigenous tradition in Australia, New Zealand, or Pacific Islands. An Australian government-linked estimate from 2019 identified approximately 53,000 women and girls born overseas who had undergone the procedure, based on census data cross-referenced with origin-country prevalence rates. [126] New Zealand lacks comparable quantitative data, though the practice has been criminalized since 1996 via amendments to the Crimes Act, with rare prosecutions tied to immigrant families. [127] Pacific migrant flows, such as from Fiji or Papua New Guinea, do not correlate with elevated risks, as the procedure is absent from those cultures. [128] Regionally, prevalence stays under 1 percent in 2024 assessments, hampered by underreporting, privacy in diaspora networks, and inconsistent health data collection. [1]
