# Female genital mutilation in Sudan

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in Sudan encompasses traditional procedures involving the partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons, predominantly in the severe form of infibulation (Type III), affecting 87% of women aged 15-49 as of the most recent comprehensive survey in 2014. [1] [2] Performed typically on girls aged 5 to 9, the practice is rooted in cultural norms associating it with purity, chastity, and marriageability, exerting strong social pressures that marginalize uncircumcised females. [1] [2]

Prevalence remains near-universal in northern and central regions, such as Northern State (98%) and North Darfur (98%), though lower in western areas like Central Darfur (45%), with urban-rural parity at around 86-87% and a modest decline observed since 2010 due to awareness campaigns. [2] [1] Infibulation, involving narrowing of the vaginal opening through stitching or other means, constitutes nearly 80% of cases, often accompanied by reinfibulation after childbirth to restore tightness, while less severe Type I (sunna) forms are reported but frequently overstated, masking underlying mutilation upon clinical examination. [2] These procedures, conducted by traditional practitioners without anesthesia, carry immediate risks of hemorrhage, infection, and shock, alongside long-term complications including chronic pain, urinary issues, and obstetric trauma. [2]

Sudan criminalized FGM nationally in July 2020 under Article 141 of the Criminal Code, imposing up to three years' imprisonment, building on prior state-level bans, yet enforcement remains negligible amid political instability and cultural resistance, with no significant prosecutions reported and prevalence persisting. [1] [2] Eradication efforts, including UNICEF's Saleema initiative promoting positive terminology for unaltered bodies and community-led declarations of abandonment by over 1,000 groups, have yielded partial shifts, particularly among educated and urban populations, but face entrenched opposition, as 41% of women still endorsed continuation in 2014 surveys. [1] [2] The practice's pre-Islamic origins and adaptation as a marker of ethnic identity, even among non-traditional groups via migration, underscore its resilience against legal and programmatic interventions. [2]

## Historical Background

### Origins and Traditional Practices

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in Sudan originated in pre-Islamic indigenous traditions of the Nile Valley and surrounding regions, with evidence linking similar practices to ancient Nubian and Egyptian societies as early as the second millennium BCE. Historical accounts, including references by Greek historians like Herodotus in the 5th century BCE, describe clitoridectomy rituals in ancient Egypt—geographically and culturally contiguous with northern Sudan—for purposes of ritual purity and social initiation, suggesting continuity in Nubian kingdoms that preceded modern Sudanese ethnic groups. [3] These practices were embedded in local cosmologies associating female genital alteration with fertility enhancement and protection against perceived spiritual impurities, independent of later Islamic influences. [4]

In traditional pastoralist and agrarian communities, such as the Beja in eastern Sudan, FGM functioned as a communal rite of passage, typically performed on prepubescent girls to signify maturity and readiness for marriage, while reinforcing tribal cohesion amid nomadic or semi-nomadic lifestyles. Ethnographic observations from early studies note its integration into fertility rituals, where excision was believed to redirect vital energies toward reproduction and avert misfortune, with collective ceremonies strengthening kinship ties and social hierarchies. [5] Among northern ethnic groups with Arabized pastoralist heritage, infibulation—a severe form involving excision and suturing—emerged as a variant tied to controlling female sexuality for lineage purity, documented in pre-colonial oral histories as a marker of group identity and endogamy enforcement. [6]

Variations by ethnic group reflect adaptive indigenous customs rather than uniform imposition; for instance, less invasive excisions prevailed in some riverine agrarian societies for symbolic cleansing, while pastoralists emphasized durability in harsh environments. Early 20th-century field reports confirm these practices' deep entrenchment, with prevalence tied to matrilocal or patrilineal structures ensuring adherence through social ostracism for non-compliance. [7] Contrary to claims of exclusive southern tribal involvement, empirical records indicate limited adoption among Nilotic groups like the Nuer and Dinka, who favored alternative scarification rites for initiation, underscoring FGM's primary roots in northeastern Sudanese agro-pastoral traditions. [8]

### Colonial Era Influences and Early Regulations

During the Anglo-Egyptian Condominium (1899–1956), British administrators governed Sudan through a system of indirect rule, relying on local tribal leaders and native administrations to maintain stability, which generally precluded direct interference in entrenched customs such as Pharaonic female circumcision to avoid provoking unrest or alienating communities. [9] This approach preserved the practice, which colonial reports described as virtually universal among Muslim populations in northern Sudan, where it was deemed essential for marriageability and social acceptance. [9] [10] Early documentation by medical officers highlighted its prevalence without advocating outright bans, prioritizing administrative harmony over reform amid fears that aggressive measures could be exploited by Egyptian nationalists or incite local backlash. [9]

Interventions began modestly in the 1920s, framed primarily as public health initiatives rather than moral or rights-based prohibitions. In 1921, British nurse-midwife Mabel Wolff established a midwifery training school in Omdurman, teaching harm reduction techniques like using sterile instruments and anesthetics to mitigate the procedure's severity while discouraging its most extreme forms. [10] [9] By the late 1930s, following the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty, efforts intensified under figures like Elaine Hills-Young, who banned all circumcision by trained midwives in 1937 and emphasized the practice's unhygienic and damaging effects through education. [9] In the 1940s, health campaigns expanded to include propaganda via pamphlets, posters, and lectures, often appealing to hygiene concerns and national modernization, though these remained persuasive rather than coercive. [10] [9]

A 1946 ordinance criminalized Pharaonic circumcision, imposing penalties of up to seven years' imprisonment and fines on practitioners, yet enforcement was minimal due to provincial governors' reluctance and resource constraints. [9] Incidents like the 1946 Rufa’a unrest, where public protests followed a midwife's conviction, prompted warnings from officials, such as a secret October 1946 civil secretary letter, against further alienating tribes by challenging core customs. [9] This non-enforcement, coupled with indirect rule's deference to local norms, inadvertently entrenched the practice; by 1949, reports noted rising illegal procedures as campaigns waned amid pre-independence political shifts. [9] The Standing Committee on Female Circumcision (1947–1949) represented the final organized push, but its limited impact underscored how colonial caution prioritized stability over eradication, sustaining FGM's cultural dominance. [10] [9]

### Post-Independence Developments

Following Sudan's independence in 1956, the new government retained the Anglo-Egyptian colonial ban on pharaonic circumcision—Type III female genital mutilation involving infibulation—in the Penal Code, prohibiting the procedure with penalties of up to five years' imprisonment or fines, while permitting excision of the clitoral prepuce. [11] This policy reflected continuity with pre-independence efforts to curb the most severe form, though enforcement remained weak amid entrenched social norms. By the 1974 Penal Code revision, the ban persisted, but the practice continued unabated, with over 80% of procedures in northern Sudan classified as Type III in studies from the early 1980s. [11]

The 1960s and 1970s saw fluctuating civilian and military regimes, including those under Ibrahim Abboud and Gaafar Nimeiri, during which FGM's institutionalization deepened through its framing as integral to Sudanese cultural identity, particularly in northern Arab-Muslim communities. [12] Prevalence data indicated widespread normalization, with surveys showing 87-91% of women in rural and urban northern areas subjected to some form of FGM by the mid-1970s. [11] Nimeiri's shift toward Islamization in the late 1970s culminated in the 1983 Penal Code under full Sharia implementation, which omitted the specific Type III prohibition, effectively deregulating the practice and aligning state legal frameworks more closely with conservative interpretations that tolerated milder forms as customary. [11] This removal coincided with heightened promotion of pharaonic-type procedures in some rural contexts as markers of ethnic heritage, contributing to sustained high rates without active state suppression. [12]

In the 1990s, under Omar al-Bashir's Islamist regime, government endorsements of sunna circumcision (Types I or II) emerged, often linked to modesty and chastity in religious discourse, with fatwas from some Sudanese Islamic scholars condoning these as permissible under Sharia to control female sexuality, despite lacking explicit Quranic basis. [13] This contrasted with internal medical opposition, as health professionals increasingly documented complications like hemorrhage and obstetric fistula, integrating anti-FGM education into nursing curricula by the late 1990s. [11] Empirical data reflected stabilization, with the 1989-1990 Demographic and Health Survey reporting 89% national prevalence among ever-married women (rising to 99% in northern regions), and 1991-1996 studies confirming 87-91% rates across urban-rural divides, indicating no significant decline pre-2000 despite sporadic campaigns. [12] [11] A gradual shift toward medicalized sunna forms began, performed by midwives in 55% of cases by the late 1970s-1980s, reflecting adaptation rather than abandonment. [13]

## Cultural and Religious Dimensions

### Motivations and Justifications for the Practice

In Sudanese communities, particularly in the northern regions, female genital mutilation (FGM) is primarily justified as a means to control female sexuality and ensure premarital chastity, with families and practitioners believing it curbs perceived excessive sexual desire and promotes fidelity within marriage. [12] [14] This rationale stems from the view that uncircumcised women are at higher risk of promiscuity, thereby reducing potential infidelity risks in familial alliances, as reported in ethnographic surveys where such control is seen as essential for maintaining social order. [15]

A core justification is the enhancement of marriageability, where FGM is regarded as a prerequisite for securing dowries, marital alliances, and social acceptance, with uncircumcised girls often deemed impure or ineligible for marriage. [12] [11] In northern areas like Haj-Yousif and Shendi, surveys indicate that 69-75% of supporting women cite adherence to social custom as the key driver, linking the practice directly to improved prospects in arranged unions and community integration. [12]

Symbolic purity and aesthetic standards further underpin the practice, with communities perceiving FGM as a way to achieve cleanliness and beauty by removing what is viewed as unclean external genitalia, thus symbolizing moral and physical refinement. [15] [11] These beliefs manifest in elaborate ceremonies that reinforce the girl's transition to womanhood, embedding FGM as a marker of social status and conformity. [11]

Regional variations highlight ethnic differences, with northern Arab-influenced groups emphasizing these controls and purity ideals more stringently, while southern communities like those in Juba exhibit lower prevalence but still invoke marriage prospects among supporters, reflecting localized social pressures over universal mandates. [12]

### Social Integration and Community Perceptions

In Sudanese communities where female genital mutilation (FGM) prevails, the practice functions as a critical rite of passage that enforces social conformity and integration, with uncut girls facing stigmatization and exclusion from peer groups and family networks. Families perceive FGM as essential for conferring respectability and eligibility within kinship systems, where deviation risks social sanctions such as ridicule or isolation, historically reinforced by derogatory terms like "Qulfa" associating uncut status with impurity, promiscuity, or lower social standing. [16] [17]

Community approval for FGM remains substantial in high-prevalence northern regions, with surveys indicating 56–67% of ever-married women in areas like Shendi and Haj-Yousif supporting its continuation as a customary norm tied to premarital chastity and communal identity. Uncut girls encounter diminished marriage prospects, as they are often deemed unmarriageable or impure by potential partners and in-laws, compelling families to adhere to the practice to secure alliances and avoid familial ostracism. [12] [17]

The procedure is predominantly executed by female elders, grandmothers, or traditional birth attendants, embedding it within women's social spheres and bolstering intergenerational female authority in regulating rites amid broader patriarchal structures that prioritize family honor. This gendered enforcement sustains solidarity among women as gatekeepers of cultural continuity, even as individual mothers may prefer milder forms under external pressures. [17]

Empirical evidence underscores limited voluntary abandonment absent collective social incentives, with national surveys showing persistent high prevalence—89% among women aged 15–49 in northern Sudan—despite declining personal intentions from 82% in 1990 to 54% by 2006, as isolated families fear exclusion without community-wide shifts. Community-driven rewards, such as public recognitions or development benefits, are required to tip norms toward cessation, highlighting FGM's reliance on interdependent social enforcement rather than isolated choices. [16]

### Religious Interpretations and Debates

Female genital mutilation (FGM) lacks any explicit endorsement in the Quran, with proponents in Sudan and broader Islamic contexts instead relying on certain hadiths that analogize it to male circumcision (khitan) for purposes of cleanliness and moral purification. [18] These hadiths, such as narrations attributed to Umm Atiyyah describing a "sunna" cutting for women, are classified by many scholars as weak (da'if) due to issues in their chains of transmission and lack of corroboration in authentic sources like Sahih Bukhari or Muslim. [18] In Sudanese discourse, such texts are invoked to frame FGM—particularly Type I or "sunna" circumcision—as a recommended (mustahabb) practice akin to male ritual, though critics emphasize that even if authentic, they do not mandate excision or infibulation prevalent in the region. [19]

Influential institutions like Egypt's Al-Azhar University have issued mixed fatwas on the matter, with earlier rulings in the 1980s and 1990s permitting mild forms of female "circumcision" (khitan al-banat) as culturally acceptable if not excessive, influencing Sudanese clerics who view it as permissible under fiqh traditions. [20] However, by 2007, Al-Azhar's Grand Mufti Ali Gomaa declared FGM forbidden (haram) as it contravenes Islamic principles against harming the body without medical necessity, a stance echoed in broader scholarly debates rejecting any obligatory status. [21] In Sudan, Sufi tariqas and some Salafi-leaning groups have historically promoted FGM as a sunnah act tied to pre-Islamic Arab customs assimilated into local Islam, with endorsements from certain imams in the 2010s framing it as essential for female piety and marriage eligibility. [19]

Reformist voices within Sudanese Islam, including imams engaged in anti-FGM campaigns like UNICEF's Saleema initiative since 2008, argue that FGM has no sharia mandate, citing the absence of prophetic compulsion and potential violation of maqasid al-sharia (objectives of Islamic law) protecting life and progeny. [22] These scholars highlight that authentic hadiths prioritize male circumcision while female practices stem from cultural ijma' rather than divine imperative, urging abandonment to align with Islam's emphasis on harm prevention. [18] Despite such fatwas and dialogues— including clerical declarations in eastern Sudan rejecting FGM as un-Islamic—the practice persists in many communities, underscoring the tension between interpretive debates and entrenched traditions. [22]

## Types, Methods, and Demographics

### Prevalent Forms of FGM in Sudan

In Sudan, female genital mutilation (FGM) is classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) typology, with Type III (infibulation) predominating nationally. [2] Type III involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora, followed by cutting and repositioning of the labia majora to narrow the vaginal opening, often with stitching to create a seal, leaving only a small aperture for urine and menstrual flow; this form is locally termed "Pharaonic circumcision." [2] [23]

Type III accounts for approximately 80% of FGM cases among women aged 15-49, based on self-reported data from the 2014 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), though clinical examinations indicate potential underreporting due to misclassification as milder forms. [2] This dominance reflects historical entrenchment in northern and central regions, where prevalence exceeds 85% in states like North Kordofan and South Kordofan. [2] A gradual decline in Type III prevalence has occurred, from 82% of circumcisions before 1980 to 72% after 2000, amid campaigns favoring less severe alternatives. [2]

Types I and II, involving lesser excision, constitute the remaining cases nationally, with Type I (partial or total removal of the clitoral prepuce and/or glans, termed "Sunna circumcision") and Type II (additional removal of labia minora, sometimes with labia majora) reported in about 16-18% of instances. [2] These milder forms are more prevalent in southern and eastern regions, such as Darfur (where Sunna comprises up to 25% in some areas) and Eastern states, contrasting with near-universal infibulation in the north. [23] Sunna is distinguished as a ritualistic procedure akin to male circumcision, often promoted by health advocates and communities as a culturally acceptable substitute to eradicate infibulation while retaining perceived purity benefits. [12] [2]

### Age, Techniques, and Practitioners

In Sudan, female genital mutilation (FGM) is most commonly performed on girls between the ages of 5 and 12 years, though the practice exhibits regional and temporal variability, with some communities conducting it as early as infancy during naming ceremonies or as late as adolescence to align with perceived healing benefits or marriage preparation. [17] Surveys reveal that prevalence accumulates rapidly in early childhood, with 16.5% of girls under 8 years having undergone FGM, rising sharply to 68.8% by age 14, indicating that procedures often cluster in the pre-pubertal period. [24] Urban and peri-urban shifts have occasionally prompted earlier interventions in some areas to mitigate social stigma, while rural ethnic groups like the Meloha may perform it immediately post-birth. [17]

Techniques employed are rudimentary and typically occur in non-sterile home environments without anesthesia, relying on sharp instruments such as razor blades to excise genital tissue, often leading to immediate bleeding controlled by manual pressure or binding. [5] The absence of pain relief or medical oversight amplifies risks of shock, infection, and incomplete healing, with procedures sometimes involving multiple cuts or stitching using thread or thorns in traditional settings. [25] Variability persists, as some practitioners adapt based on family preferences for less invasive methods, but the core method remains non-clinical and tool-limited. [17]

Practitioners are predominantly traditional midwives or birth attendants, who perform approximately 64% of procedures, followed by other traditional circumcisers at 29%, reflecting the embedded role of community elders in cultural rites. [25] A gradual medicalization trend has emerged since the 2000s, driven by perceptions of hygiene and safety, with health professionals conducting up to 58% of cases among girls aged 10–14 and overall involvement rising from 72% in the 1990s to 80% post-2000 among surveyed women. [17] Despite this, full clinical medicalization remains limited, as many trained providers refuse participation due to professional oaths or anti-FGM campaigns, pushing families back to traditional performers and perpetuating non-medical dominance. [17]

### Regional and Demographic Variations

Prevalence of female genital mutilation (FGM) exhibits stark regional disparities in Sudan, with northern states recording near-universal rates among women aged 15–49. The 2014 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) documented 98% prevalence in Northern, North Kordofan, and North Darfur states, alongside 96% in River Nile, contrasting with lower rates in southern and western regions such as 45% in Central Darfur, 61% in West Darfur, and 68% in Blue Nile. [2] These patterns reflect a north-south gradient, where northern areas approach saturation while peripheral regions show partial resistance or later adoption. [26]

Ethnic variations underpin these regional differences, with Arab groups in the Nile Valley sustaining near-universal practice—evident in 98% rates in Northern State—compared to historically lower adherence among non-Arab ethnicities like the Fur and Massalit in Darfur, though cultural diffusion via migration has elevated rates in some subgroups to 88% in South Darfur. [2] Urban-rural divides are narrower nationally, at 85.5% urban versus 87.2% rural among women aged 15–49, but urban centers like Khartoum (88% in 2014) display accelerated declines in newer cohorts, with 56% projected risk for girls aged 0–14 versus 71% rural. [2] [26]

Demographic factors further modulate prevalence, with higher rates among Muslim populations tied to interpretations framing FGM as a religious obligation, as one-third of respondents in a 2018 study affirmed. [2] Lower maternal education strongly predicts higher incidence for daughters, at 34% for those with uneducated mothers versus 15% for higher-educated, while among adult women, no-education cohorts show 77% prevalence against over 90% for educated ones, indicating cohort effects from entrenched norms. [2]

## Prevalence and Trends

### Current National and Regional Statistics

As of the latest available national surveys from the 2010s, approximately 87% of women aged 15–49 in Sudan have undergone some form of female genital mutilation (FGM). [1] This figure reflects data from the 2014 Sudan Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), showing minimal urban-rural disparities, with 86% prevalence in urban areas and 87% in rural areas. [1] Around 80% of cases involve infibulation (Type III FGM), the most severe form entailing narrowing of the vaginal opening through stitching or other means. [26]

Regional prevalence varies significantly, with the highest rates concentrated in northern and northwestern states. North Kordofan reports 97.7% among women aged 15–49, followed closely by North Darfur at 97.6% and Northern state at 97.5%; the lowest rate is in Central Darfur at 45.4%. [26] These figures derive primarily from the 2014 MICS and earlier household health surveys, highlighting entrenched practice in Arab-influenced northern regions compared to southern areas. [26]

Data collection faces limitations, including reliance on self-reported responses, which may understate prevalence due to social desirability bias, and a lack of comprehensive national surveys since 2014 amid ongoing conflicts that have disrupted fieldwork since at least 2019. [1] [26] No updated empirical estimates from the 2020s are available from UNICEF or UNFPA, underscoring gaps in real-time monitoring. [27]

### Historical Trends and Influencing Factors

Throughout the 1970s to 1990s, female genital mutilation (FGM) prevalence in Sudan remained stable at approximately 85–90% among women of reproductive age, reflecting deep-rooted cultural norms in northern regions where the practice was nearly universal among certain ethnic groups. [12] The 1989–1990 Sudan Demographic and Health Survey reported 89% prevalence among ever-married women, with regional variations such as 99% in the Northern region and lower rates in Darfur. [12] This stability persisted due to social pressures enforcing the practice as a prerequisite for marriageability and community acceptance, with limited external disruptions until the late 1990s. [28]

Following the 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement (CPA), which granted southern autonomy leading to South Sudan's independence in 2011, FGM rates in southern areas experienced a slight decline compared to the north, where prevalence hovered around 89–90% into the early 2000s. [29] Nationally, rates began a modest downward trajectory in the 2010s, dropping from 89% in 2006 to 86.6% by 2014 among women aged 15–49, with more pronounced reductions among younger cohorts—adjusted prevalence fell to 66.3% for girls aged 0–14, indicating a 21–25% intergenerational decrease. [29] This trend stalled amid the 2023 civil war, which disrupted monitoring and interventions, potentially reversing gains through heightened instability and reduced access to education. [28]

Key influencing factors include education, where higher maternal schooling correlates with significantly lower daughter circumcision rates—15.2% for mothers with higher education versus 33.6% for those with none—suggesting roughly a 10% reduction per additional year of schooling based on cohort analyses. [29] Urbanization has contributed to marginally lower urban prevalence (85.5% versus 87.2% rural in 2014), driven by greater exposure to alternative norms and media. [29] Migration effects are mixed: while internal displacements from conflict zones like Darfur spread the practice to previously low-prevalence areas, international migration has reduced support among diaspora communities through acculturation. [29] Policy shifts post-CPA facilitated localized autonomy in the south, enabling culturally tailored resistance, though northern enforcement remained weak until recent amendments. [28]

## Health and Well-Being Impacts

### Physical Health Consequences

Immediate complications of female genital mutilation (FGM) in Sudan include excessive hemorrhage, severe infections, and shock, often arising from procedures performed without anesthesia or sterile conditions by traditional practitioners. [15] Case reports document life-threatening infections such as necrotizing fasciitis in young girls post-FGM, requiring hospitalization and intensive treatment. [15] Urinary retention and acute pain frequently necessitate medical intervention shortly after the procedure. [30]

Chronic physical effects encompass keloid scarring, chronic urinary tract infections, and difficulties with urination due to anatomical distortion, particularly in Type III FGM (infibulation), the predominant form in Sudan affecting approximately 87% of women aged 15-49 as of the 2014 survey. [31] [1] Pelvic organ prolapse affects up to 87% of women with pelvic floor dysfunction who have undergone FGM, with Type 3 increasing symptomatic prolapse risk by 17-fold compared to uncircumcised women. [31] Incontinence, predominantly mixed type, is most prevalent among those with Type 3 FGM, alongside hydronephrosis from obstructed urinary flow. [31]

Reproductive physical harms include vesico-vaginal fistulas, postpartum hemorrhage, and obstructed labor, exacerbated by FGM-related scarring that impedes vaginal delivery. [15] Sudanese hospital data indicate cesarean section rates of 33.8% for Type 3 FGM cases, versus 10.8% for Type 2, reflecting heightened risks of prolonged labor and emergency interventions. [31] Deep perineal tears and increased hemorrhage during childbirth are documented, with FGM contributing to anatomical barriers that elevate maternal morbidity. [31] [15]

### Psychological and Reproductive Effects

A prospective cohort study of 420 primigravida women at Omdurman Maternity Hospital in Sudan found that 76.9% of those who had undergone FGM reported dyspareunia, compared to significantly lower rates in uncircumcised women (p < 0.001), with type III infibulation (prevalent in 67.8% of cases) contributing to scarring and adhesions that exacerbate painful intercourse. [32] This sexual dysfunction often results in reduced libido (62.6% affected) and marital discord, as chronic pain hinders intimacy and satisfaction, leading to relational strain documented in qualitative reports from Sudanese communities. [32]

Regarding fertility, while scarring from infibulation can theoretically obstruct menstrual flow or facilitate ascending infections potentially linked to tubal damage, a case-control study of 603 Sudanese women found no significant association between childhood FGM and primary infertility after adjusting for confounders (odds ratio ≈1.0). [33] Nonetheless, indirect reproductive impacts persist through dyspareunia-induced avoidance of intercourse, which may delay conception or exacerbate secondary infertility risks in affected populations.

Psychologically, FGM in Sudan correlates with heightened anxiety and depression, with a study of 66 African immigrant women (including Sudanese) reporting 30% meeting criteria for anxiety disorders and 33% for depression, particularly among those with infibulation—a form dominant in Sudan—where vivid procedural memories predict worse outcomes (p < 0.05). [34] PTSD prevalence reached 17.5% in similar cohorts, attributed to the trauma of cutting without anesthesia, often between ages 6–8 in Sudanese practice. Uncut women, conversely, endure social stigma, including exclusion from marriage prospects and community ridicule, fostering anticipatory anxiety and depressive symptoms amid peer pressure to conform. [34] These effects compound in marital contexts, where dyspareunia-related discord amplifies psychological distress, including feelings of inadequacy and isolation.

### Evaluation of Claimed Benefits Against Evidence

In Sudanese communities practicing female genital mutilation (FGM), proponents often assert benefits such as enhanced hygiene through removal of external genitalia, which they claim reduces odor and infection risks, and diminished sexual desire to curb promiscuity and preserve family honor. [35] [6] These claims lack empirical support; World Health Organization (WHO) analyses of global FGM data, including Sudanese cases, identify no verifiable health advantages, with procedures instead correlating to elevated risks of urinary tract infections, bacterial vaginosis, and menstrual complications due to altered anatomy and scarring. [36]

Regarding promiscuity reduction, no causal evidence links FGM to lower infidelity rates in Sudan or comparable settings; cross-cultural studies indicate that sexual behavior is primarily regulated by social norms, religious edicts, and community enforcement rather than anatomical modification, as evidenced by low promiscuity in non-FGM-practicing societies with analogous cultural controls. [37] Sudanese surveys, such as those from the Population Council, further reveal that perceived fidelity benefits stem from perceived social signaling—FGM as a marker of conformity—rather than physiological deterrence, with abandonment rates rising in communities without such rituals yet maintaining traditional values. [38]

The persistence of FGM in Sudan despite documented harms underscores its role in collective social utility, such as reinforcing group identity and marriage market value, over individual welfare; qualitative research in rural areas shows transmission driven by intergenerational pressure for conformity, not substantiated personal gains, with over 87% prevalence historically tied to these dynamics rather than hygiene or behavioral outcomes. [6] WHO meta-reviews confirm zero net positive effects across Types I-III prevalent in Sudan, attributing continuation to normative inertia rather than evidence-based merits. [39]

## Legal Framework and Enforcement

### Historical Legislation

During the Anglo-Egyptian condominium rule, Sudan enacted the first African legislation targeting female genital mutilation (FGM) through an amendment to the 1925 Penal Code in 1946, prohibiting the severe form known as pharaonic circumcision or infibulation (Type III FGM) while permitting the less invasive sunna circumcision (Type I). [40] [28] This partial ban imposed penalties of up to seven years' imprisonment and fines but saw limited enforcement, allowing the practice to persist widely. [11] Following independence in 1956, the law was ratified in 1957 with reduced penalties of up to five years' imprisonment for infibulation, maintaining the distinction that tolerated milder forms. [40]

Under the Islamist regime of President Omar al-Bashir, who seized power in 1989, the 1983 Penal Code revision—preceding but influencing the era—omitted the specific prohibition on infibulation, reflecting a shift toward Sharia-based governance that implicitly tolerated FGM by not explicitly addressing it. [11] The 1991 Criminal Act, enacted during this period, contained no direct reference to FGM, relying instead on general provisions against bodily harm (such as Sections 138–142), which were rarely invoked for prosecutions despite public government statements opposing infibulation as un-Islamic. [11] [41] This non-prohibition aligned with resistance from bodies like the Council of Islamic Jurists, which viewed broader bans as contrary to tradition and influenced the removal of a proposed FGM criminalization clause from the 2010 Child Act draft. [40]

In the 2000s and 2010s, national efforts emphasized gradual awareness over outright bans, with the Ministry of Health launching the Saleema Initiative in 2008 to reframe uncut girls positively and endorsing a National Strategy for the Abolition of FGM from 2008–2018 focused on education and community engagement, yet without accompanying criminal penalties. [28] Regional variations emerged, such as South Kordofan's 2008 Prevention of Female Genital Mutilation Act banning all forms with up to ten years' imprisonment, Gedaref's 2009 Child Law prohibiting harmful practices including FGM with fines or six months' imprisonment, and South Darfur's 2013 Child Act barring all types, though lacking specified penalties. [41] These state-level measures highlighted piecemeal progress amid national reticence, correlating with sustained high prevalence rates, such as 86.6% among women aged 15–49 in the 2014 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey. [28]

### Contemporary Bans and Penalties

In April 2020, Sudan's transitional government approved amendments to the Penal Code criminalizing female genital mutilation (FGM), introducing Article 141 to the Criminal Act of 1991, which defines FGM as the removal or mutilation of female genitalia leading to partial or full loss of function, performed in any setting including medical facilities. [42] [41] The law, enacted as Law No. 12 of 2020 and published in the Official Gazette on July 13, 2020, imposes penalties of up to three years' imprisonment, a judicially determined fine, or closure of the premises where the act occurred, applying nationwide across all 18 states without exception for specific types of FGM. [43] [41]

The provision targets individuals who perform FGM, including medical professionals and those traveling into Sudan to conduct the procedure, establishing a comprehensive national ban that supersedes prior state-level efforts and encompasses all forms of the practice regardless of consent or cultural justification. [41] [44]

Despite the onset of civil war in April 2023, the 2020 ban remains in force, with international organizations such as UNICEF and UNFPA continuing advocacy to uphold it amid ongoing conflict, emphasizing its role in protecting girls' rights without reported legislative repeal or amendment as of 2024. [45] [46]

### Challenges in Implementation and Compliance

Enforcement of Sudan's 2020 national ban on female genital mutilation (FGM), which imposes a three-year prison term for perpetrators, has been negligible, with no reported prosecutions under the subsequent military government following the 2021 coup. [46] This lack of judicial action underscores systemic weaknesses in the legal system, particularly in rural areas where FGM prevalence remains at 87% among women aged 15–49, comparable to urban rates of 86%, reflecting entrenched impunity and limited state reach. [1] Regional bans in states like Red Sea, where FGM affects nine out of ten women, have proven ineffective, described as "paper tiger" laws due to inadequate implementation and failure to deter practitioners. [47]

The 2023 civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and Rapid Support Forces has further eroded compliance by displacing over 7.4 million people and collapsing governance structures, including oversight mechanisms for anti-FGM efforts. [48] Conflict zones exhibit heightened impunity, with disrupted healthcare and humanitarian access enabling the persistence of medicalized FGM (previously at 67% nationally) amid resource shortages and social breakdown. [46] In displaced communities, traditional practices endure, often evading enforcement through cross-border mobility and weakened civil society advocacy. [48]

Empirical surveys reveal modest awareness gains but minimal behavioral shifts, with support for FGM's continuation dropping to 41% among women aged 15–49 by 2014 from 79% in 1990, yet prevalence holding steady at 87%. [1] Declines in younger cohorts (from 37% to 32% for ages 0–14 between 2010 and 2014) indicate limited change, under 5% nationally, hampered by community resistance rooted in perceptions of social acceptance and chastity preservation. [1] Over 1,000 communities have publicly abandoned the practice, but widespread evasion persists due to cultural backlash against legal intrusions. [1]

## Eradication Initiatives

### Government-Led Campaigns

The Sudanese government initiated organized efforts against female genital mutilation (FGM) following the establishment of dedicated committees in the early 2000s, including the National Committee for the Eradication of Harmful Traditional Practices, which focused on awareness-raising through school curricula integration and public media broadcasts targeting urban and rural audiences. [12] These programs emphasized health risks and legal reforms, with initial rollout in northern states where prevalence exceeded 90% according to 1990s surveys. [11]

By the 2010s, government rhetoric shifted toward "zero tolerance," as articulated in national strategies like the 2018 "Sudan Free From Female Genital Cutting" campaign, which involved ministerial declarations and community outreach coordinated by the Ministry of Health to promote abandonment declarations in villages. [39] The Saleema national campaign, launched in 2008 and scaled under government auspices, promoted positive messaging framing uncut girls as "healthy and complete," reaching over 1 million people via radio and workshops by 2018. [49] Despite these initiatives, empirical data indicate limited measurable impact, with FGM prevalence among women aged 15-49 declining only from approximately 89% in early 2000s surveys to 87% as measured in the 2014 survey, reflecting a drop of less than 10% amid persistent cultural adherence (latest comprehensive data as of 2014, with no national surveys since). [25]

In the 2020s, campaigns were embedded within broader health policy frameworks post the 2020 criminalization law, including training for health workers and integration into maternal care protocols, yet enforcement remained sporadic due to resource constraints and regional instability. [50] Critiques from evaluations highlight the top-down nature of these efforts, which often overlooked entrenched social norms and local resistance, resulting in declaratory commitments without sustained behavioral shifts, as evidenced by ongoing practitioner networks in rural areas. [51] Independent assessments note that while urban awareness increased, rural prevalence stagnated, underscoring causal factors like weak monitoring over ideological persuasion. [17]

### International and NGO Efforts

The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) initiated a joint programme in Sudan in June 2008 to accelerate FGM abandonment, emphasizing training for midwives and health professionals on the procedure's harms, integration into maternal health curricula, and advocacy for state-level bans in high-prevalence areas like Khartoum and Gedaref. [50] The programme's Saleema campaign rebranded uncut girls positively to counter stigma, involving media outreach, religious leader engagement, and community dialogues that trained over 4,000 facilitators and secured public declarations from 460 communities by 2012. [50] Expenditures for Sudan's component totaled $1.92 million from 2008 to 2012, with annual allocations typically under $650,000 despite higher requests, leveraging partnerships to amplify reach across 14 states. [50]

The European Union has framed interventions around human rights protections, issuing urgent 2024 appeals for safeguarding girls from FGM amid Sudan's civil war, while supporting broader gender-based violence prevention in displacement camps. [52] USAID has contributed to regional anti-FGM efforts, though specific Sudan allocations emphasize health education integration rather than standalone funding, often aligning with UN-led training. [53] These initiatives prioritize evidence-based advocacy and norm-shifting, with UNFPA-UNICEF reporting expanded community networks discussing alternatives to cutting. [50]

Despite these inputs, efficacy data reveal static prevalence at approximately 87% among women aged 15-49 based on the 2014 survey (latest comprehensive data), with minimal decline since 2000 despite awareness gains evidenced by stakeholder reports of reduced silence and increased public pledges. [25] Evaluations note heightened visibility and attitude shifts among leaders but attribute persistent practice to entrenched social norms resistant to top-down rights framing, underscoring causal gaps where external advocacy fosters dialogue without proportionally curtailing rituals. [50] [51]

### Community Resistance and Alternative Approaches

In Sudan, community resistance to FGM eradication efforts often stems from entrenched socio-cultural norms associating the practice with family honor, marriageability, and perceived hygiene benefits, leading to opposition against comprehensive bans. Following the 2020 criminalization of all FGM forms under amended Criminal Law Article 141, local acceptance remains limited, particularly in rural areas where poverty and limited education correlate with higher support for continuation—61.9% of women in poorest households favor the practice versus 23.3% in wealthiest ones. [28] Religious groups, such as Ansar Sunna, have resisted full abandonment by advocating milder "Sunna" circumcision (Type I), framing it as Islamically permissible and opposing punitive legislation as contrary to Sharia, which contributed to the 2010 removal of anti-FGM provisions from the Child Act. [50] This pushback has manifested in reluctance to engage in dialogues and fears of social stigma for uncut girls, potentially driving performances underground to evade sanctions and health center scrutiny. [28]

Alternative approaches have included promoting "Sunna" as a less severe substitute to pharaonic infibulation (Type III), historically tolerated under the 1946 law that banned only the latter while permitting Type I, reflecting pragmatic efforts to reduce harm amid cultural resistance. [28] Community-led campaigns, such as the Saleema initiative, have engaged over 970 localities by 2012 through dialogues involving religious leaders, midwives, and youth networks, yielding 460 public declarations of abandonment and pledges from thousands, with urban pilots in Khartoum showing shifts in attitudes via health center integrations and media tools reframing uncut girls positively. [50] However, these efforts highlight that legal bans alone may fail to alter root incentives like social conformity without economic alternatives for practitioners or norm-shifting incentives, as evidenced by persistent medicalization and underground risks post-2020. [28] While urban areas exhibit greater openness— with abandonment support exceeding 50% nationally but higher there—rural adherence underscores the need for tailored, incentive-based strategies over coercion. [28]

## Effects of Conflict and Displacement

### FGM Amid the 2023–Present Civil War

The civil war in Sudan, erupting on April 15, 2023, between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), has profoundly disrupted governance and social services, enabling the persistence of female genital mutilation (FGM) despite its criminalization in 2020. Although the law imposes a three-year imprisonment penalty for practitioners, no enforcement occurred under the military government in 2023, amid widespread insecurity and the collapse of legal frameworks in conflict zones like Khartoum, Darfur, and Kordofan. This breakdown has shifted priorities toward immediate survival, sidelining anti-FGM monitoring and allowing traditional cutters to operate unchecked, with medicalized FGM—previously comprising 67% of cases—further strained by the nonfunctionality of approximately 70% of hospitals in affected areas as of September 2023. [46] [48] [46]

Reports indicate a reported increase in FGM cases during the conflict, linked to heightened gender-based violence and familial coping mechanisms, though comprehensive data on a nationwide spike remains unverified due to disrupted reporting systems. Families in unstable regions have resorted to FGM alongside child marriage, perceiving it as a safeguard against sexual assault or to preserve perceived virginity amid rampant conflict-related sexual violence perpetrated by RSF militias. The war's chaos has eroded pre-conflict awareness campaigns and community protections, fostering conditions where entrenched cultural norms prevail over reforms, as ethnic groups maintain distinct FGM traditions without external intervention. By December 2023, internal displacement exceeding 7.4 million individuals amplified vulnerabilities, indirectly sustaining practices through fragmented social structures and resource scarcity. [54] [54] [48]

Causal dynamics of the conflict favor FGM's continuation by prioritizing security over individual rights during emergencies and polarizing communities along ethnic lines, where social status tied to the practice reinforces compliance. The diversion of humanitarian aid toward acute needs like food insecurity has limited targeted interventions, perpetuating a cycle where governance vacuums allow harmful traditions to endure unchecked. While prevalence stood at 87% among women aged 15-49 in 2023, varying by ethnicity and geography, the absence of enforced bans amid ongoing hostilities underscores how wartime disorder regresses prior gains in harm reduction efforts. [48] [46] [46]

### Practices in Refugee and Displaced Communities

In Sudanese refugee camps in Chad, such as Adré near the border, female genital mutilation (FGM) persists secretly despite bans in both countries, with aid workers reporting ongoing cases including preparations for girls as young as three and post-partum "adal" procedures on married women. [55] This continuation reflects cultural carryover from Sudan, where 87% of women aged 15-49 have undergone FGM, often Type 3 infibulation termed "pharaonic" or "tohoor." [55] Family networks and traditional beliefs framing FGM as essential for purity and marriageability sustain the practice amid displacement, exacerbated by conflict trauma and limited monitoring in camp settings. [55]

Among over 1.2 million Sudanese displaced to Egypt since April 2023—including around 504,000 registered with UNHCR as of October 2024—FGM remains entrenched, with families importing practices like Type 3 alongside local preferences for milder Types 1 and 2. [56] Intent to subject daughters to FGM is driven by extended family pressures, community hubs dubbed "Small Sudan" that connect families to practitioners, and perceptions of it as protection against sexual violence in unstable environments. [56] [57] However, economic barriers and reduced access to traditional cutters have delayed some procedures, while re-infibulation after childbirth occurs less frequently than in Sudan. [56]

Access to anti-FGM interventions in these communities is constrained by ad hoc humanitarian responses, exclusion of undocumented migrants from services, and low awareness of host-country laws, though partnerships with Sudanese civil society offer potential for culturally tailored outreach like the "Saleema" abandonment model. [56] In urban refugee settings, such as Greater Cairo, slight declines emerge among educated mothers and youth exposed to legal and health education, with some citing personal harms or marital issues to reject continuation, contrasting persistent intent in more insular camp or network-driven groups. [57] Overall, forced migration preserves FGM through social cohesion needs and family enforcement, amid Sudan's broader displacement crisis affecting over 7 million internally and externally. [56]

## Controversies and Broader Debates

### Cultural Relativism Versus Universal Rights

Cultural relativists argue that female genital mutilation (FGM) in Sudan functions as an adaptive cultural practice reinforcing social cohesion, family honor, and marriage eligibility within tightly knit communities, where deviation risks ostracism and economic disadvantage for girls and their kin. [58] Anthropological studies emphasize that such customs evolve from local contexts of modesty and group identity, and external prohibitions often provoke defensiveness by evoking historical impositions, such as the British colonial administration's failed 1946 ban attempt in Sudan, which fueled nationalist backlash and entrenched the practice further. [59] Relativists critique universalist interventions for hypocrisy, noting Western tolerance of analogous body modifications—like male circumcision, performed on over a million U.S. infants annually without equivalent condemnation, or elective cosmetic surgeries such as labiaplasty—while selectively targeting non-Western traditions, thereby revealing culturally biased standards rather than consistent ethical principles. [60]

In contrast, universal human rights advocates maintain that FGM constitutes a fundamental violation of bodily autonomy and dignity, transcending cultural boundaries, as affirmed by international frameworks Sudan has engaged with, including its 2006 ratification of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), which prohibits practices discriminating against women. [61] These proponents assert that empirical patterns of coercion—evident in Sudanese communities where girls face familial pressure irrespective of personal consent—justify overriding relativist deference to sovereignty, prioritizing protections akin to those against child labor or forced marriage, with UN bodies like UNICEF framing FGM as incompatible with the universal right to physical integrity under the Convention on the Rights of the Child. [62]

Sudanese-specific inquiries reveal tensions in this debate, with qualitative studies indicating that human rights-based campaigns are frequently perceived as neocolonial impositions, alienating potential allies and reinforcing FGM as a marker of cultural resistance; for instance, ethnographic work documents how urban and rural respondents in Sudan view top-down legal bans as eroding local agency, leading to clandestine practices rather than abandonment. [7] This perception persists despite declining support for severe forms, as evidenced by community-level shifts toward less invasive variants, suggesting that relativist sensitivities to sovereignty may facilitate gradual change more effectively than absolutist mandates, though universalists counter that delays perpetuate non-consensual harms without accountability. [59]

### Critiques of Anti-FGM Strategies

Critiques of anti-FGM strategies in Sudan center on their limited empirical impact, with legal bans failing to substantially reduce prevalence despite widespread implementation. The 2020 national criminalization, punishable by up to three years imprisonment, has correlated with evasion rather than elimination, as families relocate girls to rural areas or states with laxer enforcement during school closures or holidays, exploiting enforcement gaps amid the COVID-19 pandemic and ongoing instability. [63] Reports indicate no verified post-ban surveys showing more than marginal declines—prevalence hovered around 87% for women aged 15-49 prior to the ban, with anecdotal evidence of sustained or even increased cases in urban centers like Khartoum due to secrecy and medicalization by midwives. [1] [48]

This ineffectiveness stems from a reliance on coercive measures that ignore underlying causal drivers, such as economic incentives for practitioners (e.g., midwives earning supplemental income from cuttings amid low base salaries of $75-128 monthly) and social pressures prioritizing conformity over penalties. [25] Prior attempts at criminalization, including state-level bans in Darfur and Gedaref since the 2000s, similarly yielded evasion and cultural backlash, framing prohibitions as Western impositions that galvanized conservative resistance without altering entrenched norms. [22] International NGOs and media, often institutionally inclined toward highlighting legislative "wins" to sustain funding and narratives, have overstated progress—e.g., touting the 2020 ban as transformative—while downplaying persistent high rates and enforcement voids, potentially reflecting biases that prioritize symbolic victories over rigorous outcome tracking. [63]

In contrast, non-coercive pilots emphasizing aligned incentives demonstrate greater promise. A 2016-2018 health sector intervention trained 31% of community midwives on FGM harms, securing voluntary pledges to abandon the practice from the same proportion without punitive threats, shifting intent among key performers who conduct 64% of cases. [25] Such approaches, integrating education with accountability frameworks and indirect economic support via donor-funded trainings, avoid resistance by addressing practitioners' motivations, outperforming bans that drive underground activity; scaling similar incentive-based models, potentially including subsidies for alternative livelihoods, could yield more sustainable reductions than prohibition alone. [25]

### Long-Term Societal Implications

The persistence of female genital mutilation (FGM) in Sudan entrenches gender imbalances by embedding cultural norms that tie women's social value to perceived chastity and family honor, often resulting in stigma, harassment, and reduced marriage prospects for uncircumcised girls, thereby limiting female autonomy and perpetuating intergenerational cycles of control by elder women. [2] [6] This sustains broader societal disparities, as affected women face chronic health complications—including urogenital disorders, obstetric risks during childbirth, and psychological trauma—that impair productivity and impose ongoing burdens on limited healthcare resources. [64] [65]

Rapid or mandate-driven abolition efforts carry risks of social disruption in tightly knit communities, where non-compliance invites familial pressure, exclusion, or even unauthorized procedures by relatives, potentially shifting practices underground or toward less severe forms without addressing underlying stigma. [2] Prior state-level bans, such as those in Khartoum and Gadarif, have demonstrated limited efficacy and increased secrecy rather than cessation, highlighting how coercive approaches may exacerbate resistance without viable cultural alternatives. [2]

Evidence indicates a slow, organic decline in FGM prevalence linked to urbanization and education, with urban rates at 85.5% compared to 87.2% in rural areas, and support for continuation dropping below 24% in Khartoum versus a national average of 41%, driven by greater access to awareness campaigns and professional opportunities. [66] [2] However, the civil war since April 2023 has reversed such gains by destroying up to 80% of health facilities, displacing millions, and heightening poverty-driven vulnerabilities, likely postponing meaningful eradication by decades as anti-FGM initiatives falter amid service collapses and rising gender-based violence. [67]
