# Feast of the Circumcision of Christ

The Feast of the Circumcision of Christ is a Christian liturgical observance celebrated on January 1, marking the eighth day after Jesus' Nativity when he underwent circumcision in accordance with Jewish law, as recorded in Luke 2:21, and received his name "Jesus," meaning "Yahweh saves." [1] This event symbolizes Jesus' full entry into the Abrahamic covenant and his obedience to the Mosaic Law, affirming his humanity while prefiguring the spiritual circumcision of the heart in the New Covenant. [2]

Historically, the feast has been observed in the Christian Church since at least the fourth century, particularly in the Eastern tradition, where it emphasizes Christ's fulfillment of Old Testament prophecies and his role as the Messiah. [1] In early liturgical calendars, it coincided with the octave of Christmas, serving as a bridge between the celebration of the Incarnation and the broader themes of redemption. [2] By the medieval period, Western churches, particularly the Roman Catholic Church, formally recognized it. In later traditions, including Anglican, it was often linked to the Feast of the Holy Name of Jesus, which highlights the power and salvific meaning of his name. [3]

Theologically, the feast underscores the Incarnation's profundity: as the divine Word made flesh, Jesus willingly submitted to the ritual of circumcision—Genesis 17:10-14 and Leviticus 12:3—to identify with humanity's frailty and covenantal obligations, an act that also represents the first shedding of his blood in anticipation of the Cross. [4] This submission extends to his mother Mary, who, as part of Jewish custom, presented him for the rite, thereby participating in the mystery of salvation through her role in nurturing the human Jesus. [5] In Orthodox theology, it further illustrates the transition from the Old to the New Covenant, where baptism replaces physical circumcision as the sign of inclusion in God's people (Colossians 2:11-12). [1]

Today, observance varies by denomination: Eastern Orthodox churches maintain it as a major feast with vespers, divine liturgy, and hymns focusing on Christ's humility and naming. [1] In the Roman Catholic Church, following Vatican II reforms in 1969, January 1 is primarily the Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God, though the circumcision and Holy Name are still commemorated in readings and prayers, preserving its Christocentric roots. [4] Some Anglican and Lutheran traditions retain elements of the original feast, often tying it to New Year's reflections on renewal and covenant fidelity. [2] Across these, the day invites contemplation of personal spiritual renewal, echoing the covenant's call to a transformed life. [4]

## Biblical and Textual Foundations

### Scriptural Basis

The scriptural foundation for the Feast of the Circumcision of Christ is rooted in the Gospel of Luke, which records the event as occurring on the eighth day after Jesus' birth. Luke 2:21 states: "And when eight days were accomplished for the circumcising of the child, his name was called Jesus, which was so named of the angel before he was conceived in the womb" (KJV). [6] This passage describes Jesus' circumcision and formal naming, marking his entry into the covenant community as a Jewish male.

This practice aligns with the Mosaic Law, which mandated circumcision on the eighth day for male infants as a sign of the Abrahamic covenant. Genesis 17:10-14 outlines the covenant: "This is my covenant, which ye shall keep, between me and you and thy seed after thee; Every man child among you shall be circumcised. And ye shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin; and it shall be a token of the covenant betwixt me and you" (KJV), emphasizing its perpetual obligation. [7] Leviticus 12:3 further specifies the timing: "And in the eighth day the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised" (KJV). [8] By undergoing circumcision, Jesus fulfilled this requirement of Jewish law, demonstrating his identification with the people of Israel. [9] [10]

The naming of Jesus on this occasion derives from the angelic announcement to Mary in Luke 1:31: "And, behold, thou shalt conceive in thy womb, and bring forth a son, and shalt call his name Jesus" (KJV). [11] The name "Jesus," derived from the Hebrew Yeshua, means "Yahweh saves" or "the Lord is salvation," encapsulating his redemptive mission. [12] This etymology underscores the theological import of the event, linking the circumcision to Jesus' role in fulfilling and perfecting the covenant promises. [13]

### Patristic and Liturgical Texts

Early Christian writers, known as the Church Fathers, interpreted the circumcision of Jesus as a profound theological symbol, linking it to the fulfillment of Jewish law and its spiritual significance for the Church. Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–253), in his Homilies on Luke (Homily 14), viewed the circumcision of Christ as a prefiguration of Christian baptism, emphasizing that it represented the shedding of the "old man" and initiation into the new covenant, much like the baptismal washing away of sin. Origen argued that just as physical circumcision marked entry into the Abrahamic covenant, Christ's observance prefigured the spiritual circumcision of the heart through baptism, as echoed in Colossians 2:11–12. [14]

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 130–202), in Against Heresies (Book IV), explained that circumcision served as a sign of righteousness rather than its cause, and Christ's participation honored God's earlier covenants while pointing toward their completion in him. [15]

Liturgical references to the feast emerge in fourth-century documents, reflecting its integration into early Christian worship. The nomenclature of the feast developed distinctly in Greek and Latin traditions. In Greek patristic and liturgical texts, it is termed Peritomē tou Kyriou ("Circumcision of the Lord"), appearing in Byzantine hymnody and calendars from the fifth century onward, emphasizing Christ's lordship in fulfilling the law. [1] In Latin sources, Circumcisio Domini ("Circumcision of the Lord") gained prominence by the sixth century, as seen in Western lectionaries and sacramentaries that highlight the event's role in the incarnation. [16]

Early sacramentaries further attest to the feast's place as the Octave of Christmas. The Gelasian Sacramentary (c. 750, reflecting sixth-century Roman practice) titles January 1 as In Octavas Domini ("On the Octave of the Lord"), with prayers and readings focused on Christ's circumcision and naming, prohibiting pagan profanities during this sacred closure of the nativity period. [17] The Gregorian Sacramentary (c. 800) similarly structures it as the octave, incorporating collects that invoke the circumcision as a model of obedience, solidifying its liturgical form in the Western rite. [18]

## Historical Development

### Early Church Adoption

The Feast of the Circumcision of Christ emerged in the early Christian liturgical calendar during the sixth century, primarily in the Western Church, where it was established as the octave of Christmas on January 1 to commemorate Jesus' circumcision eight days after his birth, as described in Luke 2:21. This observance was first documented in Gaul at the Council of Tours in 567, which mandated its celebration with a proper Mass, reflecting its recognition as a longstanding tradition among the region's Christians by that time. The feast's title, "Feast of the Circumcision," appears as early as the 540s in the Lectionary of Victor of Capua, a non-Roman compilation that highlights its growing prominence beyond local practices. The feast was also observed in Spain before the death of St. Isidore in 636, as noted in the "Regula Monachorum." [19] [18]

In the Eastern Church, the feast gained traction through Byzantine liturgical influence, appearing in calendars by the eighth and ninth centuries, often combined with the commemoration of St. Basil the Great. Regional variations emerged early, with the Armenian Apostolic Church adopting a related observance—the Feast of the Naming of the Lord, equivalent to the circumcision event—celebrated on January 13 following their January 6 Nativity. This Eastern adoption facilitated the feast's spread across Byzantine territories, integrating it into broader liturgical cycles while adapting to local customs. By the seventh century, the feast had reached Rome, where it was incorporated into the Gelasian Sacramentary under the title "In Octavas Domini," prohibiting participation in contemporaneous pagan festivities. [17] [20]

A key factor in the feast's establishment was its alignment with the Julian calendar's New Year's Day on January 1, which by the eighth century had fixed its date universally across Christian regions. This positioning allowed the Church to overlay Christian observance on the Roman Kalends of January, a pagan celebration involving revelries, processions, and sacrifices to deities like Janus, which persisted into the Christian era despite condemnations from figures such as John Chrysostom in the late fourth century. Early Christian leaders promoted the Circumcision feast to suppress these "demonic" practices, emphasizing sobriety and covenantal themes to redirect popular customs toward Christological significance, thereby aiding its rapid institutionalization in the first millennium. [18] [21]

This papal support, alongside Byzantine dissemination, ensured the feast's endurance as a bridge between Old Testament law and Christian fulfillment, distinct from later medieval adaptations. [22]

### Medieval and Reformation Changes

During the Middle Ages, the Feast of the Circumcision received increased liturgical prominence as the Octave Day of Christmas, concluding the eight-day celebration of Christ's Nativity and emphasizing its role in the Church's calendar. This development is evident in early medieval sources like the Council of Tours in 567, which prescribed a specific Mass for the feast in the Gallican Rite, and the Gelasian Sacramentary of the seventh century, which titled it "In Octabas Domini" while cautioning against pagan profanities associated with the secular New Year. [19] By the tenth century, bishops such as Atto of Vercelli condemned lingering pagan customs like dances during the observance, promoting a more solemn focus on Christ's submission to Jewish law. [19] Papal actions further elevated its status; for instance, indulgences were granted for participation in the Christmas Octave, including January 1, as part of broader medieval efforts to encourage devotion amid seasonal festivities. [23]

The feast's integration into the Roman Missal featured dedicated collects, epistles, and gospel readings that highlighted themes of obedience to the Mosaic Law and spiritual circumcision. The traditional collect, for example, invoked God's grace for the "true Circumcision of the Spirit" through Christ's example, drawing from Philippians 3:3 and underscoring fulfillment of the old covenant. [24] Epistle readings often included Titus 2:11-15 on grace appearing to all, while the Gospel from Luke 2:21 narrated the circumcision and naming of Jesus, reinforcing liturgical continuity from ancient sacramentaries. [25] This structure persisted through medieval revisions of missals, blending Marian elements with the octave's christological focus.

The Protestant Reformation introduced varied responses to the feast. Martin Luther retained it in Lutheran calendars, viewing it as a scriptural commemoration of Christ's incarnation and obedience, integrated into the church year alongside other major festivals. [26] In contrast, Reformed traditions under John Calvin deemed such fixed observances non-essential, prioritizing the Lord's Day over traditional feasts; in Geneva, pre-Reformation bans on "Romish sacred days" were upheld, leading to minimal or absent commemoration in Calvinist communities. [27]

Following the Council of Trent, the 1570 Roman Missal promulgated by Pope St. Pius V standardized the Catholic liturgy, codifying the Feast of the Circumcision as a duplex majus feast with uniform texts across dioceses to counter Protestant critiques and ensure doctrinal clarity. However, by the nineteenth century, its popularity waned amid rising secular New Year's celebrations, which increasingly overshadowed religious observance and contributed to a broader decline in medieval-style feast devotions. [19]

The 1969 revision of the Roman Missal under Pope Paul VI marked a significant shift, renaming January 1 the Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God to restore an ancient Marian feast while subordinating the circumcision to an optional commemoration in the Roman Martyrology. [28] This change aimed to emphasize Mary's role in the incarnation and align with ecumenical sensitivities, though the circumcision's theological import remained acknowledged in the liturgy.

## Theological Significance

### Circumcision and Covenant

The circumcision of Jesus, performed on the eighth day after his birth as prescribed by Jewish law, served as a profound affirmation of his submission to the Mosaic covenant, marking his entry into the community of Israel as the promised Messiah. In the New Testament, this act is interpreted as Jesus being "born under the law" to redeem those under its authority, thereby fulfilling the Abrahamic promise while inaugurating a transition to the new covenant through his life and sacrifice (Galatians 4:4-5). This event underscores the continuity of God's redemptive plan, where Jesus' obedience to the old covenant's sign of circumcision validates its divine origin without binding it upon all believers eternally. [29]

Theologically, Jesus' circumcision carries a typological significance, symbolizing the removal of the sinful flesh and pointing forward to spiritual purification in the Christian era. Early interpreters saw it as prefiguring the "circumcision of Christ," a non-physical rite that cuts away the body of sin through faith, directly linked to baptism as the initiatory sacrament of the new covenant (Colossians 2:11). This typology emphasizes circumcision not merely as a physical marker of Abrahamic descent but as a shadow of inner renewal, where the old covenant's external sign yields to the transformative power of Christ's redemptive work. [30]

Patristic theologians, particularly Augustine of Hippo, viewed Jesus' circumcision as a demonstration of his full humanity and unwavering obedience to the law he came to fulfill. Augustine argued that by submitting to this rite, Christ, as a true man born of a woman, honored the Mosaic prescriptions without abolishing them, thereby modeling perfect conformity to God's will and prefiguring the spiritual circumcision of believers' hearts. [31] This act highlighted Christ's dual nature, subjecting his human flesh to the covenant's demands while transcending them through divine fulfillment, thus affirming the law's preparatory role in salvation history. [32]

Theological debates surrounding Jesus' circumcision have centered on balancing respect for the Mosaic law with warnings against Judaizing tendencies that might impose it as a salvific requirement. While early Church figures like Paul opposed mandating circumcision for Gentile converts to avoid reverting to works-based righteousness, they upheld Jesus' observance as an act of solidarity with Jewish tradition, not supersession (Galatians 5:2-6). [33] This tension—evident in the Acts 15 council—resolved by affirming the law's fulfillment in Christ, rejecting anti-Judaizing extremes that dismissed its covenantal value while guarding against legalism.

In modern ecumenical perspectives, Jesus' circumcision fosters dialogue on covenant continuity, portraying Christianity as extending rather than abrogating the Abrahamic promises in interfaith relations with Judaism. Documents from bodies like the World Council of Churches emphasize that through such reflection, Christians recognize the enduring vitality of the Jewish covenant, promoting mutual respect and shared heritage without implying replacement theology. [34] Theologians such as Cardinal Walter Kasper have articulated this as the "never revoked" nature of God's covenant with Israel, bridging divides in contemporary Jewish-Christian encounters. [35]

### Naming of Jesus and Incarnation

The naming of Jesus occurs on the eighth day following his birth, coinciding with the rite of circumcision, as described in the Gospel of Luke: "After eight days had passed, it was time to circumcise the child; and he was called Jesus, the name given by the angel before he was conceived in the womb" (Luke 2:21). This event fulfills the angelic announcement in Matthew, where the name "Jesus" (meaning "Yahweh saves") is specified to signify his divine mission of salvation: "She will bear a son, and you are to name him Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21). The timing underscores Jesus' full participation in Jewish law and custom, marking the beginning of his earthly identity and redemptive purpose. [36]

Theologically, Jesus' circumcision links directly to the doctrine of the Incarnation, representing the first shedding of his sacred blood and foreshadowing the redemptive sacrifice on the cross. As the incarnate Word, Christ willingly submits to this rite, initiating his obedience to the Mosaic Law and embodying the hypostatic union of divine and human natures. This act symbolizes the entry of God into human suffering, where the blood of circumcision prefigures the propitiatory blood of Calvary, fulfilling the covenant and extending salvation to all humanity. [37] [38]

The Feast of the Circumcision commemorates this naming as a profound affirmation of the Incarnation, celebrating the hypostatic union through which "God with us" (Emmanuel) assumes human flesh. In the Byzantine rite, hymns such as the troparion emphasize this mystery: "The Lord of all accepts to be circumcised, thus, as He is good, excises the sins of mortal men," linking the event to Christ's salvific presence among humanity. These liturgical texts highlight the name Jesus as revealing the divine economy, where the eternal Son becomes the savior in time. [39]

In Jewish tradition, the eighth-day circumcision (brit milah) included the formal naming of the male child, a practice rooted in biblical mandates and elaborated in rabbinic sources like the Talmud (Shabbat 134a), where the name is proclaimed during the ceremony to invoke divine protection and covenantal identity. Christians adapted this custom for Jesus, transforming it into a christological sign of fulfillment: the divine command for his name integrates Old Testament prophecy with New Testament revelation, emphasizing continuity between Jewish heritage and the new covenant inaugurated by the Incarnate Christ. [40]

The twentieth-century theological revival of this feast's significance, particularly in Vatican II documents, emphasizes Christ's obedient Incarnation. Lumen Gentium articulates how the Son of God, through his assumption of human nature, perfectly obeys the Father, redeeming humanity by fulfilling the law in love and humility. This emphasis renews appreciation for the feast as a lens on Christ's total self-emptying (kenosis), bridging divine initiative with human response in salvation history. [41]

## Observance in Eastern Christianity

### Eastern Orthodox Practices

In the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Feast of the Circumcision of Christ is observed on January 1 according to the calendar used by most jurisdictions, such as the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese, or on January 14 (Gregorian) for those following the Julian calendar, including the Russian Orthodox Church Outside of Russia. [1] [42] As one of the Twelve Great Feasts, it features a complete liturgical cycle beginning with Great Vespers on the evening of December 31, followed by Orthros (Matins) and the Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great on January 1, emphasizing Christ's submission to the Mosaic Law as the first shedding of His blood for humanity's salvation. [43] [44]

The services incorporate specific hymns that extend themes from the Nativity, highlighting the circumcision's role in fulfilling the Old Covenant. The troparion in Tone One proclaims: "Enthroned on high with the Eternal Father and Your divine Spirit, O Jesus, You willed to be born on earth of the unwedded handmaid, your Mother. Therefore You were circumcised as an eight-day old Child. Glory to Your most gracious counsel; glory to Your dispensation; glory to Your condescension, O only Lover of mankind." [39] Additional kontakia and the canon by Saint Stephen of Saint Sabbas Monastery poetically link the event to spiritual circumcision through Baptism, as described in Colossians 2:11. [1]

Iconography for the feast commonly depicts the circumcision occurring in a temple setting, with the Theotokos holding the infant Christ while a priest performs the rite, often accompanied by Saint Joseph and symbolic elements like the Tablets of the Law to underscore covenantal fulfillment; such images appear in Byzantine and post-Byzantine art from regions like Russia and Greece. [45] [46]

The feast concludes the Nativity Fast, allowing families to break abstinence with celebratory meals, and it coincides with the commemoration of Saint Basil the Great, inspiring customs like the preparation of vasilopita—a sweet bread or cake baked with a hidden coin for good fortune—in Greek Orthodox households, cut after the Liturgy to honor the saint and usher in the new year. [47] [44]

Contemporary observances remain vibrant in Greece, where parishes host vespers and liturgies drawing families for blessings, and in Russia, where January 14 services in cathedrals like Christ the Savior in Moscow emphasize national renewal amid the post-Soviet revival of Orthodox traditions. [48] [49]

### Oriental Orthodox Variations

In the Coptic Orthodox Church, the Feast of the Circumcision is observed as one of the seven minor feasts of the Lord on the sixth day of the month of Tobe, corresponding to January 14 in the Gregorian calendar, eight days after the Nativity celebrated on January 7. The liturgy features joyful chants and specific responses distinct from those of the Nativity, with a strong emphasis on Old Testament readings from Genesis and Leviticus that underscore circumcision as the covenant sign given to Abraham and mandated for Israelite males, prefiguring the New Testament sacrament of baptism as the spiritual fulfillment of this rite. [50]

Similarly, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church commemorates the feast, known as Gizret, on Tir 6 (January 14 Gregorian), highlighting Jesus' obedience to Mosaic Law as recorded in Luke 2:21 and Leviticus 12:3, where circumcision on the eighth day symbolizes the shedding of the old nature and entry into covenant with God. Liturgical readings draw heavily from Old Testament texts to illustrate this typology, linking the event to Christ's incarnation and the preservation of his divinity, while communal processions often carry the Tabot—a veiled replica of the Ark of the Covenant—to evoke the temple presentation, and the rite symbolically connects circumcision to baptism as its antitype for initiating believers into the church. [51] [52] [53]

The Syriac Orthodox Church marks the feast on January 1, incorporating it into the annual cycle outlined in the Hudra, the comprehensive liturgical book that compiles hymns, prayers, and scriptural lessons for fixed feasts throughout the ecclesiastical year. Celebrations feature the Divine Liturgy with distinctive anaphoras, such as those of the Apostles or St. James, which emphasize themes of covenant renewal and Christ's submission to Jewish custom, drawing from patristic interpretations to affirm his dual nature. [54] [55] [56]

In the Armenian Apostolic Church, the Feast of the Circumcision coincides with the commemoration of the Naming of Jesus on January 13, serving as the octave's conclusion following the Nativity and Theophany on January 6, and integrates elements from Christmas Eve vigils such as extended scriptural reflections on Christ's infancy events from Luke and the adoration by the Magi. This observance often includes the blessing of homes during the broader Christmas period, where priests sprinkle holy water and incense to symbolize divine protection and covenantal grace extended to families, echoing the feast's themes of consecration and new beginnings. [57] [58] [59]

Across Oriental Orthodox communities, modern adaptations in diaspora settings have led to a reduced emphasis on elaborate public rituals for the feast, with observances often limited to simplified vespers and liturgies in parish churches to accommodate secular calendars. However, the full traditional rites, including extended Old Testament lections and symbolic baptisms, continue to be retained in monastic environments, where communities like those in Egypt, Ethiopia, and Syria preserve the ancient practices integral to spiritual formation. [60] [61]

## Observance in Western Christianity

### Roman Catholic Liturgy

In the Roman Catholic Church following the Second Vatican Council, the Feast of the Circumcision of Christ is no longer observed as a distinct liturgical celebration but is incorporated into the Solemnity of the Blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of God, on January 1, which serves as the octave day of Christmas and a holy day of obligation. This shift, enacted in the 1969 revision of the Roman Calendar by Pope Paul VI, prioritizes Mary's divine maternity while retaining commemoration of Christ's circumcision through the proper Mass texts and readings, allowing for an optional memorial emphasis on the event when appropriate. The liturgy underscores Christ's submission to the Mosaic law, symbolizing the fulfillment of the old covenant in the new. [62]

The Mass readings for the solemnity directly evoke the circumcision and its covenantal significance. The first reading from Numbers 6:22-27 presents the priestly blessing, invoking God's favor and peace upon the people, which ties into the themes of divine protection and benediction at the threshold of the new year. [62] The second reading, Galatians 4:4-7, describes God sending his Son, "born of a woman, born under the law, to ransom those under the law," highlighting Christ's circumcision as an act of obedience that liberates believers from bondage and grants adoption as God's children. [62] The Gospel from Luke 2:16-21 recounts the shepherds' visit, Mary's pondering in her heart, and explicitly the eighth-day circumcision and naming of Jesus. [62]

According to the rubrics in the Roman Missal, the solemnity calls for white vestments, signifying joy and purity, as is standard for feasts honoring the Blessed Virgin Mary. [63] The Gloria is sung or recited, affirming praise to God in the highest, and the Creed is professed, reinforcing the faith in Christ's divinity central to the day's Marian focus. The primary preface celebrates Mary's fruitful virginity and her role in salvation history. [64] These elements ensure the liturgy remains festive yet contemplative, bridging Christmas and the new liturgical year.

In various regions, the solemnity intersects with local New Year's customs, fostering family and communal gatherings. In Poland, where January 1 is a national holiday and holy day of obligation, Catholics attend Mass for special blessings of peace and prosperity, often followed by family meals featuring traditional dishes like pierogi and bigos, symbolizing abundance and renewal. [65] Similarly, in Italy, the day includes public Masses with the Te Deum hymn for thanksgiving, tied to the World Day of Peace, and families gather for lentil-based meals believed to bring good fortune, while exchanging greetings that echo the priestly blessing from the readings. [66]

Recent papal teachings have emphasized the feast's themes of peace and covenant renewal. Pope John Paul II, in his homilies and World Day of Peace messages delivered on January 1, frequently linked the solemnity to the circumcision as a sign of Christ's solidarity with humanity under the law, calling for global renewal of the covenant through justice, forgiveness, and Mary's intercession for peace among nations. [67] For instance, in his 2001 homily for the solemnity, he reflected on Christ "born under the law" (Galatians 4:4), urging humanity to embrace divine sonship as a path to reconciling conflicts and building a civilization of love. [67]

### Protestant Traditions

In Lutheran traditions, particularly within synods like the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod (LCMS), the Feast of the Circumcision and Name of Jesus is retained as a major festival observed on January 1, featuring services that emphasize Christ's obedience to the Mosaic Law through specific readings and hymns such as "To Shepherds as They Watched by Night," which reflects on the angelic announcement and the humility of the Incarnation. [26] [68] These observances highlight themes of covenant fulfillment and the shedding of Christ's first blood, often integrated with New Year's reflections on renewal. [69]

Anglican practices show variation, with the Book of Common Prayer (1662 edition) designating January 1 as the Circumcision of Christ, a lesser festival that includes collects focused on the naming of Jesus and his submission to the law, such as "Almighty God, who madest thy blessed Son to be circumcised, and obedient to the law for man." [70] In high church contexts within the Anglican Communion, this day is marked by liturgical services that blend the feast with Epiphany preparations, underscoring the Holy Name's significance, while low church or evangelical parishes may observe it more modestly or align it with general New Year's worship. [71]

Reformed and Calvinist traditions exhibit minimalism regarding fixed feasts, largely rejecting obligatory holy days beyond the Lord's Day in favor of Scripture-regulated worship, though the feast is occasionally referenced in modern lectionaries like the Revised Common Lectionary, which assigns readings for the Holy Name of Jesus on January 1, such as Numbers 6:22-27 and Luke 2:15-21, allowing for optional incorporation in Presbyterian or Reformed churches. [72] This approach stems from post-Reformation emphases on sola scriptura, limiting elaborate observances to avoid perceived superstition.

In Methodist and Baptist communities, the feast is not formally mandated but appears through occasional sermons on Luke 2:21 during New Year's services, often linking Christ's circumcision and naming to themes of personal commitment and resolution-making for the coming year, as seen in Baptist preaching that portrays the event as a model for spiritual renewal without ritual emphasis. [73] [74] These denominations prioritize pulpit exposition over liturgical calendars, using the occasion to connect biblical obedience with contemporary discipleship. [75]

Modern ecumenical trends in united churches, such as the United Church of Christ, increasingly include January 1 observances with a focus on global peace, aligning the Holy Name feast with the World Day of Peace through shared prayers and messages promoting reconciliation, reflecting broader Protestant engagement in interdenominational efforts for justice and unity. [76] This integration draws from post-Reformation adaptations while emphasizing Christ's name as a symbol of peace across divided traditions. [73]
