# Circumcision in Brunei

Circumcision in Brunei, known locally as bersunat, is the ritual surgical removal of the foreskin from boys' penises, serving as a religious rite of entry into the Muslim community in this absolute monarchy where Islam is the official religion. Predominantly performed on males aged 7 to 12 as an obligation under Sunni Islam's Shafi'i school of jurisprudence, it integrates traditional Malay customs—adopted since the 14th century conversion of the ruling house—with Islamic practices, evolving from pre-Islamic animist influences under Islamic law. The procedure, historically conducted by local practitioners called dukun and increasingly by medical professionals for hygiene and safety, symbolizes maturity and faith, with boys reciting the Shahada beforehand amid communal celebrations featuring processions, ceremonial attire, feasts, and recovery rituals like sandang bersunat. As a rite of passage, bersunat reinforces social bonds and religious adherence in Brunei's Malay-Muslim society, bolstered by state support and ties to national identity, including occasional links to royal events.

## History

### Pre-Islamic Origins

Indigenous groups in Borneo practiced male circumcision as part of tribal customs predating Islamic influence, often conducted without elaborate religious solemnity. [1] These customs were observed among Dayak peoples, serving functions akin to initiation into maturity and communal roles.

### Islamic Integration

Islam reached Brunei in the 14th century via trade networks linking the region to Java and Middle East merchants. Chinese records confirm its presence by 1371, alongside adoption of a Jawi script resembling Arabic. [2] Sultan Muhammad Shah's conversion to Islam in 1376 marked a pivotal milestone, as noted by Bruneian historians, securing the faith within the ruling dynasty until his death in 1402. [3]

This shift embedded Islamic practices in Bruneian governance and society. It elevated male circumcision—locally termed bersunat—from a recommended sunnah rite in Sunni tradition to near-obligatory for Muslim males, symbolizing religious commitment. [4] Under early sultanate rule, such rites became standardized, enhancing social cohesion during the transition to an Islamic state while pre-Islamic customs shaped communal observances.

## Religious Significance

### Islamic Mandates

In Sunni Islam, male circumcision derives its religious basis from hadiths associating it with fitrah, the natural state of purity and disposition inherent to humans, as exemplified in a narration where the Prophet Muhammad identified circumcision among five innate practices alongside grooming rituals. Although the Quran does not explicitly mandate it, prophetic traditions and scholarly consensus (ijma) across Sunni schools, including the predominant Shafi'i madhhab in Brunei, affirm its practice for male Muslims to align with this primordial purity.

Interpretations vary on its legal status, with some scholars classifying it as wajib (obligatory), incurring sin for omission if capable, while others regard it as sunnah mu'akkadah (a strongly recommended prophetic practice) short of full obligation. [5] In the Shafi'i school, the relied-upon view holds circumcision as obligatory for males, emphasizing its role in fulfilling religious hygiene and covenant-like purity. [6]

Brunei's State Mufti has issued fatwas through religious councils affirming male circumcision as required under Islamic law for Muslim males, reinforcing its integration into the nation's Shafi'i-oriented jurisprudence without elevating it to the status of Islam's five pillars. [7]

### Bruneian Interpretations

In Brunei, the State Mufti has issued fatwas declaring male circumcision obligatory under Islamic law, an essential religious duty in the Sharia-influenced framework where non-adherence constitutes a moral failing under state enforcement and oversight. [8] [9]

Local scholars emphasize communal bersunat events for strengthening ummah bonds and social cohesion in Brunei's Muslim society. The Sultan, as supreme religious authority, promotes the practice by endorsing these edicts and royal precedents, including royal family ceremonies. [10]

## Cultural Practices

### Bersunat Ceremonies

Bersunat ceremonies in Brunei involve communal participation, with family and community members playing key roles in supporting the boy during the ritual, emphasizing collective affirmation of faith. Immediately prior to the procedure, the boy recites the kalimah syahadat to declare his commitment to Islam, underscoring the event's religious symbolism as a marker of true Muslim identity. [11]

In prominent instances, such as the 1989 circumcision of the Sultan's eldest son, these ceremonies are commemorated through elaborate public events integrated into broader celebrations. [12]

### Age and Social Role

In Brunei, male circumcision, or bersunat, typically occurs on boys aged 6 to 12 during primary school years. This aligns with Islamic expectations before puberty while suiting children's physical resilience. [13]

Bersunat serves as a key rite of passage, marking transition to social maturity and preparation for male responsibilities in Bruneian Malay Muslim society, including religious duties after reaching baligh (puberty), such as Ramadan fasting. [13] Afterward, boys gain elevated status, becoming eligible for expanded community roles linked to Islamic adherence and advancing toward adulthood under gender-specific norms. [13]

Exclusive to males, bersunat reinforces identity in Bruneian Malay culture by embedding Islamic principles of cleanliness and maturity, thus distinguishing gendered paths to adulthood in a society governed by Malay Islamic Monarchy ideology. [13]

## Procedures and Methods

### Traditional Techniques

Traditional circumcision in Brunei was carried out by a specialist known as the penyunat, who employed simple local tools such as sharpened bamboo referred to as sembilu, particularly in rural or community environments. These folk methods involved direct incision without anesthesia, relying on the boy's restraint during the procedure to complete the removal of the foreskin. Over time, sharpened knives replaced bamboo tools, though the community-based approach persisted. Such non-sterile practices in the pre-modern era carried inherent risks, including potential infections from unsterilized instruments and environments.

### Modern Medical Approaches

In contemporary Brunei, bersunat occurs mainly in Ministry of Health-regulated hospitals and clinics, such as the equipped Kiarong Circumcision Clinic. Services are scheduled during school holidays for efficiency. [14] [15]

Procedures use modern techniques like local anesthesia and electrosurgical units for precise tissue removal. Trained doctors perform them in controlled, hygienic settings, blending surgical standards with ritual timing and communal elements. [14] [16]

## Health and Societal Aspects

### Medical Considerations

In Brunei, national strategies for HIV prevention incorporate voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC), aligning with World Health Organization recommendations that endorse the procedure for reducing heterosexual HIV acquisition risk in men by approximately 60%. [17] [18] This approach adapts global evidence to Brunei's context, where male circumcision prevalence is estimated at 51.9% due to religious norms, potentially amplifying protective effects against HIV and other sexually transmitted infections. [19]

General medical assessments indicate that circumcision confers benefits such as lowered urinary tract infection rates in infancy, though Brunei-specific epidemiological data on post-bersunat outcomes remain limited in public records. [20] Complications, including minor bleeding or infection, occur in under 3% of cases globally, with no localized incidence rates detailed in Bruneian health ministry publications. [21]

### Legal and Policy Framework

In Brunei, male circumcision is obligatory for Muslims under Sharia, with the State Mufti issuing fatwas declaring it so within Sharia-influenced policies. [7] [9] The Ministry of Religious Affairs oversees promotion of such obligations, integrating the procedure into the framework of Sunni practices in this absolute monarchy. [7]
