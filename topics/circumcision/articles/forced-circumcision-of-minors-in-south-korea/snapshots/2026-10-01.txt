# Forced circumcision of minors in South Korea

Forced circumcision of minors in South Korea refers to the parental authorization of surgical foreskin removal on male children or adolescents without the minor's informed consent, a routine non-therapeutic procedure deeply embedded in the country's social and cultural norms. [1] [2]

This practice surged post-Korean War under American influence, evolving from near absence (less than 0.1% in 1945) to over 90% prevalence among high school-aged boys by the late 20th century, though rates have since declined, often performed around ages 9 to 14, commonly during winter school vacations, amid peer pressure and expectations of hygiene or masculinity. [3] [1] [4] Parents typically drive decisions based on perceived benefits like disease prevention, despite medical consensus questioning routine necessity, with procedures sometimes conducted by non-specialists in community settings. [5] [6] Unlike neonatal timing in the United States, South Korean circumcision targets peripubertal youth, reflecting unique adaptation of Western practices without religious mandate. [2]

## Legal Framework

### Parental Authority Over Minors

In South Korean law, parents exercise parental authority over their minor children, which entails the rights and duties to supervise, educate, represent them in juristic acts, and manage their property. [7] This authority extends to decisions on medical procedures for minors, positioning parental authorization of circumcision as a private family matter rather than a public offense.

The legal framework defers to family autonomy in such actions, exempting them from criminal liability under the Criminal Act due to the absence of state involvement, which would otherwise distinguish them from scenarios invoking constitutional protections against bodily harm. Parental decisions on circumcision thus fall within the scope of representation rights, reflecting the view that non-consensual procedures on minors by guardians do not constitute offenses warranting public intervention.

### Criminal Liability Exemptions

In South Korea, parental decisions to circumcise minors fall outside the scope of criminal penalties under the Criminal Act, as they are classified as private exercises of parental authority rather than public offenses requiring state intervention. Provisions such as Article 257 on bodily injury do not extend to these family matters, where parents hold authority over non-consensual procedures on children deemed beneficial or customary. No documented cases exist of parents facing prosecution under broader criminal provisions for authorizing or performing such circumcisions on minors, reinforcing the exemption based on the domestic, non-public character of the act. This contrasts briefly with potential assault applications in non-familial contexts but remains confined to parental discretion here.

### Assault Law Applications

In South Korea, Article 257 of the Criminal Act defines the offense of inflicting bodily injury on another person, punishable by imprisonment or fines. [8] However, this provision has not been applied to parental decisions regarding circumcision of minors, as such acts are viewed through the lens of private family authority rather than public criminal conduct. [8]

No judicial precedents exist where parental circumcision of a minor has been prosecuted as assault or bodily harm under Article 257 or related statutes, reflecting the boundary that exempts routine parental medical choices from criminal scrutiny in the absence of abuse or state involvement. [8] This approach prioritizes the internal regulation of family affairs over intervention via assault laws typically reserved for interpersonal violence outside parental purview.

## Constitutional and Judicial Review

### Challenges to Legal Non-Prohibition

Advocates argue that the lack of statutory prohibitions permits parental actions to override a minor's right to physical integrity in non-consensual circumcision absent medical necessity or the child's informed consent, framing such procedures as unjustified interventions. [9] These arguments invoke broader human rights principles, emphasizing that parental authority, while recognized in family law, should not exempt irreversible alterations to a child's body from scrutiny under constitutional standards of personal dignity and security. [9]

### Court Dismissals and Rationales

In the case of 2021헌마1320, the South Korean Constitutional Court dismissed a constitutional complaint filed by a claimant who alleged that their mother had subjected them to circumcision at age 12 without consent, arguing that the absence of legal prohibitions violated their bodily freedom. [10] The court ruled the complaint inadmissible under Article 68(1) of the Constitutional Court Act, as it did not involve an infringement of basic rights by public authority but rather a private action by a parent exercising authority over a minor. [11]

The rationale emphasized that constitutional protections against violations of fundamental rights, such as bodily integrity, apply specifically to state actions or omissions, not to decisions within private family spheres absent state involvement or compulsion. [10] Without evidence of public power directly enabling or enforcing the procedure, the court found no basis for review, thereby upholding the existing legal framework that treats such parental choices as non-justiciable under constitutional law. [11]

This dismissal reflects the broader judicial position that private familial actions, including those under parental authority, fall outside the scope of constitutional scrutiny, as the Constitution primarily safeguards against state overreach rather than regulating interpersonal or intra-family conduct. [10] Consequently, challenges to non-prohibited practices like forced minor circumcision maintain the status quo, with no obligation for the state to intervene in the absence of public authority's role. [11]

## Adult vs. Minor Distinctions

### Non-Consensual Procedures on Adults

In South Korea, non-consensual circumcision performed on adults may be classified as bodily injury under Article 257 of the Criminal Act, which penalizes acts causing injury to another's body with imprisonment or imprisonment without prison labor for not more than seven years, or by a fine. [8] This provision applies where consent is absent, treating the procedure as an unlawful interference with bodily integrity, unlike the exemptions afforded to parental authority over minors. [8]

The distinction highlights a consent threshold that shifts at the age of majority, enabling potential criminal liability for procedures on adults that would not trigger such charges when authorized by parents for children. [8] No specific court precedents directly addressing non-consensual adult circumcision under this article were identified, but the law's general applicability to non-therapeutic, non-consensual bodily alterations supports this interpretation. [8]

### Implications of Consent Thresholds

In South Korea, the legal framework for medical procedures like circumcision hinges on the age of majority at 19, below which parental proxy consent governs decisions for minors, permitting such interventions without triggering criminal liability for bodily injury. [12] [5] Above this threshold, personal consent becomes mandatory, with absence of it exposing performers to potential charges akin to those for non-consensual adult procedures. This pivot reinforces parental authority as a buffer against public law intervention in minor cases, while emphasizing individual autonomy post-adulthood.

These consent thresholds fuel policy debates on enhancing minors' involvement in healthcare choices, particularly for elective surgeries. Recent initiatives, such as proposals allowing teens aged 14 and older to co-consent for genetic testing, highlight growing recognition of adolescent maturity and could extend to requiring informed assent from older minors for procedures like circumcision, potentially narrowing the proxy consent gap. [13] Such reforms aim to balance familial rights with emerging bodily autonomy norms amid declining circumcision prevalence. [14]

## Cultural and Historical Context

### Origins and Adoption in Korea

Circumcision was not practiced in traditional Korean culture, which lacked any historical or ritualistic basis for the procedure among ethnic Koreans prior to the mid-20th century. [2] [5]

The adoption of male circumcision in South Korea occurred primarily after World War II, coinciding with the end of Japanese colonial rule in 1945 and the subsequent influx of American influence during the U.S. military presence. [14] U.S. military medical personnel and aid programs promoted the procedure as a hygienic measure, framing it as a modern health practice amid post-war reconstruction and exposure to Western medical norms. [15] This external influence marked a departure from indigenous customs, with Korean physicians trained in the U.S. further disseminating the idea through hospitals and public health campaigns emphasizing prevention of infections and diseases. [16]

By the mid-20th century, particularly in the 1950s and 1960s following the Korean War, circumcision transitioned from a novel import to a normalized routine, often performed on school-aged boys rather than neonates, reflecting the procedural adaptations influenced by American models but localized in timing and context. [15] This rapid integration elevated prevalence rates dramatically within a generation, embedding the practice in everyday family and medical decisions. [14]

### Prevalence and Societal Norms

Circumcision of male minors remains highly prevalent in South Korea, with age-standardized rates reaching 75.8% among males aged 14–29, reflecting widespread parental decisions to perform the procedure during childhood or adolescence. [14] While some procedures occur neonatally, a significant portion—around 37%—takes place between ages 9 and 12, often during winter vacations, underscoring the norm of deferring to parental authority over minors' bodily modifications without requiring the child's consent. [17] Winter vacation is widely regarded as an ideal period for the procedure among elementary and middle school boys because the holiday allows recovery without missing classes. Commonly cited advantages include reduced sweating and lower inflammation risk in cold weather plus scheduling convenience, while disadvantages include roughly a week of post-surgical discomfort and possible delays from high seasonal demand. [4] [18]

Societal norms in South Korea treat male circumcision as a routine medical intervention, often justified by beliefs in improved hygiene, prevention of balanoposthitis, reduced risk of penile cancer, and lower STI transmission risk, although it is not strictly necessary with good hygiene practices, with minimal public controversy surrounding parental choices for minors. [19] [20] This acceptance is perpetuated by cultural expectations of conformity, where uncircumcised males may face social disadvantages, such as stigma in group settings like military service or communal bathing. [21] Parents commonly view the procedure as essential for their sons' future well-being, aligning it with broader health practices rather than religious mandates. [5]
