# Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019

The Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that amends section 8(4) of the Children Act 1989 to classify proceedings under Part 1 of Schedule 2 to the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003—excluding paragraph 3—as "family proceedings," thereby integrating female genital mutilation (FGM) protection order applications into the family court framework for England and Wales. [1] Enacted on 15 March 2019 following Royal Assent, the legislation addresses a prior legal gap by permitting courts to issue child welfare orders, such as interim care orders, prohibited steps orders, or supervision orders, concurrently with FGM protection orders (FGMPOs), rather than requiring separate applications. [1] [2]

Introduced as a Private Member's Bill by Crossbench peer Lord Berkeley of Knighton and Conservative MP Zac Goldsmith, the Act builds on existing prohibitions against FGM—criminalized in the UK since 1985 and bolstered by civil protection measures in the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003, as amended by the Serious Crime Act 2015—to enhance judicial efficiency in safeguarding children at risk. [3] [2] By embedding FGMPO proceedings within the Children Act's family proceedings definition, it empowers judges to exercise broader welfare powers without procedural transfers from magistrates' courts, reducing delays in cases involving potential harm to girls from non-therapeutic genital cutting. [2] [3] The government supported the measure, noting its minimal fiscal impact given the relatively low volume of FGMPO applications—117 granted in 2018—while emphasizing its role in prioritizing child protection through unified legal processes. [2]

## Legislative History

### Introduction and Sponsorship

The Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019 is a Private Member's Bill that amends the Children Act 1989 to classify certain proceedings under the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003 as family proceedings, thereby enabling courts to issue broader child welfare orders in cases involving risks of female genital mutilation (FGM). [3] Introduced in the House of Lords during the 2017-19 parliamentary session, the bill was sponsored by Lord Berkeley of Knighton, a Crossbench peer, who identified a specific legal limitation preventing seamless integration of FGM protection measures with existing child safeguarding powers. In the House of Commons, the bill was sponsored by Conservative MP Zac Goldsmith for Richmond Park. [3] [4] [5]

The origins of the bill trace to concerns over jurisdictional silos in the UK's family court system, where applications for FGM Protection Orders (FGMPOs)—introduced by the Serious Crime Act 2015—operated in isolation from the full suite of protective orders available under the Children Act 1989 for general child welfare risks, such as residence or contact restrictions. [3] Lord Berkeley's initiative sought to rectify this technical gap, allowing magistrates' or family courts handling FGMPO applications to concurrently address wider welfare needs without requiring separate proceedings, thus expediting interventions for at-risk children. [3] This motivation aligned with ongoing UK government efforts to strengthen FGM prohibitions, including extraterritorial jurisdiction expansions, but focused narrowly on procedural efficiency rather than imposing new criminal sanctions. [3]

In the sponsorship phase, the bill encountered no significant opposition, reflecting its status as a non-partisan, remedial measure supported by the government, which endorsed its aim to enhance judicial flexibility in FGM-related child protection cases. [3] Lord Berkeley's Crossbench affiliation underscored the bill's apolitical inception, drawing on practical insights into family law applications rather than ideological drivers, with initial tabling emphasizing the need for unified court responses to FGM threats prevalent in certain communities. [3] The legislation applies exclusively to England and Wales, addressing devolved family jurisdiction boundaries. [1]

### Parliamentary Passage

The Bill received its second reading in the House of Lords on 20 July 2018, passing without opposition as members from various parties expressed support for clarifying family court jurisdiction over FGM protection orders. [6] It then advanced through remaining Lords stages with no substantive changes before being sent to the House of Commons. [7]

In the Commons, second reading occurred on 25 February 2019, where the Bill passed on a voice vote amid broad agreement on its procedural aims to protect vulnerable girls. [8] The committee stage followed on 5 March 2019, featuring limited debate and no amendments tabled, indicative of consensus on integrating FGM proceedings into existing child welfare frameworks.

Third reading in the Commons took place on 11 March 2019, again passing without a division and with contributions from MPs highlighting the measure's role in addressing risks to children during overseas travel. [9] The Bill returned to the Lords for formal agreement on 12 March 2019, completing its parliamentary progression with cross-party endorsement and no notable dissent recorded across stages. [7]

### Royal Assent and Enactment

The Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019 received Royal Assent on 15 March 2019, formally enacting it as Chapter 10 of the public general acts for that year. [1] This assent by the monarch, conveyed through the Lord Speaker in the House of Lords and the Speaker in the House of Commons, marked the ceremonial conclusion of the legislative process without requiring further parliamentary approval. [1]

Section 2(2) of the Act specifies that it "comes into force on the day on which it is passed," thereby taking immediate legal effect upon Royal Assent, with no separate commencement order or delay provision needed. [10] The full text was promptly published on the official UK legislation website, legislation.gov.uk, and integrated as an amendment to section 8(4) of the Children Act 1989, extending its application to England and Wales only under section 2(1). [1]

## Legal Provisions

### Core Amendments to the Children Act 1989

The Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019 amends section 8(4) of the Children Act 1989 by inserting a new paragraph (j), which designates proceedings under Part 1 of Schedule 2 to the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003—excluding paragraph 3 relating to appeals—as "family proceedings" for the purposes of the 1989 Act. [1] This textual change expands the scope of family proceedings, enabling family courts to exercise powers under section 8, including the issuance of child arrangements orders, contact orders, specific issue orders, or prohibited steps orders, directly within FGM protection order applications. [1] [11]

Prior to this amendment, FGM-related proceedings under the 2003 Act operated outside the full framework of the Children Act 1989, necessitating separate applications for welfare orders, which could delay interventions. [11] The insertion of subsection (4)(j) rectifies this by aligning FGM protection mechanisms with the 1989 Act's procedural tools, allowing courts to evaluate and mitigate FGM risks concurrently with assessments of the child's overall welfare, such as living arrangements or parental contact, in consolidated hearings. [11]

Explanatory notes to the Act clarify that this provision permits applicants seeking FGM protection orders to request additional orders like care or supervision orders within the same process, thereby avoiding duplicative litigation and enhancing judicial efficiency in child protection cases. [11] The amendment applies exclusively to England and Wales, effective from 15 March 2019, the date of royal assent. [1]

### Linkage to Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003

The Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019 interfaces with the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003 by classifying proceedings for FGM protection orders—governed by section 5A and Part 1 of Schedule 2 of the 2003 Act—as "family proceedings" under section 8(4) of the Children Act 1989. This amendment ensures that applications for orders prohibiting FGM risks, such as removal from the UK or the procedure itself, trigger the broader child protection framework of the Children Act without modifying the 2003 Act's definitions of FGM or its criminal sanctions. [3]

Prior to the 2019 amendment, FGM protection order proceedings under the 2003 Act were not automatically deemed family proceedings, limiting courts' ability to issue interim orders like care or supervision under sections 38 of the Children Act 1989, which could delay protective interventions for at-risk children. [12] The linkage addresses this statutory gap by integrating FGM-specific orders into the family proceedings regime, allowing concurrent use of Children Act powers to safeguard welfare while preserving the standalone enforcement mechanisms of the 2003 Act, such as variation or discharge of protection orders.

This cross-statute mechanism enhances judicial flexibility for cases involving potential FGM, enabling courts to combine prohibitions on mutilation with assessments of a child's overall needs, but it does not expand the scope of FGM offenses or introduce new evidentiary standards under the 2003 Act. [3] The amendment took effect on 15 March 2019, coinciding with royal assent, to streamline responses without overlapping prior bans on FGM established in 2003. [1]

### Procedural Implications for Family Courts

The 2019 amendment to the Children Act 1989 classifies proceedings for female genital mutilation protection orders (FGMPOs) under Part 1 of Schedule 2 to the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003 (excluding paragraph 3) as "family proceedings" by inserting them into section 8(4). [1] This reclassification vests family courts with the full jurisdictional powers of the Children Act 1989 during FGMPO applications, permitting the issuance of ancillary orders such as interim care orders under section 38 or supervision orders under section 31, based on thresholds of significant harm posed by FGM risks. [11] Such integration enables courts to conduct holistic child welfare evaluations, addressing not only immediate FGM threats but also associated familial or environmental factors contributing to harm.

Previously, FGMPO proceedings operated outside the core family proceedings framework, necessitating parallel applications to invoke Children Act powers, which often protracted emergency responses and increased procedural complexity. [11] The amendment mitigates this by allowing simultaneous pursuit of FGMPOs and broader protective measures within unified proceedings, thereby expediting judicial interventions and reducing the administrative burden on courts handling at-risk children. [11]

Local authorities, empowered as relevant third parties under the Female Genital Mutilation Protection Order (Relevant Third Party) Regulations 2015, gain enhanced procedural leverage to initiate or intervene in FGM-related family proceedings. This facilitates proactive assessments under the Children Act's harm threshold—where FGM constitutes physical or emotional harm—without requiring distinct evidentiary tracks, streamlining local authority duties in safeguarding referrals and multi-agency collaborations. [11]

## Context of Female Genital Mutilation in the UK

### Prevalence and Demographic Patterns

Female genital mutilation (FGM) in the United Kingdom is predominantly concentrated within immigrant communities originating from countries in Africa and parts of the Middle East where the practice is culturally entrenched, with prevalence estimates derived from demographic analyses of the 2011 Census for England and Wales indicating approximately 137,000 women and girls aged 0 and over born in such countries had undergone FGM. [13] This total encompasses 103,000 women aged 15-49 (prevalence rate of 7.7 per 1,000 women in that group), 24,000 women aged 50 and over (2.3 per 1,000), and nearly 10,000 girls aged 0-14 (2.0 per 1,000). [13] Over half of these cases (around 53,000 women aged 15-49) trace to Horn of Africa nations such as Somalia, Eritrea, and Sudan, where FGM—often Type III infibulation—is near-universal, while significant portions originate from East and West African countries like Kenya, Nigeria, and Sierra Leone, associated with Types I and II. [13]

Geographic patterns show urban clustering, with London exhibiting the highest regional rate at 21.0 per 1,000 population, and specific boroughs like Southwark (47.4 per 1,000) and Brent (38.9 per 1,000) far exceeding national averages; outside London, elevated rates appear in cities including Manchester, Birmingham, Leicester, and Bristol (12-16 per 1,000). [13] Dispersion occurs across nearly all local authorities, though rural areas typically register below 1 per 1,000, reflecting migration patterns from FGM-prevalent regions. [13] Between 2001 and 2011, the population of women aged 15-49 born in these countries residing in England and Wales rose from 182,000 to 283,000, driven by increases from the Horn of Africa (up 34,000). [13]

Risks extend to UK-born girls of affected diaspora communities, with approximately 60,000 girls aged 0-14 in 2011 born to mothers from FGM-practicing countries, over half to those from the Horn of Africa. [13] Elevated incidence occurs during school holidays, termed the "cutting season," when families may transport daughters abroad for the procedure or import practitioners domestically, pooling resources for group cuttings. [14] [15] Activists highlight winter and summer breaks as peak periods for overseas travel to origin countries, sustaining the practice across generations despite UK prohibitions. [14]

Since the 2015 inception of the NHS Female Genital Mutilation Enhanced Dataset, healthcare providers in England have recorded notifications for FGM in clinical settings, with quarterly reports documenting ongoing identifications primarily among women from the same demographic groups. [16] These data underscore persistence in affected communities, though underreporting limits full prevalence capture. [17]

### Health and Causal Harms of FGM

Female genital mutilation (FGM) entails the partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to female genital organs for non-medical reasons, resulting in immediate physical risks including severe pain, excessive bleeding (hemorrhage), shock from blood loss, urinary retention due to tissue swelling, and infections such as wound infections, sepsis, tetanus, or transmission of HIV and hepatitis B and C when performed with shared unsterilized instruments. [18] [19] These complications arise directly from the invasive cutting, often without anesthesia or sterile conditions, leading to acute trauma and potential death in severe cases, particularly with higher WHO-classified types like infibulation (Type III), which involves narrowing the vaginal opening. [20]

Long-term physical harms include chronic genital pain, keloid scar formation, female genital tract infections, menstrual difficulties such as dysmenorrhea and retention of menstrual blood, infertility linked to adhesions or blockages, and sexual dysfunction manifested as dyspareunia (painful intercourse) and reduced sexual satisfaction due to nerve damage and tissue removal. [21] [22] A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed elevated risks of urogenital fistulas, cysts, and keloids, with odds ratios indicating statistically significant associations between FGM exposure and these outcomes across multiple studies. [21] Childbirth complications are causally tied to scarring and anatomical alterations, increasing maternal risks of postpartum hemorrhage, extended labor, and perineal tears, while elevating neonatal mortality by up to 55% in cases of prolonged labor from infibulation. [19] [20]

Psychological sequelae, empirically linked to the trauma of the procedure and its lifelong repercussions, encompass post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, depression, and somatic disorders, with studies reporting higher prevalence among FGM survivors compared to uncircumcised peers, independent of socioeconomic confounders. [22] No evidence supports health benefits from FGM; systematic reviews affirm it confers zero medical advantages while imposing verifiable harms across physical, sexual, and mental domains. [18] [22] The persistence of FGM despite these causally attributable damages stems from non-health rationales like cultural rites or social control, underscoring the disconnect between practice and empirical harm data, which prioritizes child bodily integrity over relativistic justifications. [19]

### Prior Legislative Framework

The Prohibition of Female Circumcision Act 1985 established the first statutory prohibition on female genital mutilation (FGM) in the United Kingdom, criminalizing the excision, infibulation, or mutilation of the labia majora, labia minora, or clitoris, with a maximum penalty of five years' imprisonment; the Act applied to England, Wales, Scotland, and came into force on 16 September 1985. [23] This legislation targeted the practice primarily within domestic borders but lacked provisions for extraterritorial application or assisting self-mutilation. [24]

The Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003 repealed and replaced the 1985 Act, expanding the offence to include assisting a girl in mutilating her own genitalia and aiding UK nationals or permanent residents in performing FGM overseas, while raising the maximum sentence to 14 years' imprisonment; it applied to England, Wales, and Northern Ireland. [25] These changes aimed to address gaps in scope and deterrence, reflecting growing awareness of FGM's prevalence among diaspora communities, though prosecutions remained rare prior to 2015. [24]

Subsequent amendments via the Serious Crime Act 2015 enhanced protections by introducing Female Genital Mutilation Protection Orders (FGMPOs), civil injunctions enabling courts to impose restrictions on individuals to prevent FGM, alongside extraterritorial jurisdiction for cases involving UK girls taken abroad, a mandatory reporting duty for regulated professionals upon discovering FGM in under-18s, and a new offence of failing to protect a girl from FGM punishable by up to seven years' imprisonment. [26] [27]

A notable shortfall in this framework was the separation of FGMPO proceedings from family law mechanisms under the Children Act 1989, which governed child welfare and arrangements orders; this disconnection barred courts from issuing concurrent Children Act orders during FGMPO applications, fostering potential inconsistencies and delays in holistic child protection responses. [3]

## Implementation and Enforcement

### Operational Mechanisms Post-2019

Following the enactment of the Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019, family courts operationalize Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) protection orders (FGMPOs) by classifying proceedings under Part 1 of Schedule 2 to the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003 (excluding paragraph 3) as "family proceedings" under section 8(4)(j) of the Children Act 1989. [1] This integration permits courts to apply a range of protective powers from the Children Act, including interim care orders under section 38, which grant local authorities shared parental responsibility to safeguard a child at risk, and emergency protection orders (EPOs) under section 44, allowing temporary removal to suitable accommodation for up to eight days (extendable by seven). [28] Applications for FGMPOs, made via Form FGM001 in the family court, can incorporate these mechanisms without separate proceedings, enabling courts to impose conditions such as travel restrictions or passport surrender alongside care arrangements. [28]

Operational coordination occurs through multi-agency strategy meetings, convened within two working days of a referral, involving family courts, local authorities, and other professionals to align FGMPO enforcement with Children Act interventions. [28] In concurrent civil and criminal matters, courts may issue FGMPOs during existing family proceedings or even criminal trials for FGM offences, extending protection to siblings or others at risk, while adhering to Family Procedure Rules 2010 for information sharing and avoiding duplication. [28] Local authorities, designated as relevant third parties, apply directly for FGMPOs without court leave, conducting section 47 enquiries to assess harm and initiating care orders if needed. [28]

Police and social services facilitate streamlined referrals under mandatory reporting obligations in section 5B of the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003, with regulated professionals notifying police of identified FGM cases in girls under 18, prompting immediate referral to children's social care. [28] Police enforce FGMPOs by serving orders at local stations, arresting for breaches (prosecutable as contempt or criminal offence), and invoking section 46 police protection powers for up to 72 hours in imminent risk scenarios, notifying social services concurrently. [28] Social services, via Multi-Agency Safeguarding Hubs where available, process referrals to trigger court applications, ensuring linkage between FGM-specific orders and broader child welfare processes under the Children Act. [28]

### Statistical Outcomes and Case Examples

Following the enactment of the Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019, Ministry of Justice statistics recorded 199 applications for female genital mutilation (FGM) protection orders in family courts for that year, reflecting an initial surge likely tied to expanded procedural access under section 8 proceedings. [29] Subsequent years showed variability, with 136 applications in 2020, 54 in 2021, and 71 in 2022, indicating a modest post-amendment uptick amid broader fluctuations possibly influenced by external factors like the COVID-19 pandemic reducing court activity. [29] These figures derive from HM Courts and Tribunals Service data, capturing cases commencing in England and Wales family courts.

In practice, the amendment has enabled courts to issue FGM protection orders concurrently with child welfare hearings, as demonstrated in a 2020 Court of Appeal ruling upholding such an order for a child at risk despite contrary immigration tribunal findings on credibility. [30] The court prioritized empirical risk assessment under the Children Act, prohibiting travel to high-prevalence countries and mandating safeguarding measures to avert mutilation, thereby illustrating the provision's role in preventive civil interventions. Similarly, in Re X (Female Genital Mutilation Protection Order) (No 2) [2019], the High Court granted an order under the linked frameworks, restricting family actions based on evidence of cultural intent, which prevented potential harm without requiring criminal thresholds. [31]

Criminal prosecutions under the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003, which the 2019 amendment complements through family court linkages, have remained persistently low, with only isolated convictions recorded since 2015—such as the landmark 2019 Old Bailey case involving a Ugandan woman sentenced for mutilating a three-year-old. [32] By 2023, total FGM convictions numbered fewer than five, primarily attributable to evidential hurdles including long-delayed disclosures, absence of contemporaneous medical proof, and jurisdictional issues over overseas acts, rather than gaps in the amended civil-criminal interface. [33] Crown Prosecution Service guidance underscores these causal barriers, noting that while mandatory reporting aids detection, proving specific intent and causation often falters absent direct witnesses. [33]

## Reception, Impact, and Criticisms

### Positive Assessments and Achievements

The Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019 has been commended for addressing a prior legislative gap by empowering family courts to issue FGM protection orders (FGMPOs) directly within proceedings under the Children Act 1989, such as those for care or supervision orders, thereby avoiding the need for separate applications and enabling faster judicial interventions to safeguard at-risk girls. [8] This integration was described by supporters in Parliament as a vital enhancement to child protection frameworks, allowing judges to incorporate FGM risks holistically into welfare assessments without procedural fragmentation. [9]

Advocates, including crossbench peer Lord Berkeley who initiated the Bill, emphasized its role in amplifying existing anti-FGM measures introduced under the Serious Crime Act 2015, positioning it as a practical advancement in preventing harm to vulnerable children who lack a voice. [9] FGM campaigner Nimco Ali, an FGM survivor and prominent advocate, was publicly acknowledged by MPs for her contributions to elevating the issue, with her efforts credited for sustaining momentum behind legislative refinements like this amendment that prioritize girl safety. [34]

Post-enactment, the Ministry of Justice's quarterly family court statistics reflect ongoing utilization of FGMPOs within integrated proceedings, with data indicating their role as a key tool for prevention and victim support, as affirmed in government responses to parliamentary inquiries. [35] This has been viewed by proponents as evidence of improved operational efficacy in family courts, facilitating more cohesive responses to FGM risks amid broader child welfare cases. [36]

### Challenges in Enforcement and Cultural Resistance

While the 2019 amendment enhances civil protections via integrated family court proceedings, broader challenges in addressing FGM persist, including low criminal prosecution rates for FGM offenses—with only a handful of convictions since 1985 despite tens of thousands of cases identified by the NHS since 2015—and evidential difficulties such as proving procedures performed abroad. [37] [38] Victim reluctance to testify against family members due to fear of ostracism or reprisal remains a key obstacle. [33] [39]

Cultural norms within practicing communities perpetuate resistance by framing FGM as an essential rite of passage or family honor obligation, discouraging reporting and cooperation. [40] This is compounded by community pressures, with under 10% of police referrals resulting in charges. [41] Surveys indicate many families prioritize tradition over legal risks. [42]

Evasion tactics, such as taking girls overseas during school holidays, continue, with the amendment's focus on domestic civil protections not directly addressing cross-border risks. [43] [14] NHS data show a 15% rise in identified cases in 2023-2024, highlighting ongoing challenges. [38]

### Debates on Multiculturalism and Child Protection Priorities

The Children Act 1989 (Amendment) (Female Genital Mutilation) Act 2019 contributed to ongoing debates over balancing multicultural accommodation with child protection. Some academics argue stringent FGM prohibitions stigmatize migrant communities by imposing Western norms, overlooking cultural significances and comparing to procedures like labiaplasty, while eroding trust via mandatory reporting. [44] [45]

Opponents prioritize universal child rights, citing FGM's harms like chronic pain and complications, rejecting relativism. Estimates indicate over 137,000 affected women and girls in the UK, mainly from high-prevalence countries like Somalia and Egypt. [46] Figures such as Claire Coutinho have called FGM an "abhorrent act" requiring firm enforcement. [45]

Critics note the amendment's judicial focus does not tackle root causes like immigration from endemic areas without integration measures. [47] Studies affirm no benefits to FGM, emphasizing child protection over cultural concessions. [48]
