Circumcision surgical procedure

Fact-checked by Grok · 2 days ago
Circumcision is the surgical removal of the foreskin, or prepuce, that covers the glans of the penis.[1] Performed predominantly on male newborns but also on adolescents and adults, the procedure serves religious, cultural, and medical purposes, with global prevalence estimated at 30-38% of males. Common techniques for neonatal circumcision include the Gomco clamp, Mogen clamp, and Plastibell device, which facilitate precise excision while controlling hemorrhage through compression or ligation.[2] Empirical evidence from systematic reviews indicates preventive benefits, such as a 90-100% reduction in urinary tract infections during infancy, decreased incidence of penile cancer, and lowered heterosexual transmission of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections.[3][4] Complication rates in neonatal settings are low, typically under 0.5-1%, encompassing minor issues like bleeding or infection, though severe outcomes such as excessive skin removal or necrosis occur rarely when performed by trained providers.[3][5] Despite these findings, infant circumcision sparks ethical debate, with critics contending it infringes on bodily autonomy by altering functional tissue absent immediate therapeutic need, while proponents emphasize net health gains and parental decision-making authority.[6][7]

Overview

Definition and Procedure Basics

Circumcision is the surgical removal of the foreskin, or prepuce, which is the retractable fold of skin covering the glans of the penis.[1] This procedure exposes the glans permanently and is one of the most common surgical interventions worldwide, particularly among newborns.[1] [8] The foreskin consists of an outer layer continuous with the penile shaft skin and an inner mucosal layer that adheres to the glans during early development, separating naturally by age 3 in most males.[1] The basic procedure involves retracting or freeing the foreskin from adhesions to the glans, marking the line of excision, and then cutting away the redundant preputial tissue while preserving sufficient shaft skin for normal penile function.[1] It is typically performed as an outpatient surgery under local anesthesia, lasting 5 to 10 minutes in neonates, with hemostasis achieved through clamping, cautery, or ligation.[1] In adults or older children, circumcision is frequently performed under local anesthesia with a dorsal penile nerve block or subcutaneous ring block, allowing the patient to remain awake while numbing the procedure site effectively. This is the preferred method in many urology practices, with reports indicating over 95% of adult patients opting for local anesthesia due to its safety, minimal risks compared to deeper sedation, and suitability for outpatient settings. General anesthesia or spinal anesthesia may be used in cases involving significant patient anxiety, panic disorders, or other medical considerations, but these are less common, and dissolvable sutures often close the wound edges. Post-procedure care includes petroleum jelly application to prevent adhesions and monitoring for bleeding or infection, with healing generally complete in 7 to 10 days.[8]

Variations by Age and Setting

Neonatal circumcision, typically performed within the first 28 days of life and often in the first week, employs specialized devices such as the Gomco clamp, Mogen clamp, or Plastibell ring to excise the foreskin, minimizing tissue trauma and enabling rapid healing without sutures.[1] These procedures are usually conducted in hospital or outpatient settings under local anesthesia like dorsal penile nerve block, with complication rates ranging from 0.2% to 0.6%, primarily minor bleeding or swelling.[9] In contrast, infant circumcisions beyond the neonatal period but before age one year show slightly elevated risks, with adverse events approximately twofold higher than in newborns due to increased penile size and vascularity complicating device use.[9] For children aged 1 to 9 years, techniques shift toward open surgical methods like dorsal slit or sleeve resection, often requiring general anesthesia owing to greater patient cooperation needs and pain sensitivity, with complication incidences rising 10- to 20-fold compared to neonates, including higher rates of bleeding (up to 5%) and infection.[9] [10] Adolescent and adult circumcisions (typically post-puberty) favor freehand excision or device-assisted approaches like the Shang Ring or PrePex, performed under regional or general anesthesia to manage substantial discomfort and erection risks, yielding complication rates of 2-10%, with prolonged recovery (up to 4-6 weeks) and elevated hemorrhage or adhesions due to thicker foreskin and higher vascular density.[11] [1] Empirical data indicate overall adverse event rates increase progressively with age, from under 1% in newborns to over 5% in adults, attributable to anatomical maturation and procedural complexity.[12] There is no strict upper age limit for circumcision in adult men; the procedure can be performed at any age, including in older adults (e.g., 60s, 70s, or 80s+), provided the individual is in reasonable health following medical evaluation. Risks such as bleeding, infection, longer recovery times, and anesthesia complications generally increase with age, but many men successfully undergo it for medical or personal reasons.[13][14] In medical settings such as hospitals or clinics, procedures adhere to sterile protocols with trained surgeons, yielding lower complication frequencies (median 2-6% across ages) through standardized anesthesia and hemostasis.[10] Ritual or traditional settings, including Jewish brit milah by mohels or tribal initiations, often forgo anesthesia and modern clamps in favor of sharp instruments like ritual knives, resulting in substantially higher risks—up to 14% severe complications like excessive bleeding or sepsis—due to non-sterile conditions and untrained performers.[10] Outpatient non-ritual circumcisions mirror hospital safety when device-based but elevate risks in resource-limited environments without oversight.[9] Across settings, neonatal timing in controlled medical environments empirically minimizes morbidity compared to delayed or ceremonial procedures.[3]

Indications and Empirical Rationale

Medical Benefits Supported by Evidence

Circumcision in male infants reduces the risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) during the first year of life by approximately 90%, with meta-analyses indicating a number needed to treat (NNT) of 111 to prevent one UTI in otherwise healthy boys.[15] This protective effect stems from the removal of the foreskin, which can harbor bacteria under suboptimal hygiene conditions, as evidenced by cohort studies showing a 10-fold lower incidence in circumcised versus uncircumcised infants.[16] The benefit persists into later childhood, with a 6.6-fold risk reduction observed in boys aged 1-16 years.[16] In adulthood, circumcision substantially lowers the incidence of invasive penile cancer, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses reporting odds ratios as low as 0.33 for men circumcised in childhood or adolescence compared to uncircumcised men.[17] This reduction is attributed to decreased chronic inflammation, phimosis-related issues, and human papillomavirus (HPV) persistence under the foreskin, conditions that facilitate carcinogenesis; penile cancer remains rare overall, with annual U.S. incidence rates of 1 in 100,000 uncircumcised men versus near-zero in circumcised populations.[17] [18] Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in sub-Saharan Africa demonstrate that adult male circumcision reduces heterosexual HIV acquisition by 50-60%, with three major trials (involving over 10,000 participants) showing sustained efficacy over 5-6 years of follow-up.[19] Meta-analyses confirm this effect, attributing it to the foreskin's vulnerability to microtears and epithelial disruption during intercourse, which increases HIV entry points; the protective estimate holds in high-prevalence settings but requires consideration of behavioral and epidemiological context for generalizability.[20] [19] Similar evidence supports reductions in HPV (by 30-35%) and herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) acquisition (by 25-30%), both linked to foreskin biology.[1] Circumcision also mitigates risks of phimosis, balanitis, and paraphimosis, with longitudinal data indicating these conditions affect up to 10% of uncircumcised males versus negligible rates post-neonatal circumcision.[1] The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) 2012 policy, reaffirmed in subsequent reviews, concludes that these cumulative benefits—encompassing UTI prevention, STI reduction, and lower cancer risk—outweigh procedural risks in newborns, supporting informed parental decision-making without universal recommendation.[21] The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) echoes this, estimating neonatal circumcision averts 1 in 100 UTIs and contributes to long-term STI prevention based on systematic evidence reviews.[22]

Cultural, Religious, and Preventive Contexts

In Judaism, male circumcision, known as brit milah, constitutes a core religious covenant established in Genesis 17:10–14, mandating the procedure on the eighth day after birth for all male infants as a perpetual sign of the agreement between God and Abraham's descendants.[23] This ritual, performed by a mohel using a scalpel or similar instrument, includes additional elements such as the prikah (tearing of remaining tissue) and application of a styptic agent, and remains nearly universally observed among Orthodox and Conservative Jews, with over 99% adherence in Israel.[24] In Islam, circumcision (khitan) follows the sunnah of Prophet Muhammad rather than explicit Quranic command, emphasizing ritual purity (fitrah) and hygiene, and is classified as obligatory by Hanafi and Maliki schools or recommended by others, with no fixed timing but commonly performed between infancy and puberty—often around ages 7–10 in many traditions.[25] [26] Prevalence exceeds 90% in Muslim-majority countries like Turkey, Indonesia, and those in the Middle East and North Africa.[27] Christianity generally rejects ritual circumcision as a requirement for salvation or covenant, with the New Testament—particularly Acts 15 and Paul's epistles—affirming its obsolescence for Gentile converts, viewing spiritual "circumcision of the heart" through faith as sufficient.[28] [29] Exceptions persist in some Ethiopian Orthodox and Coptic traditions, where it holds cultural continuity from pre-Christian practices, but rates remain low in most Western Christian populations, under 20% in Europe excluding immigrant communities.[27] Culturally, circumcision spread in the 19th-century Anglosphere amid Victorian concerns over hygiene and morality; in the United States, rates rose from negligible in 1870 to peaks of 80–90% by the 1970s, driven by physicians like Lewis Sayre who advocated it to prevent conditions such as phimosis, paralysis, and masturbation-induced neurosis, framing the foreskin as a vector for infection and vice.[30] By 2022, U.S. newborn rates had declined to approximately 58%, influenced by immigration patterns, cost barriers post-1999 Medicaid exclusions in some states, and shifting parental preferences, though it persists non-religiously in about 55–60% of cases.[31] Globally, about 30–38% of males are circumcised, with highest non-religious concentrations in the U.S., Philippines, and South Korea, often tied to perceived cleanliness or conformity.[27] [32] Preventive contexts historically emphasized prophylaxis against disease and behavioral issues; Victorian proponents, including British surgeon Jonathan Hutchinson, promoted it to avert syphilis, epilepsy, and "self-abuse," with endorsements extending to routine infant practice by the early 20th century despite limited empirical support at the time.[33] In contemporary public health, the World Health Organization endorsed voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) in 2007 for high-HIV-prevalence regions in sub-Saharan Africa, citing randomized trials showing 50–60% reduction in heterosexual acquisition risk, leading to over 30 million procedures by 2023 in 15 priority countries to curb epidemics where prevalence exceeds 10%.[34] Programs target adolescents and adults, integrating it with education and testing, though uptake varies due to access, stigma, and traditional practices.[35]

Preoperative Preparation

Patient Assessment and Contraindications

Patient assessment prior to circumcision involves a thorough medical history review to identify any familial or personal bleeding disorders, such as hemophilia or von Willebrand disease, and evaluation of overall health status to ensure the patient is stable for an elective procedure.[1] [3] A physical examination focuses on the genitalia to detect anatomic anomalies, including assessment of penile curvature, preputial adhesions, and skin conditions, with particular scrutiny in neonates where such issues may be subtle.[1] [36] Laboratory tests, such as coagulation profiles or complete blood counts, may be indicated in cases of suspected hematologic risks but are not routinely required for healthy infants.[37] Contraindications to circumcision encompass conditions that increase procedural risks or necessitate alternative management, primarily congenital penile abnormalities like hypospadias, epispadias, chordee, buried or concealed penis, webbed penis, or micropenis, which can compromise surgical outcomes or require prior correction.[1] [36] Active infections, including urinary tract infections or balanitis, represent absolute contraindications due to heightened risk of dissemination or poor healing.[1] Bleeding diatheses, confirmed or familial, contraindicate the procedure absent specialized hematologic oversight, as excessive hemorrhage can occur even in minor surgeries.[38] Neonatal instability, such as prematurity with instability or acute illness, warrants postponement, as circumcision is elective and deferred until recovery to minimize complications.[1] Ambiguous genitalia mandates endocrinologic and genetic evaluation prior to any intervention.[36] In older children or adults, additional considerations include phimosis secondary to lichen sclerosus or penile trauma, where circumcision may exacerbate underlying pathology.[1]

Anesthesia, Analgesia, and Consent Processes

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends that effective analgesia be provided for all newborn circumcisions, as the procedure causes significant pain comparable to that experienced by older children and adults.[39][40] Local anesthesia is the primary method, with dorsal penile nerve block (DPNB) involving injection of lidocaine at the base of the penis to interrupt sensory nerves, proven safe and effective in reducing procedural pain in neonates.[41][42] Subcutaneous ring block, another local technique encircling the penile base with anesthetic, provides superior pain relief compared to DPNB in randomized trials, though both carry minor risks such as hematoma or penile ischemia if improperly administered.[43] Topical agents like EMLA cream (eutectic mixture of lidocaine and prilocaine) offer partial relief when applied 60-90 minutes prior but are less effective alone than injectable blocks, often used adjunctively.[44][45] General anesthesia is rarely used for neonatal procedures due to higher risks in infants, reserved for older children or complex cases.[42] Analgesia extends beyond anesthesia to multimodal approaches for optimal pain control. Oral sucrose solution (2 mL of 24% concentration) administered via pacifier provides significant early pain reduction during circumcision phases like clamping, though effects wane later without local agents.[46] Combination regimens, such as ring block plus EMLA and sucrose, minimize pain scores across the procedure more effectively than single modalities, as evidenced by randomized controlled trials measuring neonatal facial expressions, heart rate, and crying duration.[43][45] Postoperatively, acetaminophen (10-15 mg/kg every 4-6 hours for 24 hours) is recommended to manage residual discomfort, with evidence from protocols showing reduced need for additional interventions.[47] Non-pharmacologic aids like swaddling or non-nutritive sucking enhance but do not replace pharmacologic methods.[48] Informed consent for circumcision requires parental authorization, as the procedure is elective and performed on minors incapable of consenting. Providers must disclose the nature of the surgery—removal of the foreskin via incision or device—along with evidence-based benefits (e.g., reduced urinary tract infection risk), risks (e.g., bleeding in 0.2-0.6% of cases, infection in 1-2%), alternatives (e.g., conservative hygiene), and pain management options.[49][50] Consent forms typically affirm understanding of these elements and authorize the provider to proceed or adapt as needed intraoperatively.[51] Ethical debates persist, with some legal scholars arguing that non-therapeutic infant circumcision infringes bodily autonomy and should require adult consent, but prevailing medical and legal standards uphold parental proxy decision-making for minors' procedures absent imminent harm.[52][50] Documentation must reflect voluntary agreement without coercion, adhering to jurisdictional guidelines like those from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG).[53]

Surgical Techniques

Open Surgical Methods

Open surgical methods for circumcision involve the direct excision of the foreskin without reliance on clamps, shields, or disposable devices, allowing for precise control over tissue removal and suitable for patients across age groups including neonates, children, and adults.[1] These techniques prioritize anatomical landmarks to ensure adequate glans exposure while minimizing risks to underlying structures such as the frenulum and penile blood supply.[2] The two primary variants are the dorsal slit method and the sleeve resection (or excision) method, each offering distinct approaches to foreskin division and removal.[1] In the dorsal slit technique, the foreskin is first retracted or incised longitudinally along its dorsal aspect from the tip to the coronal sulcus, creating an opening that exposes the glans and facilitates further excision.[54] This initial slit, typically 1-2 cm in length depending on foreskin redundancy, is extended by crushing and dividing the inner and outer preputial layers, followed by trimming excess tissue circumferentially while preserving ventral structures like the frenulum when possible.[54] Hemostasis is achieved through electrocautery or ties, and the mucosal edge is approximated to the shaft skin with interrupted absorbable sutures.[2] This method is particularly useful in cases of phimosis or when minimal equipment is available, as it requires only basic surgical instruments, though it demands careful extension to avoid uneven scarring or excessive glans coverage.[1] The sleeve resection technique employs two parallel circumferential incisions: one on the outer preputial skin proximal to the desired scar line and another on the inner mucosal layer at or near the coronal sulcus after foreskin retraction over the glans.[2] The segment of foreskin between these incisions—forming a "sleeve"—is connected by dorsal and ventral longitudinal cuts and excised en bloc, ensuring uniform removal while assessing penile vasculature to prevent ischemia.[2] Remaining edges are undermined slightly for tension-free closure with fine absorbable sutures, often in a subcuticular fashion to optimize cosmesis.[55] Adaptable for local or general anesthesia, this approach excels in adults due to its precision in controlling scar placement and frenulum handling, with operative times averaging 20-30 minutes in experienced hands.[55] Studies indicate comparable complication rates to device methods when performed by trained surgeons, including bleeding under 5% and infection rates below 2%, though it may involve slightly higher suture use.[56] Both methods necessitate sterile conditions, penile block anesthesia for pain control, and intraoperative verification of adequate skin mobility to avert postoperative phimosis recurrence.[1] They contrast with device-assisted procedures by enabling customization for anatomical variations, such as buried penis or redundant prepuce, but require surgeon proficiency to mitigate risks like asymmetry or meatal stenosis, reported in 1-3% of cases across series.[2]

Device-Assisted Methods

Device-assisted circumcision methods employ clamps or plastic rings to compress the foreskin, facilitating its excision with reduced bleeding and operative time, primarily for neonatal cases. Common devices include the Gomco clamp, Mogen clamp, and Plastibell, each providing mechanical hemostasis through tissue crushing or ligation. These techniques prioritize glans protection and precise skin removal, with complication rates generally under 5% in controlled settings.[39][57] The Gomco clamp procedure begins with dorsal slitting of the foreskin to break adhesions, followed by insertion of a metal bell over the glans to shield it. The foreskin is then drawn over the bell and secured in a crushing clamp applied to the shaft, with excision performed proximal to the clamp using scissors. The clamp remains in place for approximately five minutes to achieve hemostasis before removal, yielding a uniform scar line. This method controls foreskin quantity removed and minimizes glans injury risk.[58][59][60] In comparative trials, Gomco circumcision exhibits a postoperative bleeding incidence of 4.42%, higher than some alternatives but with overall low adverse event rates.[61] The Mogen clamp technique involves retracting the foreskin, aligning the clamp's slot along the coronal sulcus to enclose the glans margin, drawing excess foreskin through the slot for compression, and transecting it with scissors. Performed rapidly—often in under two minutes—it requires minimal training and associates with reduced bleeding due to immediate crushing.[62][2][39] Community-based analyses report Mogen-associated complications at 2%, exceeding Gomco's 0.9%, potentially linked to glans amputation risks if misaligned.[63] Plastibell circumcision selects a bell sized to the glans, inserts it beneath the foreskin after adhesion lysis, advances the foreskin over the bell, ties a suture tightly into the bell's groove for ligation, and excises protruding foreskin. The device maintains compression postoperatively, promoting necrosis and auto-amputation of the ligated segment with the bell's detachment in 5-10 days, obviating immediate clamp removal.[64][65][39] Controlled studies indicate Plastibell bleeding at 1.06%, lower than Gomco, though overall complication rates reach 7.08% in some cohorts, including ring migration or incomplete sloughing.[61][66] Recent large-scale studies reinforce that Plastibell and Gomco techniques show no significant differences in overall post-procedural complications, required interventions, or unplanned hospital visits. A 2021 single-center review of 746 office circumcisions (257 Gomco, 489 Plastibell) reported hazard ratios of 0.9 for complications, 0.89 for interventions, and 1.0 for hospital visits (all non-significant), though Plastibell cases had higher emergency/urgent care presentations (odds ratio 1.6, p=0.02) and more parental phone calls (63% vs 52%), likely due to anxiety over the retained ring.[67] A 2025 comparative analysis of Mogen, Gomco, and Plastibell similarly found no statistically significant differences in complication rates or ER returns.[57] Plastibell remains favored for infants (approximately 60% of U.S. cases) due to lower immediate bleeding risk from ongoing ligation, while Gomco avoids foreign body retention. Overall, outcomes depend more on provider experience and comfort than device choice, with both methods safe when performed skillfully.

Adult and Revision Procedures

Adult circumcision procedures are generally performed on an outpatient basis under local anesthesia, utilizing dorsal penile nerve block at the base of the penis and/or circumferential ring block with a lidocaine/bupivacaine mixture without epinephrine, often supplemented with sedation or regional blocks to manage discomfort from the greater innervation and vascularity of adult penile tissue compared to infants.[1][68] Key steps include: preparation with informed consent, cleaning the area with antiseptic, and sterile draping; administration of the local anesthetic as described; and execution of the surgical procedure, typically via the sleeve resection technique predominating, which involves marking incision lines on the outer and inner prepuce, making two circumferential incisions to create a "sleeve" of foreskin, excising the sleeve while preserving adequate shaft skin to prevent tension during erection, controlling bleeding with cautery or ties for [hemostasis](/page/Hemostasis], reapproximating skin edges with absorbable interrupted sutures (e.g., 4-0 or 5-0 Vicryl), and addressing the frenulum if needed with a stitch; followed by application of a petroleum gauze or similar dressing.[1][55] This method allows precise control over the amount of skin removed. Dorsal slit techniques may be employed initially for tight phimosis, followed by complete excision, though freehand methods without clamps are favored in adults to accommodate penile size variability and reduce device-related risks like necrosis.[1] Procedures take 30-60 minutes, with postoperative care emphasizing wound hygiene, pain management, abstinence from sexual activity for 4-6 weeks, and monitoring for erection-induced bleeding; recovery involves swelling and soreness resolving in days to weeks.[14] Complication rates in adult circumcision range from 0.9% to 8.8%, exceeding those in neonates due to factors such as patient comorbidities, anticoagulation use, and involuntary erections complicating hemostasis; common issues include bleeding (up to 5%), infection (1-3%), and wound dehiscence, with rare severe events like glans ischemia.[69] [12] Device-assisted methods, while faster by approximately 17 minutes, show marginally higher minor adverse events like edema or need for antibiotics compared to open surgery, though no increase in serious complications.[70] [71] Revision procedures correct suboptimal outcomes from prior circumcisions, such as redundant or asymmetric foreskin remnants, skin bridges, buried penis, or excessive scarring, which occur in 1-5% of cases overall but prompt surgical intervention in dissatisfied adults seeking functional or aesthetic improvement.[72] [73] In adults, circumcision revision for redundant inner prepuce (also termed redundant foreskin or incomplete circumcision) generally has favorable outcomes. A 2011 study of 48 men undergoing revision (23% for redundant foreskin) reported 96% achieving good aesthetic and functional results after one procedure, with minor complications (partial wound gaping) in only 2 cases that resolved conservatively; 4% required a second revision.[74] Techniques involve local excision of excess tissue, adhesiolysis, or Z-plasty for scar revision under local anesthesia, with rare use of grafts for significant skin deficits; indications prioritize symptomatic issues like recurrent balanitis over pure cosmesis.[73] [75] Revision carries risks of bleeding, infection, or worsened scarring (rates 2-10%), necessitating experienced surgeons to minimize recurrence, with recovery mirroring primary adult procedures but extended abstinence to avoid graft failure.[75] [12] Following revision, some patients report pain from nocturnal erections; anecdotal patient experiences suggest strategies such as pseudoephedrine for vasoconstriction, over-the-counter analgesics, supportive garments, and avoiding stimulants to mitigate discomfort, with symptoms often resolving as healing progresses, though professional medical consultation is essential. Instruments and Devices

Clamp-Based Instruments

Clamp-based instruments in circumcision, such as the Gomco and Mogen clamps, facilitate foreskin excision by applying pressure to crush tissue, thereby achieving hemostasis prior to cutting. These devices are predominantly employed in neonatal procedures due to their simplicity and efficacy in minimizing blood loss compared to freehand techniques.[2][39] The Gomco clamp, introduced in the 1930s, comprises a circular bell placed over the glans penis to shield it and a locking clamp that compresses the foreskin against the bell's baseplate for approximately five minutes, necrosing the tissue and stemming bleeding. After compression, the foreskin is incised distal to the clamp, and the device is removed, leaving a raw edge that heals by secondary intention. This method is widely used in the United States, with reported complication rates as low as 0.2%, primarily minor issues like bleeding or infection.[76][58][77] The Mogen clamp, a shield-and-clamp variant, involves inserting a narrow metal shield between the foreskin and glans to protect the latter, followed by application of a hinged clamp to crush a segment of foreskin for 2-3 minutes before excision. It requires minimal training, procedure time of about 30 seconds for clamping, and is associated with reduced bleeding and fewer complications relative to the Gomco clamp, though it carries a rare risk of glans injury if misalignment occurs. No foreign material remains post-procedure, promoting faster healing.[62][39][78] Comparative studies indicate both clamps yield similar overall safety profiles, with low acute complication incidences of 1-2%, though the Mogen clamp may offer advantages in pain reduction when combined with dorsal penile nerve block anesthesia. Selection between them often depends on practitioner experience and institutional protocols, with evidence supporting their use in outpatient settings for infants under local anesthesia.[61][1]

In Situ and Ring Devices

The Plastibell device represents a primary example of an in situ ring mechanism for neonatal circumcision, consisting of a clear plastic bell-shaped ring with a circumferential groove designed to accommodate a ligature suture. During the procedure, adhesions between the foreskin and glans are lysed, a dorsal slit is made if necessary, and the appropriately sized Plastibell (typically 1.2 to 1.7 cm diameter for newborns) is inserted over the glans beneath the foreskin, which is then retracted proximally over the device. A non-absorbable suture is tied tightly in the groove to compress the foreskin against the ring, excess foreskin distal to the ligature is excised with scissors, and the handle of the device is removed, leaving the ring in situ to induce ischemic necrosis of the foreskin edge through sustained compression.[39][64][65] This in situ approach provides continuous hemostasis beyond the immediate operative period via the persistent ligature, while the plastic ring shields the glans from external trauma and demarcates the excision line for consistent cosmetic outcomes, with the entire device and necrotic foreskin typically sloughing spontaneously within 5 to 8 days as healing proceeds by secondary intention. Complications such as ring migration, incomplete detachment, or excessive bleeding occur in fewer than 5% of cases when proper sizing and technique are employed, though parental education on monitoring for ring retention beyond 10 days is essential to prevent phimosis or infection.[64][79][65] Ring devices like the Shang Ring, primarily utilized for adolescent and adult circumcision, employ a dual-concentric ring system of inner and outer silicone or plastic components to clamp the foreskin without sutures, facilitating necrosis through avascular compression. The inner ring (sized to the coronal sulcus) is positioned around the glans beneath the foreskin, the foreskin is everted over it, and the outer ring is snapped onto the inner to secure the tissue, followed by excision of the protruding foreskin distal to the outer ring using scissors; the assembled device remains in situ for 4 to 7 days to allow initial necrosis and epithelialization before outpatient removal of the outer ring, with the inner ring often retained briefly longer or detaching naturally.[80][81] This method reduces operative time to under 5 minutes and minimizes intraoperative bleeding compared to freehand techniques, though it requires a follow-up visit for device removal to avoid prolonged ischemia or adhesion formation.[81] Comparative studies indicate that in situ ring devices such as Plastibell and Shang Ring variants yield adverse event rates of 1-2% for moderate to severe complications, including hematoma or wound dehiscence, similar to clamp methods but with potentially lower acute bleeding risk due to the compressive mechanism; healing times extend 1-2 weeks longer than sutured procedures owing to reliance on secondary intention.[82][83] Variants like the Circumplast ring mirror Plastibell mechanics but incorporate modifications for reduced migration risk in infants, demonstrating minor complication rates under 3% in controlled evaluations.[84]

Restraint and Support Tools

In neonatal circumcision, restraint devices immobilize the infant to prevent movement that could result in imprecise incisions or injury to surrounding tissues. These tools generally comprise a rigid, padded board approximately 14-25 inches in length, equipped with adjustable fabric or foam straps to secure the arms and legs in extension while maintaining a supine position. This setup facilitates access to the penis and allows the procedure to proceed under controlled conditions, typically lasting 5-10 minutes.[85][86] Specific examples include the Circumstraint Newborn Immobilizer, which supports infants up to 8 pounds for circumcision and other minor interventions like catheterization, and the wee SECURE board, adaptable for neonates up to 20 pounds with four-point restraints for rapid positioning. Support straps, such as Argyle Baby Board Straps, provide gentle fixation without excessive pressure, though proper sizing and padding are critical to avoid skin abrasions or circulatory compromise. In adult or older child procedures, restraint is rarely required due to local or general anesthesia enabling cooperation; instead, supportive positioning via leg stirrups or padded tables suffices to maintain exposure.[85][87][88] Rigid boards carry minimal reported risks when sanitized appropriately, but inadequate cleaning has been linked to bacterial transmission, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) outbreaks in neonatal units. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends gentler alternatives, such as upper extremity swaddling over four-point fixation, to mitigate observable distress behaviors like crying or grimacing, as rigid immobilization may exacerbate stress despite concurrent analgesia. Empirical observations in procedural settings confirm that effective restraint enhances surgical accuracy, with complication rates from movement remaining low at under 1% in standardized protocols.[89][90][91]

Intraoperative and Postoperative Management

Surgical Execution Steps

The surgical execution of circumcision commences with patient positioning and preparation to ensure sterility and safety. In neonatal procedures, the infant is placed supine on a circumstraint board or similar device to immobilize the limbs, preventing inadvertent movement that could lead to injury. The genital area is cleansed with a mild antiseptic solution, such as povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine, to reduce infection risk.[8] [1] Anesthesia is then applied to mitigate pain, a critical step endorsed by medical guidelines for both efficacy and ethical reasons. For neonates, local options include dorsal penile nerve block or subcutaneous ring block using 1% lidocaine without epinephrine (up to 0.1 mL/kg), or topical agents like 4% lidocaine-prilocaine cream applied 30-60 minutes prior; oral sucrose may adjunctively soothe via non-nutritive sucking. Adults typically receive infiltrated local anesthesia (e.g., 1-2% lidocaine, up to 10 mL) or general anesthesia for extensive cases, with procedures lasting 20-30 minutes compared to 5-10 minutes in neonates.[1] [8] Adhesions between the inner foreskin (preputial epithelium) and glans are separated using a sterile probe or hemostat to fully expose the glans without trauma, confirming normal anatomy and avoiding phimosis-related complications. The foreskin is retracted and grasped at the corona with toothed forceps for traction.[1] [2] Excision follows, tailored to the selected technique but universally involving precise removal of the prepuce distal to the corona. In clamp or shield methods, the device is positioned over the glans to protect it, and redundant foreskin is crushed and incised with scissors or scalpel, excising approximately 0.5-1 cm beyond the planned margin to account for contraction. Sleeve resection in open or adult procedures entails two parallel circumferential incisions (one proximal to the corona, one at the corona's base), removal of the intervening skin strip, and approximation of mucosal edges. Care is taken to preserve the frenulum if indicated and to avoid excessive tension or urethral injury.[2] [1] Hemostasis is secured immediately post-excision, typically by applying pressure with the clamp for 3-5 minutes, followed by electrocautery, absorbable ligatures (e.g., 4-0 chromic gut), or silver nitrate for minor bleeders; complication rates for bleeding are under 1% with proper technique. In device-assisted neonatal cases, such as Plastibell, a ligature ties the foreskin base, allowing necrotic sloughing over 5-8 days without sutures.[1] [8] Closure, if required, involves interrupted or running absorbable sutures (e.g., 5-0 chromic) for mucosal approximation in open methods, ensuring a tension-free edge-to-edge fit; many neonatal procedures omit sutures due to rapid healing. The site is inspected for completeness, applied with petroleum jelly or antibiotic ointment, and lightly dressed with gauze to prevent adhesion. Total operative time for standard neonatal circumcision is 5-10 minutes, with adult variants extending due to vascularity and suturing needs.[1] [8]

Wound Care and Recovery Protocols

Post-circumcision wound care for neonates and infants emphasizes gentle cleaning and lubrication to promote healing and prevent adhesion of the diaper to the site. The penis should be washed with plain warm water after each diaper change, patted dry, and coated with petroleum jelly or bacitracin ointment to avoid sticking and reduce irritation; alcohol, hydrogen peroxide, or soap should be avoided as they may delay healing or cause irritation.[92][93][49] Initial bandages are typically removed within 24 hours, after which the area is left exposed to air, with ointment applied several times daily. Swelling is normal after circumcision and typically peaks in the first few days before reducing over 1-2 weeks; minor bruising may also occur. For infants, warm baths and loose diapers are recommended to minimize irritation. Consult a doctor if swelling worsens or persists.[92][94][95][96] Pain management in infants involves acetaminophen or ibuprofen dosed by weight as needed, particularly in the first few days when discomfort from swelling peaks. Sponge baths are recommended for 3-7 days to minimize water exposure until initial healing progresses. Caregivers must monitor for infection indicators such as excessive redness, pus, foul odor, or fever above 100.4°F (38°C), which warrant immediate medical evaluation; proper hygiene reduces complication rates, including bleeding or wound dehiscence, which occur in under 1% of cases with standard protocols.[97][98][1] For adults, recovery protocols focus on protecting the wound from friction and infection during the initial 48 hours, when swelling and bruising are most pronounced, followed by gradual resumption of activities. Swelling is normal, peaks in the first few days, and typically reduces over 1-2 weeks. To reduce swelling, apply wrapped ice packs or cold compresses for 10-20 minutes every 1-2 hours in the first 24-48 hours; wear supportive underwear to keep the penis elevated/upright; avoid strenuous activity; and keep the area clean with petroleum jelly or ointment. Bandages should remain dry initially, with showers permitted after 48 hours using mild soap and water; Consult a doctor if swelling worsens or persists.[99][8][100][96] Pain is generally mild and managed with over-the-counter analgesics or prescribed medications, though erections may cause discomfort in the first week.[101] In circumcision revision procedures, pain from nocturnal erections can be particularly challenging during early recovery. User experiences shared in online forums like Reddit suggest measures such as taking pseudoephedrine (e.g., Sudafed) before bed to suppress erections through vasoconstriction, using over-the-counter pain relievers like ibuprofen or acetaminophen, wearing tight underwear to limit penile movement and reduce tension on healing tissue, and avoiding stimulants like caffeine that might promote erections. Some report that masturbation or sexual activity can relieve tension if approved by a doctor, with pain often subsiding as healing progresses over a few weeks. These are anecdotal reports, not evidence-based medical recommendations, and individuals should consult their surgeon or physician for personalized guidance due to potential risks of self-medication. Healing typically completes in 8-10 days for the wound to close, but full keratinization of the glans and resumption of sexual activity require 4-6 weeks to avoid dehiscence or scarring. Patients should abstain from strenuous activity, masturbation, or intercourse during this period, and monitor for signs of complications like persistent bleeding, hematoma, or infection, which affect fewer than 5% with adherence to guidelines; secondary intention healing occurs naturally for minor separations without intervention.[1][102][1] Follow-up visits at 1-2 weeks assess progress, with evidence indicating that meticulous care lowers reoperation needs to around 3%.[103][104] During recovery from circumcision, some medical sources recommend limiting milk and dairy products (e.g., cheese) to prevent constipation, as straining during bowel movements can cause pain or complications in the healing area. Constipation prevention is prioritized through high-fiber foods, hydration, and avoiding low-fiber items. Authoritative sources like Mayo Clinic and Healthline do not specifically mention dairy restrictions, focusing instead on hygiene, pain management, and general care.[105][106][8][107]

Risks, Complications, and Outcomes

Acute Complications and Incidence Rates

Acute complications of circumcision encompass adverse events occurring during the procedure or within the immediate postoperative period, typically defined as within 30 days, including bleeding, infection, penile trauma, and anesthesia-related issues. Bleeding is the most frequently reported acute complication, often arising from inadequate hemostasis or vascular anomalies, with incidence varying by patient age and surgical technique.[108] Infections, primarily superficial wound infections, result from bacterial contamination and are managed with antibiotics, though systemic sepsis is rare in sterile medical settings.[109] Other acute issues include penile edema, excessive or insufficient foreskin removal leading to asymmetry or redundancy, and inadvertent glans or urethral injury from clamping devices.[69] In neonatal circumcision performed in hospital settings by trained providers, overall acute complication rates range from 0.2% to 0.6%, with bleeding accounting for the majority at approximately 0.1% to 0.2%.[109] [69] A systematic review of studies reported median complication frequencies of 2% to 6% for medical provider-performed procedures in infants, rising to 6% (range 2-14%) in older children due to increased vascularity and patient movement.[10] Serious adverse events, such as major penile injury or necrosis, occur at rates below 0.01% in U.S. medical contexts, though underreporting in outpatient settings may underestimate totals.[69] For adult circumcision, acute complication rates are higher, typically 1% to 9%, attributed to greater tissue vascularity, patient cooperation challenges, and comorbidities like diabetes or coagulopathies.[69] A meta-analysis found an overall risk of 3.8% across ages, with therapeutic (medically indicated) procedures showing elevated rates compared to elective ones.[110] In community-based primary care analyses, bleeding occurred in 0.08% to 0.18%, penile injury in 0.04%, and infection in 0.06%.[111]

Long-Term Effects and Mitigation

Meatal stenosis, a narrowing of the urethral opening, represents one of the most commonly reported long-term complications following circumcision, with incidence rates estimated at 8-10% globally among circumcised males, particularly when the procedure is performed in the first year of life.[115] Screening studies have detected asymptomatic cases in up to 17.9% of circumcised boys, with risks elevated twofold if circumcision occurs before age one compared to later.[116] [117] This condition typically manifests 3-5 years post-procedure and may require meatotomy for correction if symptomatic, though many cases remain undetected without routine follow-up.[118] Studies on sexual function and sensitivity yield mixed but predominantly neutral to positive findings in high-quality reviews; systematic analyses of randomized trials and cohort studies indicate that circumcision does not adversely affect penile sensitivity, erectile function, orgasm ease, or overall sexual satisfaction, with some reports of enhanced satisfaction post-procedure.[119] [120] [121] Earlier observational data suggesting reduced sensitivity or erectile issues have been contradicted by prospective studies controlling for confounders, which show no significant differences or improvements in sexual drive and performance.[122] [123] However, subjective long-term complaints such as skin color mismatch or altered masturbation habits occur in up to 90% of some cohorts, though these rarely impair function.[124] In low and tight circumcisions, which remove significant inner foreskin and shaft skin resulting in minimal loose skin and reduced gliding action, masturbation techniques often shift from foreskin rolling or gliding to direct glans and shaft stimulation, frequently requiring lubricant due to increased friction and lack of natural skin movement. Anecdotal reports vary: some men experience decreased pleasure, increased difficulty, or need adaptations such as different grips, more vigorous stimulation, or aids, while others report no major issues or satisfaction with new methods. Scientific studies on circumcision show mixed effects on masturbatory pleasure and difficulty but do not specifically isolate low and tight styles.[125] Other long-term penile issues include skin bridges, adhesions, and concealed penis, with meta-analyses reporting higher cumulative complication rates (up to 8.3% in neonates) when procedures occur at older ages or without meticulous technique.[12] [113] Claims of psychological trauma, such as links to autism spectrum disorder via stress hormone accumulation, lack empirical support from controlled studies examining glucocorticoid levels.[126] Mitigation of meatal stenosis and similar issues emphasizes perioperative steroid application to reduce inflammation, preservation of penile fascia during surgery to maintain meatal caliber, and routine postoperative monitoring with emollient dressings to prevent adhesions.[104] [72] Trained providers using standardized techniques, such as avoiding excessive frenular artery ligation, lower stenosis rates to 2-5%, while long-term follow-up protocols—including annual exams until toilet-trained—enable early detection and intervention like dilation or minor revision.[127] [109] For sexual function concerns, preoperative counseling on expected sensory adaptations and device-assisted methods in adults may enhance outcomes, though evidence supports overall low risk with proper execution.[112]

Historical Development

Origins and Early Practices

The earliest archaeological evidence of circumcision dates to ancient Egypt during the Sixth Dynasty, circa 2400 BCE, as depicted in a bas-relief in the tomb of Ankhmahor at Saqqara, showing the procedure performed on adolescents using a flint knife while the subjects are restrained.[128] This representation illustrates circumcision as a ritual initiation, likely marking transition to adulthood or priestly status, with wall reliefs and hieroglyphs indicating it was customary among elites and nobility several millennia prior.[129] Egyptian mummies from around 1300 BCE confirm the practice through physical evidence of removed foreskins, suggesting it was widespread by the New Kingdom period.[129] Greek historian Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BCE, attributed the origins of circumcision to the Egyptians, describing it as a hygienic measure to promote cleanliness, performed on boys before puberty rather than in infancy. He noted its adoption by neighboring groups such as the Colchians, Ethiopians, and Phoenicians, positioning Egypt as the primary ancient innovator, though some evidence points to independent origins in sub-equatorial African ethnic groups predating written records.[130] In Egyptian contexts, the procedure involved rudimentary tools like obsidian blades and early anesthetics derived from calcium carbonate mixtures as far back as 2500 BCE, reflecting an evolution from punitive genital mutilations on war captives to structured rites.[131] Circumcision spread to Semitic peoples, with biblical accounts in Genesis 17 portraying it as a covenantal sign instituted by Abraham around the estimated 2nd millennium BCE, mandating the removal of the foreskin on the eighth day for male offspring as an enduring religious obligation.[23] This Jewish practice, distinct from Egyptian adolescent rites, emphasized infant timing and theological permanence, influencing later Abrahamic traditions including Islam, where it is sunnah (recommended) based on prophetic example rather than direct Quranic mandate.[23] Archaeological and textual gaps preclude definitive tracing of transmission, but the procedure's persistence across these cultures underscores its roles in hygiene, tribal identity, and ritual purity, unlinked to modern medical rationales at inception.[130]

Modern Medical Adoption and Evolution

The medical adoption of circumcision as a routine procedure began in the late 19th century in English-speaking countries, particularly the United States and United Kingdom, where it transitioned from a primarily religious or cultural rite to a prophylactic intervention aimed at preventing conditions such as phimosis, infections, and moral failings like masturbation.[33] In 1870, Dr. Lewis Sayre, a New York orthopedist, performed the first documented medical circumcision on a child with leg paralysis, attributing the condition to penile adhesions and advocating the procedure as a cure for various neurological and genital ailments based on observed improvements post-surgery.[132] This marked a shift toward viewing circumcision through a biomedical lens, with proponents like Sayre and later Jonathan Hutchinson promoting it to reduce risks of diseases such as syphilis and urinary tract issues, though initial rationales often lacked rigorous empirical support and reflected Victorian-era concerns over hygiene and self-abuse.[133] By the early 20th century, circumcision gained traction in American hospitals as a newborn procedure, with rates rising to approximately 55% by 1938 and peaking at around 83% in the 1960s, driven by institutional endorsements and perceived benefits in preventing penile cancer, balanitis, and other infections.[134] [135] Evolution in surgical techniques paralleled this adoption, moving from freehand excision with a scalpel—which risked uneven cuts and hemorrhage—to specialized devices emphasizing hemostasis and precision. The Gomco clamp, invented in 1935 by Dr. Henry Goodwin, compressed the foreskin to control bleeding before excision, reducing operative time and complications in infants.[2] Subsequent innovations included the Mogen clamp in the 1940s for shield-guided resection and the Plastibell device in the 1950s, which used a plastic ring left in place to facilitate natural sloughing of the foreskin, further standardizing neonatal procedures under local anesthesia.[136] These advancements prioritized asepsis, glans protection, and minimal tissue trauma, with modern protocols incorporating dorsal penile nerve blocks to mitigate pain, reflecting a causal understanding of nociception and improved outcomes data.[2] By the late 20th century, while prevalence began declining—to 58.3% by 2010 amid debates over necessity—evidence from randomized trials in Africa demonstrated reduced HIV acquisition (by 50-60%), prompting renewed policy discussions by bodies like the WHO for voluntary medical male circumcision in high-prevalence regions, though adoption remained uneven globally due to cultural and evidentiary variances.[137] [138]

Controversies and Debates

Ethical Perspectives on Consent and Autonomy

The ethical debate on consent and autonomy in neonatal circumcision centers on the procedure's performance on infants incapable of providing informed consent, raising questions about violations of bodily integrity and the right to self-determination over irreversible modifications to healthy tissue.[139] Proponents of infant circumcision, including the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) in its 2012 policy statement, argue that parents hold proxy decision-making authority, justified by potential preventive health benefits such as reduced urinary tract infections and sexually transmitted diseases, which they contend outweigh ethical concerns when weighed against the child's best interests.[3] However, critics contend that proxy consent is ethically invalid for non-therapeutic interventions lacking immediate medical necessity, as it substitutes parental preferences—often cultural or religious—for the child's autonomous right to an intact body, paralleling prohibitions on other non-consensual genital alterations.[140][6] From a first-principles ethical framework emphasizing autonomy as a core principle in bioethics, infant circumcision contravenes the requirement for voluntary, informed agreement to procedures altering functional anatomy, as the foreskin serves protective, sensory, and immunological roles without posing inherent harm if left intact.[141] Ethicists like J. Steven Svoboda argue that such acts infringe on the infant's fundamental human right to bodily integrity, comparable to female genital cutting, which is universally condemned despite analogous cultural rationales; they assert that deferring the decision until adulthood respects the principle of non-maleficence by avoiding unnecessary risks like infection (occurring in up to 10% of cases per some reviews) or surgical complications.[139][140] In contrast, defenders invoke parental rights under frameworks like the "best interests" standard, positing that evolutionary and epidemiological data on benefits—such as a 60% reduction in HIV acquisition in heterosexual men per randomized trials—permit anticipatory consent, though these benefits are contested as overstated for low-risk populations in non-endemic areas.[50][6] Legal-ethical analyses further highlight tensions, noting that while U.S. courts generally uphold parental consent for circumcision absent abuse, international bodies like the Royal Dutch Medical Association (2010) deem routine neonatal circumcision a violation of children's rights under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, prioritizing autonomy over tradition.[52] This perspective aligns with bioethical reviews arguing that true proxy consent requires demonstrable net benefit without alternatives, a threshold unmet by circumcision's modest, proxy-derived advantages versus certain sensory losses and psychological impacts reported in adult surveys.[142] Sources opposing routine practice, often from European and Australian ethicists, emphasize empirical scrutiny over institutional biases favoring tradition, critiquing U.S.-centric policies like the AAP's for underweighting autonomy amid declining procedure rates (from 80% in 1979 to 58% by 2010).[143] Ultimately, the autonomy argument posits that ethical practice demands postponement until the individual can consent, preserving causal agency over one's body absent compelling therapeutic imperatives.[141][140]

Medical and Public Health Disputes

Proponents of routine male circumcision argue that empirical evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses demonstrates net public health benefits, including a 50-60% reduction in heterosexual HIV acquisition among men in high-prevalence settings, as evidenced by three RCTs conducted in South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda between 2005 and 2007.[34] [35] These trials prompted World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations in 2007 for voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) programs in 15 priority sub-Saharan African countries with HIV prevalence exceeding 15% among adolescent girls and young women, estimating potential prevention of 3.4 million HIV infections and 1.3 million deaths by 2025 if scaled appropriately.[144] However, critics contend that these findings have limited applicability to low-prevalence populations like those in the United States or Europe, where HIV transmission dynamics differ (predominantly via men who have sex with men rather than heterosexual contact), and observational studies yield inconsistent results, potentially overestimating benefits due to confounding factors such as behavioral differences.[145] [146] Additional claimed benefits include a 10-fold reduction in urinary tract infections (UTIs) during the first year of life, with meta-analyses estimating an absolute risk reduction from 1% in uncircumcised infants to 0.1% in circumcised ones, potentially averting rare long-term renal damage.[147] Circumcision is also associated with lower risks of penile cancer (odds ratio 0.33 in meta-analysis of case-control studies) and human papillomavirus (HPV)-related outcomes, though penile cancer remains exceedingly rare (1 in 100,000 annually in developed nations), necessitating over 900 circumcisions to prevent one case when performed neonatally.[17] [148] Opponents highlight that these absolute risk reductions are marginal in hygienic contexts with access to modern sanitation and treatments like antibiotics for UTIs or HPV vaccination for cancer prevention, arguing that non-surgical interventions suffice without procedural risks.[149] Complication rates from meta-analyses range from 1-3% for non-therapeutic neonatal procedures, predominantly minor issues like bleeding or infection, compared to 7% for therapeutic circumcisions in older males, with severe outcomes (e.g., penile amputation) under 0.1%.[150] [108] Risk-benefit analyses estimate benefits outweighing risks by ratios of 100:1 to 200:1 when aggregating lifetime protections against infections and cancers.[151] Disputes arise over underreporting in provider surveys and long-term effects like keratinization potentially reducing sensitivity, though prospective data show no consistent impact on sexual function.[152] Major medical organizations reflect these tensions: the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) 2012 policy (reaffirmed without major revision as of 2022) states preventive benefits outweigh risks but stops short of universal recommendation, deferring to parental choice amid cultural and ethical variances.[3] The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) endorses counseling on benefits for HIV/STI reduction, particularly in high-risk groups, but acknowledges insufficient evidence for routine infant circumcision in the general population.[138] In contrast, bodies like the Royal Australasian College of Physicians emphasize rarity of diseases prevented and advocate against routine practice outside therapeutic need, citing high numbers needed to treat (e.g., 111 for UTIs, 900+ for penile cancer). Public health modeling supports VMMC scale-up in epidemic epicenters for cost-effective HIV control (cost per infection averted: $200-600), but in low-incidence settings, critics argue resources are better allocated to education, condoms, and PrEP, given circumcision's incomplete protection (40-50% residual risk) and reliance on high coverage rates unachievable without compulsion.[153] [154]
