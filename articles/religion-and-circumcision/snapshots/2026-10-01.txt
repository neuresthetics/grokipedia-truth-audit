# Religion and circumcision

Religion and circumcision denotes the ritual excision of the male foreskin as a divinely ordained or prophetically endorsed act in Abrahamic faiths, symbolizing covenantal fidelity, ritual purity, and initiation into the community of believers, with roots traceable to ancient Semitic practices predating formalized Judaism. [1] In Judaism, brit milah constitutes an immutable commandment from the Torah (Genesis 17:10-14), executed precisely on the eighth postnatal day by a mohel trained in surgical precision and halakhic observance, affirming the eternal pact between God and Abraham's descendants. [2] Islam classifies male khitan as a sunnah mu'akkadah—emphatically recommended—rooted in hadith traditions enumerating it among the innate acts of fitrah (natural disposition), typically performed in infancy or childhood with near-universal adherence among the global Muslim population exceeding 1.8 billion. [3] [4] The rite's antiquity extends to pre-Abrahamic Egyptians circa 3000 BCE, where it intertwined hygienic, martial, and cultic motifs, later influencing sporadic adoption in Ethiopian Orthodox and certain African Christian sects alongside animist customs. [5] [6] Contemporary tensions arise from secular challenges invoking infant consent and bodily integrity against religious imperatives, amplified by empirical inquiries into prophylactic benefits versus procedural risks, though source biases in academic and media analyses often skew toward cultural relativism over causal health outcomes. [1] [7]

## Historical Origins

### Ancient Egypt

Male circumcision was practiced in ancient Egypt from at least the Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BCE), as evidenced by tomb reliefs depicting the procedure on young males, often in contexts suggesting ritual significance for elite or priestly classes. [8] The earliest known representations appear in the Sixth Dynasty mastaba of Ankhmahor at Saqqara (c. 2350 BCE), showing attendants performing the cut on restrained boys using knives, interpreted as a consecration or initiatory rite marking entry into adulthood or religious service. [9] Physical evidence from mummified remains, including those of nobles and royalty like from the pyramid complex of Djedkare (Fifth Dynasty, c. 2400 BCE), confirms the procedure's execution, with healed circumcision scars visible on pre-pubescent individuals. [10]

Greek historian Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BCE, described Egyptian circumcision as a practice for cleanliness and purity, distinct from aesthetic motives, and noted it was performed by priests who maintained bodily purity through regular shaving; he attributed its origins to Egypt, influencing Ethiopians and Colchians. [11] In religious contexts, the rite aligned with Egyptian concepts of ma'at (order and purity), serving as a form of purification for temple service or royal initiation, though not universally mandated across all social strata. [12] Scholarly analyses propose it functioned as sympathetic magic for fertility or virility, tied to deities like Min, but primary evidence emphasizes hygienic-ritual purification over explicit sacrificial theology. [13] No archaeological or textual records indicate female circumcision in ancient Egypt, distinguishing it as a male-specific custom. [14]

### Near Eastern and Pre-Abrahamic Cultures

Circumcision practices in pre-Abrahamic Near Eastern cultures, predating the biblical covenant with Abraham (circa 2000–1800 BCE), show limited archaeological and textual evidence, primarily confined to certain Levantine and Syrian Semitic groups rather than widespread across the region. Unlike in ancient Egypt, where depictions date to the Sixth Dynasty (circa 2400 BCE), routine male circumcision was not practiced in Mesopotamia, as confirmed by the absence of osteological or cuneiform references to the procedure in Sumerian, Akkadian, or Babylonian records. Mesopotamian medical texts, such as those from the Old Babylonian period (circa 1800–1600 BCE), describe surgical interventions like abscess drainage and wound care but lack any mention of penile foreskin removal as a standard or ritual act. [15] [16]

In northern Syria, early evidence emerges from the third millennium BCE, with artifacts suggesting the practice may have been introduced by migratory groups, possibly Semitic traders or pastoralists interacting with Egyptian influences via trade routes. This aligns with textual hints in Eblaite archives (circa 2500–2300 BCE), where administrative records imply ritual body modifications among elites, though direct depictions of circumcision remain absent. Further south in the Levant, Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (14th–12th centuries BCE) contain obscure references to genital mutilation in mythological contexts, potentially linked to rites of passage or purification for warriors, as interpreted in comparative studies of Semitic incantations; however, these are not explicitly tied to religious covenants and may represent symbolic rather than literal practices. [17] [18] [19]

Among pre-Abrahamic Canaanite and Phoenician-related peoples, classical sources like Herodotus (5th century BCE) report that Phoenicians circumcised for hygienic or initiatory reasons, attributing the custom to Egyptian origins adopted during the Late Bronze Age (circa 1550–1200 BCE). Scholarly analyses of Phoenician inscriptions and burial practices yield no confirmatory artifacts, but comparative ethnography with later Semitic groups suggests it served as a marker of tribal identity or puberty rite rather than a core religious sacrament. Hittite records from Anatolia (circa 1600–1200 BCE) mention purification rituals (mala) for children of both sexes but provide no evidence of genital cutting, emphasizing offerings over bodily alteration. Overall, these practices appear sporadic and culturally embedded, lacking the theological framing seen in later Abrahamic traditions, with potential roles in status signaling or hygiene amid arid environments rather than divine mandate. [20] [21]

## Abrahamic Religions

### Judaism

In Judaism, male circumcision, known as brit milah or covenant of circumcision, constitutes a foundational religious commandment signifying the eternal covenant between God and the Jewish people, as established with the patriarch Abraham. The Torah mandates this rite in Genesis 17:10-14, where God instructs Abraham: "This is My covenant, which you shall keep, between Me and you and your descendants after you: Every male child among you shall be circumcised," specifying the removal of the foreskin as the physical sign of inclusion in the covenant promising numerous descendants and the land of Canaan. [22] [23] Leviticus 12:3 further prescribes the timing: "On the eighth day, the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised." Failure to perform circumcision results in spiritual excision (karet) from the people, underscoring its obligatory nature. [24]

The procedure, conducted by a mohel—a ritually qualified individual trained in both surgical technique and Jewish law—occurs during a ceremonial gathering on the eighth day of life, barring health contraindications. It comprises three stages: milah, excision of the foreskin; pri'ah, tearing away the underlying mucosal membrane to expose the glans; and metzitzah, suction to draw blood from the wound to promote healing and prevent infection, historically performed orally (metzitzah b'peh). [25] The ceremony includes blessings, naming the child, and a festive meal, reinforcing communal bonds. While most contemporary mohelim employ sterile tubes or gauze for metzitzah to mitigate risks, direct oral suction persists in some ultra-Orthodox communities despite documented health concerns. [26]

Circumcision observance remains nearly universal among Jewish males worldwide, with rates approaching 100% in Israel across secular and religious families, reflecting its enduring role as an identity marker. [27] In the United States and elsewhere, Jewish circumcision rates similarly exceed 90%, often integrated into hospital settings while preserving ritual elements. [28] Empirical data indicate low overall complication rates for neonatal circumcision, comparable to other routine procedures, though metzitzah b'peh has been causally linked to neonatal herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) transmission. In New York City from 2000 to 2011, health authorities documented 11 cases of infant HSV-1 following ritual circumcision with oral suction, resulting in two deaths and two cases of neurological damage; further incidents occurred post-2011, prompting regulatory efforts balanced against religious autonomy. [29] [30] These risks stem from potential mohel carriage of HSV-1, an asymptomatic adult virus transmissible to infants whose immature immunity heightens severity. [31]

### Islam

In Islam, male circumcision, known as khitan, is regarded as part of the fitrah—the innate human disposition—based on a hadith narrated by Abu Hurayra in which the Prophet Muhammad stated, "Five acts are part of the fitrah: circumcision, shaving the pubic hair, trimming the mustache, clipping the nails, and plucking the armpit hair." [32] The practice is not explicitly commanded in the Quran but derives from the sunnah, as the Prophet Muhammad followed and endorsed the custom of his forefather Abraham, who was circumcised at age 80 according to some traditions. [4] It symbolizes ritual purity and adherence to prophetic tradition, facilitating cleanliness required for acts of worship such as prayer. [33]

The juristic ruling on male circumcision varies across Islamic schools of thought. The Shafi'i and Hanbali madhabs consider it obligatory (wajib), while the Hanafi and Maliki views classify it as sunnah mu'akkadah (emphasized recommended), though some scholars argue it becomes obligatory if necessary for leading prayer due to purity requirements. [34] [3] Traditions report that the Prophet Muhammad was either born circumcised or underwent the procedure, reinforcing its status as a prophetic practice without a direct Quranic mandate. [35] Female circumcision, by contrast, is not part of the male khitan obligation and receives lesser emphasis, often viewed as optional or cultural rather than core sunnah. [34]

Circumcision is typically performed on boys usually on the 7th day after birth, often combined with the aqiqah celebration, or alternatively in early childhood or at puberty, with flexibility varying by region and culture; no fixed timing is prescribed in Islamic texts, prioritizing before the onset of sexual maturity to align with prophetic recommendations for early implementation. [36] [37] The procedure involves surgical removal of the foreskin, typically conducted by a doctor, traditional practitioner (khatib), or occasionally a family member, with accompanying prayers and celebrations; simpler than the Jewish ritual, it lacks fixed timing and specialized ceremonial training. It is often accompanied by communal feasts in cultures like those in Turkey, Indonesia, or North Africa, marking the boy's entry into religious obligations. [38] Globally, prevalence among Muslim males exceeds 99% in majority-Muslim countries, contributing to the overall rate of about 38% worldwide, driven primarily by religious adherence rather than medical factors. [7] [39]

### Christianity

In Christianity, physical circumcision is not required for salvation or church membership, as articulated in the New Testament. The Apostle Paul argued that reliance on circumcision nullifies the benefits of faith in Christ, emphasizing instead a spiritual "circumcision of the heart" by the Holy Spirit (Romans 2:25-29; Galatians 5:2-6). [40] This view resolved early disputes, culminating in the Jerusalem Council around 48-50 AD, where apostles and elders decreed that Gentile converts need not undergo circumcision to observe the Mosaic Law, basing the decision on God's acceptance of Gentiles through faith without such rituals (Acts 15:1-29). [41]

Early Church Fathers reinforced this stance, rejecting physical circumcision as obsolete under the New Covenant. For instance, John Chrysostom warned that accepting circumcision renders Christ's work ineffective, aligning with Pauline theology. [42] Justin Martyr similarly critiqued ongoing observance of circumcision alongside Sabbath-keeping as incompatible with Christian liberty. [43] Consequently, mainstream Christian denominations—Catholic, Protestant, and Eastern Orthodox—do not mandate or perform circumcision as a religious rite, viewing it as a cultural or medical practice where prevalent, such as in parts of the United States. [44]

Exceptions persist among certain Oriental Orthodox traditions influenced by regional customs. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church observes a Feast of the Circumcision on January 6 (or equivalent in its calendar), commemorating Jesus' circumcision, and practices male infant circumcision widely as a traditional rite, though not dogmatically tied to salvation. [45] Similarly, Coptic Orthodox Christians in Egypt often circumcise male infants, rooted in pre-Christian Egyptian heritage rather than doctrinal necessity, with official church positions disclaiming it as a required practice. [46] Eritrean Orthodox followers maintain comparable customs. These practices reflect syncretism with local traditions rather than universal Christian theology, as no denomination enforces circumcision for soteriological reasons. [47]

### Samaritanism, Druze, and Other Abrahamic Faiths

In Samaritanism, male circumcision, known as berit milla, serves as the primary rite initiating boys into the Abrahamic covenant, performed strictly on the eighth day after birth unless the infant's life is endangered. [48] This practice adheres closely to the Pentateuchal command in Genesis 17:12, emphasizing its obligatory nature without the Jewish peri'ah (tearing of the membrane under the foreskin) or subsequent rituals like sucking the blood. Samaritans regard uncircumcision as a grave violation, historically viewing failure to circumcise by the eighth day's end as potentially warranting severe consequences, though modern observance prioritizes health. [49] The ceremony involves communal participation on Mount Gerizim when feasible, reinforcing ethnic and religious identity amid a small population of approximately 800 individuals as of 2023. [48]

The Druze faith, an esoteric Abrahamic offshoot emerging from 11th-century Ismaili Shi'ism, does not mandate circumcision as a religious precept, distinguishing it from Judaism and Islam. [50] Despite this, male circumcision remains prevalent among Druze communities as a cultural tradition, often conducted in a manner akin to Islamic khitan to maintain outward conformity amid historical persecution and the doctrine of taqiyya (concealment of faith). Performed typically in infancy or childhood without scriptural basis in Druze texts like the Epistles of Wisdom, it functions more as a social norm than a theological imperative, with rates approaching universality in regions like Lebanon and Israel due to regional influences. [50]

Among other Abrahamic faiths excluding Judaism, Islam, and mainstream Christianity, circumcision lacks uniform religious endorsement. The Bahá'í Faith, founded in 1863, views it as a non-obligatory personal or cultural choice, aligning with its emphasis on progressive revelation over ancient Mosaic laws. Mandaeism, an ancient Gnostic tradition, rejects circumcision entirely, prioritizing ritual immersion (masbuta) for purification and deeming genital alteration incompatible with the soul's pristine creation. Smaller groups like the Samaritans' close kin or Druze variants show no divergent mandates, with practices generally influenced by host cultures rather than core doctrine. [51]

## Dharmic and Indian Religions

### Hinduism

Hinduism lacks any scriptural mandate for male circumcision, with core texts such as the Vedas, Upanishads, and Dharma Shastras containing no references to the procedure as a religious rite or requirement. [52] Unlike Abrahamic traditions, Hindu rituals emphasize bodily integrity and purity through practices like shuddhi (purification) without surgical alteration of genitalia, viewing the natural form as divinely ordained and interference as potentially disruptive to prana (vital energy) flow. [53] Ancient surgical texts like the Sushruta Samhita describe procedures for medical necessities, such as treating phimosis or injuries, but frame genital surgeries in contexts of restoring function rather than ritual excision, and do not endorse routine circumcision. [54]

Prevalence of male circumcision among Hindus remains extremely low, primarily limited to non-religious motivations like hygiene, medical advice, or Western influences, rather than doctrinal adherence. In India, where Hindus constitute approximately 80% of the population, the practice is overwhelmingly associated with Muslim communities (about 14% of the populace), leading to cultural aversion among Hindus due to historical connotations, including identification markers during events like the 1947 Partition violence. Studies in regions like Mysore indicate high theoretical acceptability (over 90%) among Hindu mothers for voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) as an HIV prevention strategy when informed of benefits, yet actual uptake remains negligible without religious impetus, with national estimates placing non-Muslim circumcision rates below 1-2%. [55] [56]

Hindu philosophical perspectives, rooted in concepts like ahimsa (non-violence) and reverence for the body as a temple (deha-devalaya), further discourage non-therapeutic modifications, equating them to unnecessary harm without soteriological value. Reformist or diaspora Hindus occasionally adopt circumcision for assimilation or health reasons, but this deviates from orthodox traditions, which prioritize intactness to align with yogic and tantric practices emphasizing sensory wholeness. No major Hindu sect or guru has historically promoted it as a sacrament, distinguishing Hinduism from circumcision-centric faiths. [57] [58]

### Buddhism and Sikhism

Buddhism does not mandate or incorporate male circumcision as a religious practice or rite of passage. Originating in the 5th century BCE in ancient India, where the procedure held no cultural or ritual significance among the prevailing Hindu and subsequent Buddhist communities, the tradition emphasizes ethical precepts, meditation, and the pursuit of enlightenment through mental discipline rather than physical alterations to the body. [59] The core doctrine of ahimsa (non-violence or non-harm) prioritizes avoiding unnecessary injury, which some interpretations extend to critiquing non-therapeutic circumcision; for instance, efforts to promote voluntary medical male circumcision in Thailand during the 2000s encountered resistance on grounds of conflicting with indigenous Buddhist ethical norms against bodily harm without compelling medical justification. [60] In Buddhist-majority regions like Southeast Asia and East Asia, circumcision rates remain low outside Muslim minorities, reflecting its absence from canonical texts such as the Pali Canon or Mahayana sutras. [61]

Sikhism explicitly prohibits circumcision, viewing it as a violation of the natural human form created by Waheguru (God) and an adherence to superfluous rituals rejected by the faith's founders. Established in the late 15th century CE in Punjab by Guru Nanak and codified through the ten Gurus' teachings compiled in the Guru Granth Sahib (finalized in 1708 CE), Sikhism mandates preserving the body's integrity, with scriptural injunctions against cutting or removing flesh—such as in the verse "By cutting the body parts, the filth of the mind is not removed" (Guru Granth Sahib, p. 93)—applied to genital modifications. [62] This stance aligns with broader Sikh rejections of practices like those in Hinduism or Islam deemed ritualistic distortions, emphasizing equality, uncut hair (kesh), and inner purity over external alterations. [63] While routine neonatal circumcision for religious or hygienic reasons is forbidden as a kurahit (major taboo), exceptions may apply for genuine medical necessity, such as phimosis requiring surgical intervention to prevent health complications, provided it avoids cultural conformity. [61] In practice, Sikh communities in India and the diaspora maintain low circumcision prevalence, consistent with these doctrinal prohibitions. [62]

## Traditional and Indigenous Religions

### African Traditions

In numerous indigenous African ethnic groups, particularly in sub-Saharan regions, male circumcision functions as a pivotal rite of passage within traditional belief systems, symbolizing the transition from boyhood to manhood and reinforcing communal bonds through rituals that invoke ancestral spirits. These practices, predating Abrahamic influences, emphasize physical endurance, moral instruction, and spiritual purification, often conducted by non-medically trained practitioners using rudimentary tools in secluded bush camps. Among groups such as the Xhosa in South Africa, the ulwaluko ceremony entails circumcision followed by weeks of isolation, dietary restrictions, and teachings on masculinity, with participants applying herbal remedies like mathunga for hemostasis and healing; failure to undergo it results in social ostracism, underscoring its role in cultural identity. [64] [65]

Prevalence of traditional male circumcision varies regionally, with rates of 25-90% among circumcised males in eastern and southern Africa, performed at ages typically 13-20 years as part of initiation sequences that include scarification, seclusion, and ethno-musical elements to impart discipline and lore. In East African societies like the Maasai and Akamba, circumcision anchors the boy's entry into adult responsibilities, integrated into broader rites that honor ancestral forces for protection during the ordeal. Western African traditions, such as among the Banda, incorporate circumcision into ga'nza rituals that dramatize maturation while invoking spiritual oversight, a practice documented for over 5,000 years in indigenous contexts with moral and reverential undertones. [65] [66] [67]

Ritual masks and invocations frequently represent ancestral spirits that sanction the procedure and safeguard initiates, as seen in nkanda initiations where such symbols legitimize the cutting and subsequent training phases. These elements reflect a cosmological framework where circumcision affirms lineage continuity and wards off malevolent forces, distinct from medical rationales yet persisting amid modern health campaigns due to entrenched customary authority. Complications like sepsis and amputation arise from non-sterile conditions, prompting regulatory efforts in places like South Africa, where the practice is constitutionally shielded as cultural expression despite risks. [68] [64] [65]

### Oceanic and Pacific Islander Practices

In traditional Australian Aboriginal religions, circumcision serves as a foundational male initiation rite, performed on pubescent boys to symbolize their transition to manhood and integration into the sacred realm of ancestral law and totemic knowledge. The procedure, often executed with stone tools by initiated elders during secluded ceremonies, embodies purification through blood sacrifice and pain endurance, mirroring mythic acts of creation from the Dreaming era. In many central and western groups, it precedes subincision—a deeper incision along the penile urethra—performed one to two years later to replicate ancestral fertility rites, release "subincision blood" analogous to menstrual flow for totemic renewal, and confer spiritual authority within men's secret cults. These practices, varying by tribal moiety and region (e.g., Aranda and Walbiri emphasize sequential rites), reinforce causal mechanisms of social cohesion and cosmological continuity, with non-participation barring access to sacred sites and lore. [69] [70] [71]

Pacific Islander traditional practices, rooted in pre-colonial animistic and ancestral belief systems, similarly frame circumcision as an adolescent rite of passage denoting maturity, bravery, and communal belonging, though with marked regional diversity. In Polynesia, particularly Samoa, Tonga, Niue, and Tikopia, the procedure—historically conducted with bamboo or shell knives amid feasts and chants—is a cultural imperative around ages 13–16, where refusal incurs lifelong shame and exclusion from warrior or chiefly roles; it symbolized spiritual cleansing and alignment with mana (supernatural power) in indigenous cosmologies before widespread Christianization. Melanesian variants, such as in Fiji and Vanuatu's Tanna Island, involve ritual seclusion in forests or sacred waters for weeks to months post-circumcision, evoking rebirth motifs and elder-guided transmission of myths; Fijian boys, for example, underwent operations in protected river precincts at 13–14 years, linking the act to ancestral guardianship of waterways. [72] [73] [74]

Micronesian customs are sparser in ethnographic records but echo Oceanic patterns, with circumcision in some atolls serving as a puberty threshold tied to navigation lore and clan spirits, often less formalized than in Polynesia or Melanesia. Notably, Māori society in New Zealand eschewed routine circumcision, favoring other genital modifications or none, highlighting adaptive variations uninfluenced by Abrahamic contacts. Across these traditions, empirical accounts from early 20th-century anthropologists document declining ritual intensity due to colonial missions and medicalization, yet persistence as markers of ethnic identity amid modernization. [72] [69]

### Other Indigenous Contexts

In certain indigenous cultures of the Americas, early European explorers documented instances of male circumcision among native groups, though the practice was neither widespread nor uniform across tribes. Christopher Columbus reported observing circumcised individuals among Caribbean natives during his 1492 voyages, a finding echoed in subsequent accounts from explorers in regions like the Orinoco River basin, where infant circumcision was described as a ritual procedure performed shortly after birth. [75] [76] [77] These observations suggest localized traditions possibly linked to rites of passage or hygiene, but archaeological and ethnographic evidence remains sparse, with no definitive pre-Columbian artifacts confirming standardized foreskin removal akin to Old World practices. [78]

Mesoamerican civilizations such as the Aztecs and Mayans engaged in elaborate genital bloodletting rituals, often involving penile piercing or incision for sacrificial purposes, but historical analyses distinguish these from full circumcision, attributing persistent claims of the latter to conflation with autosacrifice traditions rather than empirical proof of routine prepuce excision. [79] In some Amazonian tribes, including the Taparapay, circumcision formed part of male initiation ceremonies documented in mid-20th-century ethnographies, utilizing ritual masks and communal seclusion to mark transition to adulthood, though such customs have largely diminished due to cultural disruption and modernization. [80] Overall, American indigenous circumcision appears sporadic and regionally variant, lacking the scriptural or theological codification seen in Abrahamic faiths, and often serving pragmatic or symbolic roles in tribal identity rather than universal religious mandate. [81]

## Theological and Ritual Dimensions

### Scriptural Justifications

In Judaism, the scriptural foundation for male circumcision (brit milah) originates in the Torah, where it serves as the physical sign of God's everlasting covenant with Abraham and his male descendants. Genesis 17:9-14 commands: "Every male among you shall be circumcised... and it shall be a sign of the covenant between Me and you," mandating the removal of the foreskin from every male child on the eighth day of life, with non-compliance resulting in exclusion from the covenant community. [23] [82] Leviticus 12:3 reiterates the eighth-day requirement as part of postpartum purity laws: "On the eighth day the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised." [83] This obligation extends to all males in the household, including servants and converts, underscoring circumcision's role in marking perpetual fidelity to the divine promise of land and progeny. [84]

In Islam, circumcision (khitan) lacks direct Quranic prescription but derives justification from prophetic tradition (sunnah) and emulation of Abrahamic practice. The Quran alludes indirectly through verses urging adherence to Abraham's monotheistic path (e.g., Surah An-Nahl 16:123: "Then We revealed to you, [O Muhammad], to follow the religion of Abraham, inclining toward truth"), interpreted by scholars as including his circumcised state as part of fitrah (innate human disposition). [85] Primary support comes from Hadith literature, such as narrations in Sunan Abu Dawood attributing to the Prophet Muhammad the statement that the fitrah includes circumcision among five natural acts of purification. [33] Classical jurists across Sunni and Shia schools classify it as wājib (obligatory) or sunnah mu'akkadah (emphasized recommendation) for males, typically performed between birth and puberty, to promote hygiene and conformity to prophetic example, though timing varies by madhhab. [36] [34]

Christian scriptures present a theological shift away from physical circumcision as a covenantal requirement. While acknowledging the Old Testament mandate (e.g., Acts 7:8 referencing Abraham's obedience), the New Testament, particularly Pauline epistles, subordinates it to spiritual circumcision of the heart. Galatians 5:6 declares: "For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision has any value. The only thing that counts is faith expressing itself through love," arguing that ritual observance cannot justify before God. [40] The Apostolic Council in Acts 15 (circa 49-50 CE) explicitly rejected imposing circumcision on Gentile converts, stating: "It is my judgment, therefore, that we should not make it difficult for the Gentiles who are turning to God" (Acts 15:19), prioritizing baptism and moral conduct over Mosaic law. [86] This abrogation reflects a first-century debate, resolved against Judaizing influences, rendering physical circumcision non-essential for salvation across major denominations. [87]

Among other Abrahamic faiths like Samaritanism, scriptural justification mirrors Judaism's Torah-based covenant in Genesis 17, with Samaritans performing it on the eighth day as a core identity marker. [82] In Druze and certain Ethiopian Orthodox traditions, practices persist culturally but lack unique scriptural mandates beyond shared Abrahamic texts. Non-Abrahamic religions, such as Hinduism or Buddhism, contain no scriptural endorsements for male circumcision, with any rare instances rooted in regional customs rather than canonical texts. [1]

### Rite of Passage and Symbolic Meanings

In Judaism, the brit milah (covenant of circumcision) serves as a foundational rite marking the newborn male's entry into the covenant between God and the Jewish people, as commanded in Genesis 17:10-14, where it is described as an "everlasting covenant" symbolized by the removal of the foreskin on the eighth day after birth. [25] This ritual, performed by a mohel in a communal ceremony, underscores themes of divine promise, generational continuity, and perpetual obligation to Torah observance, rather than a transition to physical maturity, though it physically alters the body as a perpetual sign of Jewish identity and fidelity. [88] Symbolically, it represents purification and the human role in fertility and nation-building, distinct from mere hygiene, as evidenced by its retention through exile as a core emblem of faith alongside the Sabbath. [23]

In Islam, male circumcision (khitan) functions as a sunnah (recommended practice) rooted in prophetic tradition, often conducted in infancy, childhood, or early adolescence, symbolizing spiritual purification (tahara), restoration to the primordial state of fitrah (natural purity), and affiliation with the Muslim ummah (community). [89] Unlike Judaism's covenantal mandate, it lacks direct Quranic prescription but draws from hadith emphasizing cleanliness and aversion to excess skin as impure; in some cultural contexts, it marks a partial rite of passage toward manhood, reinforcing communal belonging without obligatory ritual elements. [90]

Among certain Christian denominations, such as the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, circumcision retains ritual observance tied to the Feast of the Circumcision on January 7 (Julian calendar), commemorating Jesus' submission to Mosaic law (Luke 2:21) as a sign of blood covenant signifying life and divine fidelity, though New Testament theology subordinates it to baptism as the true spiritual circumcision of the heart (Colossians 2:11). [91] In practice, it symbolizes continuity with Old Testament promises fulfilled in Christ, performed on male infants or youth not for salvific necessity but as cultural affirmation of obedience and identity. [92]

In many African traditional religions, circumcision constitutes a core element of male initiation rites (lebollo, ulwaluko, or bogwera), typically at puberty (ages 12-18), embodying symbolic death and rebirth to signify the profound transition from boyhood dependency to adult responsibilities, warrior status, and procreative roles within the community. [93] These ceremonies, involving pre-ritual seclusion, moral instruction, and post-circumcision reintegration, interpret the act as purification from childhood impurities, endowment of strength and fertility, and subjugation to ancestral spirits or societal norms, with the physical scar serving as an enduring emblem of maturity and tribal allegiance; complications arise when medical safety is overlooked in favor of traditional symbolism. [94] [95]

## Contemporary Controversies

### Ethical and Bodily Autonomy Debates

Critics of religious infant circumcision argue that it infringes on the child's right to bodily integrity by performing a permanent, non-therapeutic alteration without the individual's consent. [96] Ethicists such as J. Steven Svoboda contend that proxy parental consent for such procedures is invalid, as it violates core human rights protections against unnecessary intrusions into an infant's body, drawing parallels to protections against female genital cutting. [96] This perspective emphasizes that infants possess an inherent interest in future autonomy, making irreversible genital modifications ethically problematic even if motivated by tradition or faith. [97]

Proponents of deferring circumcision until adulthood highlight it as a form of iatrogenic injury, removing functional foreskin tissue—estimated at 30-50 cm² in adulthood—that serves protective, sensory, and immunological roles, without compensating medical necessity for newborns in low-risk settings. [98] The American Medical Association's ethics analysis asserts that nontherapeutic circumcision conflicts with principles of nonmaleficence and respect for autonomy, recommending postponement to allow informed adult decision-making, as endorsed by bodies like the Danish Medical Association in 2016. [98] In religious contexts, this raises questions of whether communal rituals justify overriding individual genital integrity, with scholars arguing that liberal frameworks prioritize personal rights over group claims to prevent coerced belonging. [99]

Defenders invoke parental proxy authority, asserting that decisions in the child's best interest, including religious upbringing, align with legal standards like the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (Articles 14, 20, 30), which accommodate cultural practices when welfare is not prejudiced. [100] Some ethicists, applying a best-interest standard, maintain that circumcision falls within parental prerogative if it confers net benefits or integrates the child into a supportive community, as in Jewish or Muslim traditions, without violating autonomy since infants lack capacity for such choices. [101] However, this proxy consent is contested when procedures lack therapeutic urgency, with critics noting inconsistencies in applying "best interest" selectively to endorse permanent alterations absent imminent harm. [102]

The debate intensifies over religious exemptions, where freedom of belief clashes with universal child protections; for instance, European human rights frameworks (e.g., ECHR Article 9) permit parental religious decisions but subordinate them to health imperatives, prompting calls for regulation or bans on infant procedures to safeguard bodily sovereignty. [100] Empirical ethical analyses suggest weighing future autonomy interests—treating the child as an ideal agent likely to value intact genitals—against immediate communal bonds, often favoring delay to resolve consent deficits. [97] Positions vary by jurisdiction, with some medical ethics bodies viewing routine religious circumcision as deferrable to respect emerging self-determination. [98]

### Legal Challenges and Religious Freedom

In various jurisdictions, religious infant circumcision has encountered legal opposition centered on children's rights to bodily integrity and informed consent, often clashing with parental claims to religious freedom under constitutional or human rights protections. Courts and legislatures have weighed these interests, with outcomes frequently favoring religious practice when deemed non-lethal and culturally entrenched, though restrictions on procedure safety persist. [103] [104]

A prominent challenge arose in Germany on June 26, 2012, when the Cologne Regional Court ruled that circumcising a four-year-old boy for religious reasons constituted grievous bodily harm under criminal law, as the procedure lacked therapeutic necessity and the child could not consent, overriding parental religious rights. [105] The decision, stemming from a botched procedure causing bleeding, sparked unified protests from Jewish and Muslim organizations, who argued it effectively criminalized core rituals like brit milah and effectively expelled observant communities by infringing on Article 4 of Germany's Basic Law guaranteeing religious freedom. [106] [107] In response, Chancellor Angela Merkel pledged legislative protection on July 13, 2012, leading to the passage of the Circumcision Act on December 14, 2012, which legalized the procedure for boys under six months when conducted by qualified practitioners with parental consent and post-operative care, explicitly balancing child welfare against religious exercise. [108] [109]

Similar tensions surfaced in Iceland in February 2018, when members of the Progressive Party proposed amending the Penal Code to ban non-therapeutic male circumcision on minors under 15, punishable by up to six years imprisonment, framing it as a violation of bodily autonomy akin to female genital mutilation. [110] Religious leaders from Jewish, Muslim, and Christian groups condemned the bill as an unprecedented attack on freedom of religion protected by Iceland's constitution and the European Convention on Human Rights, warning it would isolate small faith communities numbering fewer than 1,500 Muslims and 100 Jews. [111] [112] The proposal stalled in committee and was withdrawn by April 2018 amid international backlash, including from the Anti-Defamation League, without advancing to a vote, preserving the status quo where circumcisions occur under medical supervision. [113]

In the United States, religious circumcision enjoys robust protection under the First Amendment's Free Exercise Clause and precedents affirming parental authority in child-rearing, such as Parham v. J.R. (1979), which upheld parents' medical decisions for minors absent clear harm. [114] Local challenges, including a 2011 San Francisco ballot initiative (Proposition E) seeking to prohibit circumcision on those under 18 without medical necessity, were preempted by state legislation before the vote, with proponents arguing it targeted Jewish and Muslim practices in violation of religious liberty; courts have consistently rejected broader bans, viewing non-therapeutic circumcision as within parental discretion rather than state-compelled harm. [115] No federal or Supreme Court ruling has curtailed religious circumcision, distinguishing it from prohibited practices like female genital cutting due to differences in health outcomes and cultural acceptance. [116]

European human rights frameworks, including Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights, have upheld religious circumcision against blanket bans, as in a 2015 Dutch court dismissal of assault claims against parents, emphasizing proportionality and lack of severe risk when hygienically performed. [117] Non-binding Council of Europe resolutions in 2013 and 2015 urged member states to scrutinize non-medical circumcision for consent violations but deferred to national laws protecting religious minorities, reflecting empirical data on low complication rates (under 1% in regulated settings) over absolutist child-rights arguments. [118] These cases illustrate a pattern where initial judicial skepticism yields to legislative safeguards for religious continuity, informed by demographic realities of minority faiths facing assimilation pressures.

### Medical Evidence and Health Claims

Medical claims surrounding circumcision often invoke potential preventive effects against urinary tract infections (UTIs), sexually transmitted infections (STIs) including HIV, penile cancer, and conditions like phimosis, though the absolute risk reductions vary by population and context. Systematic reviews indicate that neonatal circumcision reduces the incidence of UTIs in male infants by approximately 90% during the first year of life, with uncircumcised males facing a baseline risk of about 1% compared to 0.1% in circumcised males. [119] [120] This benefit stems from the removal of the foreskin, which can harbor bacteria, though the overall number needed to circumcise to prevent one UTI is around 111 in the general population.

For HIV prevention, three randomized controlled trials in sub-Saharan Africa demonstrated that adult male circumcision reduces heterosexual HIV acquisition by about 60%, prompting World Health Organization recommendations for voluntary medical male circumcision in high-prevalence regions. [121] [122] Evidence for other STIs is weaker but includes modest reductions in human papillomavirus (HPV) prevalence and incidence, as well as some protection against herpes simplex virus type 2. [123] Penile cancer, a rare malignancy with an incidence of less than 1 per 100,000 in developed countries, shows a strong protective association with childhood or adolescent circumcision (odds ratio 0.33), linked to decreased chronic inflammation and human papillomavirus persistence under the foreskin. [124] Circumcision also lowers the risk of phimosis and balanoposthitis, potentially averting later therapeutic procedures. [119]

Procedural risks include bleeding, infection, and adhesions, with neonatal complication rates typically ranging from 0.2% to 2%, though some studies report up to 3% for minor issues and rare severe events like excessive skin removal or penile injury at rates below 1 per 1000. [125] [126] Older-age circumcisions carry higher risks, up to 7.4%, due to increased vascularity and cooperation challenges. [127] Meta-analyses find no consistent adverse effects on sexual function, sensitivity, or satisfaction post-circumcision. [128]

The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2012 policy states that health benefits of newborn male circumcision outweigh the risks but are insufficient to recommend it universally, emphasizing parental decision-making informed by evidence. [129] In low-prevalence settings like North America, absolute benefits for HIV or cancer prevention are minimal given baseline risks, whereas in high-HIV endemic areas, the preventive impact is more substantial. [130] Critics argue some benefits are overstated relative to risks, particularly for non-therapeutic neonatal procedures, but risk-benefit analyses suggest net positives exceeding 100:1 for certain conditions over a lifetime. [131] Religious motivations for circumcision do not alter these empirical findings, though they influence uptake independent of medical rationales.
